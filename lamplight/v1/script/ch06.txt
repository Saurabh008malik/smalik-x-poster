## Chapter 6: Noah Builds a Boat on Dry Land

Many generations after the garden, the world had grown crowded, and it had grown cruel. The sixth chapter of Genesis says that the earth was corrupt before God, and filled with violence. Not a little violence. Filled.

Here are the old words, and they are hard words, so I will read them gently.

The Lord saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually. The Lord was sorry that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him in his heart. The Lord said, "I will destroy man whom I have created from the surface of the ground; man, along with animals, creeping things, and birds of the sky; for I am sorry that I have made them." But Noah found favor in the Lord's eyes.

It grieved him in his heart. I want to stay on that phrase for a moment, because people sometimes read the flood story as the act of an angry God, and the text itself says something different. It says sorrow. It says grief. It is the grief of a maker looking at a thing that was called very good and seeing it tear itself apart. And then, at the end of the darkest paragraph in the early Bible, there is a small turn. But Noah. The whole future of the world hangs on those two words.

Noah, the story says, was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time. And then the same phrase that was used for Enoch. Noah walked with God. He had three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

God told Noah what was coming. The end of all flesh has come before me, he said, for the earth is filled with violence through them. And then, instead of only a warning, he gave Noah a task. Make a ship of gopher wood. Make rooms in the ship, and seal it inside and out with pitch. Three hundred cubits long. Fifty cubits wide. Thirty cubits high. A cubit is roughly the length of a forearm, so the boat was something like four hundred and fifty feet long, the length of a large modern ship. Make a window in it, and a door in its side, and build it with lower, second, and third decks.

And then the promise. I will establish my covenant with you. You shall come into the ship, you and your sons and your wife and your sons' wives with you. And of every living thing of all flesh, you shall bring two of every sort into the ship, to keep them alive with you. Male and female. Of the birds after their kind, of the cattle after their kind, of every creeping thing of the ground after its kind. Two of every sort will come to you, to keep them alive. And take food, for you and for them.

And the chapter ends with one of the plainest and most beautiful sentences in the Bible. Thus Noah did. According to all that God commanded him, so he did.

I want you to imagine what that obedience looked like from the outside, because the story does not describe it, and the picture is worth having. A man, no longer young, on dry ground, far from any sea, begins to build a boat. Not a fishing boat. A boat the size of a hillside. Year after year, the frame goes up. The neighbors walk by. There is no rain. There is no water anywhere near the thing. His sons help him. His wife feeds them. The smell of pitch hangs over the valley.

Later traditions say it took him many decades, and that he warned the people around him the whole time, and that nobody listened. The Bible itself does not give those details. It only gives us the sound of a hammer on wood, under a clear sky, for a very long time. And it gives us that one sentence. Thus Noah did.

There is something restful in that kind of faith. Not the kind that feels brave or sees results. The kind that just does the next plank. Noah was not told when the rain would come. He was told to build. And he built, under a sky that showed no sign of changing, until the boat was finished and the animals began to arrive.

In the seventh chapter, the Lord says to Noah, come into the ship, you and all your household. In seven days, he says, I will cause it to rain on the earth forty days and forty nights. And so they went in. Noah and his sons and his wife and his sons' wives. And the animals came, two by two, clean and unclean, birds and everything that creeps on the ground, male and female, as God had commanded. Picture the long line of them going up the ramp into the dark of the hold. The great slow beasts and the quick small ones, the ones with feathers and the ones with scales, all of them finding their rooms.

And then one more small line, easy to miss. The Lord shut him in.

Noah did not close the door himself. Someone closed it for him from the outside. It is the same hand that made coats of skin in the garden. The same practical care. When the storm comes, you will not be the one holding the door shut against it.

Noah was six hundred years old, the story says, when the flood of waters came on the earth. On the seventeenth day of the second month, all the fountains of the great deep burst open, and the windows of the sky were opened. The waters that had been divided on the second day of creation, above and below, came together again. And it rained on the earth forty days and forty nights.

That is where we will stop for now. Inside the ship, in the dark, with the rain beginning on the roof. The animals settling into the straw. The family listening to a sound that had never been heard on the earth before. And the door shut, and held, by a hand that was not theirs.

If it is raining where you are tonight, listen to it for a while. If it is not, you can imagine it. The steady fall of it on wood, and the whole world rocking gently under you, carried.
