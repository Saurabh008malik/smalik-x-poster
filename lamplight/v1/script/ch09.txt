## Chapter 9: Get Out of Your Country

The twelfth chapter of Genesis begins without any warning. There is no burning bush, no dream, no storm. There is only a voice, and a man in Haran who hears it.

Now the Lord said to Abram, "Get out of your country, and from your relatives, and from your father's house, to the land that I will show you. I will make of you a great nation. I will bless you and make your name great. You will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and I will curse him who curses you. In you will all of the families of the earth be blessed."

Listen to what Abram is asked to leave. His country. His relatives. His father's house. The circles get smaller and closer with each phrase. First the land, then the wider family, then the very house he grew up in. And listen to what he is promised in return. Not a map. Not a destination. A land that I will show you. He is asked to leave everything he knows for a place he will only recognize when he gets there.

And the promise at the end is the hinge of the whole Bible. In you will all of the families of the earth be blessed. Remember the families of Babel, scattered across the world with their new languages? Here they are again. God has not forgotten them. The plan, it turns out, was never to abandon the scattered world. The plan was to choose one family, and through that one family, to reach every other.

Abram went, as the Lord had told him. That is the whole description of his decision. He went. He was seventy-five years old. He took Sarai his wife, and Lot his brother's son, and all their possessions, and the people they had gathered in Haran, and they set out for the land of Canaan.

Picture that caravan, if you like, moving slowly south along the old road. The tents rolled on the backs of donkeys. The flocks spread out on either side, kept together by servants with staffs. The dust. The evening halts. An old man and his wife at the front, going somewhere they have never been, because of a voice.

They came to the land of Canaan, and passed through it to a place called Shechem, to the oak of Moreh. And the Lord appeared to Abram and said, I will give this land to your offspring. And Abram built an altar there. Then he moved on to the hills east of Bethel, and pitched his tent, and built another altar, and called on the name of the Lord. And then he journeyed on, still going toward the south. Everywhere he stopped, he left a small pile of stones behind him. The land was being quietly marked with places of prayer.

The story is honest about Abram. It does not make him a hero without fault. When a famine came, he went down to Egypt, and he was afraid that the Egyptians would kill him to take his beautiful wife, so he asked Sarai to say she was his sister. It went badly. Pharaoh took her into his house, and plagues fell on Pharaoh's household, and Pharaoh found out the truth and sent Abram away with a stern word. The man who had just been promised that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him had, in his first test, brought trouble on a foreign king out of fear. The Bible lets that stand. It wants you to know that the promise does not depend on Abram being brave.

Abram came back up out of Egypt into the Negev, and then back to the place between Bethel and Ai where his tent had been at the beginning, to the altar he had made there. And he called on the name of the Lord.

Now Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold. And Lot, who went with him, also had flocks and herds and tents. And the land could not bear them both. There was strife between the herdsmen of Abram's livestock and the herdsmen of Lot's livestock. Two large households, one small stretch of pasture, and a quarrel at the wells.

What Abram does next is one of the quiet moments that shows you who he is. He said to Lot, please let there be no strife between me and you, and between my herdsmen and your herdsmen, for we are relatives. Is not the whole land before you? Please separate yourself from me. If you go to the left hand, then I will go to the right. Or if you go to the right hand, then I will go to the left.

Abram was the elder. The land had been promised to him, not to Lot. He could have chosen first. Instead he let the younger man choose. Lot lifted up his eyes and saw the whole plain of the Jordan, well watered everywhere, like the garden of the Lord, like the land of Egypt. And Lot chose the plain, and moved his tent as far as Sodom. The story adds, almost under its breath, that the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked. Lot chose the greener grass, and did not look at who lived on it.

And then, when Lot had gone, the Lord spoke to Abram again. Now, lift up your eyes, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward. For all the land which you see, I will give to you, and to your offspring forever. I will make your offspring as the dust of the earth. Arise, walk through the land in its length and in its breadth, for I will give it to you.

Abram had just given away his first choice of the land. And God gave him all of it. That is the shape the story keeps taking. The one who holds things loosely ends up holding everything.

So Abram moved his tent, and came and lived by the oaks of Mamre, which are in Hebron, and built an altar there to the Lord. Those oaks will come up again. There is going to be a visit there one hot afternoon that changes everything. But for tonight, Abram is simply an old man sitting in the shade of some trees in the hill country, with his wife, and his flocks, and a promise he cannot see the end of.

Let the picture settle. Oak trees. Dry warm air. The sound of sheep somewhere out of sight. A man who left everything on the strength of a voice, and has not been told when the promise will come true, and has decided to wait anyway.

You can wait with him for a while. It is restful country.
