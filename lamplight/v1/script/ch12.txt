## Chapter 12: The Mountain, the Well, and Goodnight

After these things, God tested Abraham. That is how the twenty-second chapter begins, and the word tested is important. The reader is told from the first line that this is a test. Abraham is not told. He only hears the voice, and the voice says his name, and he answers the way he always answers. Here I am.

Take now your son, the voice said, your only son, whom you love, even Isaac, and go into the land of Moriah, and offer him there for a burnt offering on one of the mountains which I will tell you of.

I am not going to soften this. It is the hardest passage in Genesis, and people of faith have wrestled with it for three thousand years. I am only going to tell it the way the text tells it, slowly, and let you notice what the text notices.

Abraham rose early in the morning. He did not argue, as he had argued for Sodom. He saddled his donkey, and took two of his young men with him, and Isaac his son. He split the wood for the burnt offering, and rose up, and went to the place of which God had told him. On the third day, Abraham lifted up his eyes and saw the place far off. He told the young men to stay with the donkey. The boy and I will go yonder, he said, and we will worship, and come back to you. We will come back. He said we.

Abraham took the wood and laid it on Isaac his son. He took the fire and the knife in his own hand. And they both went together.

Here are the old words.

Isaac spoke to Abraham his father, and said, "My father?" He said, "Here I am, my son." He said, "Here is the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?" Abraham said, "God will provide himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son." So they both went together.

Twice the text says it. They both went together. An old man and his son, walking up a mountain, carrying wood and fire, and the boy asking a question, and the father giving the only answer he has. God will provide.

They came to the place. Abraham built the altar, and laid the wood in order, and bound Isaac his son, and laid him on the altar on the wood. And Abraham stretched out his hand, and took the knife.

The angel of the Lord called to him out of the sky, and said, "Abraham, Abraham!" He said, "Here I am." He said, "Don't lay your hand on the boy, neither do anything to him. For now I know that you fear God, seeing you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me." Abraham lifted up his eyes, and looked, and saw that behind him was a ram caught in the thicket by his horns. Abraham went and took the ram, and offered him up for a burnt offering instead of his son.

Abraham, Abraham. Twice, urgently, the way you call someone's name when you need them to stop right now. And he stopped. And there was a ram, caught by its horns in a thicket, that had been there the whole time, behind him. God will provide, he had told his son on the way up, not knowing if it was true. And it was true. Abraham called the name of that place, the Lord will provide. And the saying is still used, the story says. On the mountain of the Lord it will be provided.

The test is over. The father and the son came down the mountain together, and went back to the young men, and the donkey, and the long road home to Beersheba. The text never tells us what Isaac thought. It only tells us that he lived, and that the promise lived with him.

Sarah died in Hebron, at one hundred twenty-seven years old. Abraham came to mourn for her and to weep for her. And then he did something very ordinary and very human. He bought a field. He went to the people of the land and asked, with great courtesy, for a burying place, and he insisted on paying for it, and he weighed out four hundred shekels of silver, and the field of Machpelah, with its cave and all its trees, became his. It was the only piece of the promised land that Abraham ever owned. A grave for his wife. And he buried Sarah in the cave of the field of Machpelah, facing Mamre.

And then, old and well advanced in years, Abraham turned his mind to one last thing. Isaac needed a wife. Not from the Canaanites, but from Abraham's own people, back in the north, back in the country he had left. So he sent his oldest servant, the one who ruled over all that he had, with ten camels and good gifts, on the long road back toward Haran.

The servant arrived at evening, outside the city of Nahor, by the well, at the hour when the women go out to draw water. And he prayed. He asked for a sign. Let it be, he said, that the girl to whom I say, please let down your pitcher that I may drink, and she says, drink, and I will also give your camels a drink, let her be the one.

Here are the old words.

It happened, before he had finished speaking, that behold, Rebekah came out, who was born to Bethuel the son of Milcah, the wife of Nahor, Abraham's brother, with her pitcher on her shoulder. The young lady was very beautiful to look at, a virgin, neither had any man known her. She went down to the spring, filled her pitcher, and came up. The servant ran to meet her, and said, "Please give me a drink, a little water from your pitcher." She said, "Drink, my lord." She hurried, and let down her pitcher on her hand, and gave him drink. When she had done giving him drink, she said, "I will also draw for your camels, until they have done drinking." She hurried, and emptied her pitcher into the trough, and ran again to the well to draw, and drew for all his camels.

Ten camels. A thirsty camel can drink a great deal of water. She ran back and forth to the well, again and again, for a stranger, without being asked. That was the sign. Kindness that goes further than it has to. The servant watched her in silence, the story says, to know whether the Lord had made his journey prosperous or not. And then he gave her a gold ring and two bracelets, and asked whose daughter she was, and when she told him, he bowed his head and worshiped.

Rebekah ran home and told her family. Her brother Laban came out to the well and brought the servant in, and fed the camels, and washed his feet, and set food before him. But the servant would not eat until he had told his errand. He told the whole story, from Abraham's prosperity to the prayer at the well, and the family listened, and said, the thing proceeds from the Lord. And in the morning, when they wanted her to stay a few more days, they called Rebekah and asked her, will you go with this man? And she said, I will go.

I will go. The same words, nearly, that began the whole story. Abram went. Now Rebekah goes. The promise moves forward on the willingness of people to leave home for a road they cannot see the end of.

Isaac was out walking in the field at evening, meditating, when he lifted up his eyes and saw camels coming. And Rebekah lifted up her eyes and saw Isaac, and got down from the camel, and covered herself with her veil. And Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah's tent, and took Rebekah, and she became his wife, and he loved her. And Isaac was comforted after his mother's death.

That is the line I want to leave you with. Isaac was comforted. The empty tent of a mother who had died was filled again with someone to love. The grief did not disappear. It was comforted.

Abraham lived on, and married again, and had more sons, and gave gifts to them, and gave all that he had to Isaac. And then, in the old words, these are the days of the years of Abraham's life which he lived: one hundred seventy-five years. Abraham gave up the spirit, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years, and was gathered to his people.

Full of years. And his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him, together, in the cave of Machpelah, beside Sarah. The two brothers, the child of laughter and the child of hearing, stood side by side at their father's grave. The story does not say what they said to each other. It only says they were both there.

And that is where we will stop tonight. With the world made, and spoiled, and washed, and begun again. With the languages scattered and the promise given. With an old man who believed under the stars, and a woman who laughed, and a boy who came down the mountain alive, and a girl who ran to the well for a stranger's camels. With a cave in a field facing Mamre, where two old people rest, and two brothers stand together.

The night is still going on. The seventh day has never closed. Whatever is unfinished in your own story can wait until morning, which is coming, as it was promised, seed time and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, as long as the earth remains.

Thank you for listening. Goodnight. Sleep well. You are seen.
