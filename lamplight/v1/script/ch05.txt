## Chapter 5: Two Brothers, and a Long List of Names

Outside the garden, life went on. That is the first thing to say about the fourth chapter. The man knew his wife, and she conceived, and bore a son, and she named him Cain. And she said, I have gotten a man with the help of the Lord. Even outside the gate, she still spoke his name with gratitude. Then she bore Cain's brother, Abel.

Abel was a keeper of sheep, and Cain was a tiller of the ground. Two brothers, two ways of living. One followed the flocks, moving with the grass and the seasons. One stayed put and worked the soil. You can picture them growing up side by side, the one who wandered and the one who stayed.

In the course of time, the story says, Cain brought an offering to the Lord from the fruit of the ground. And Abel also brought an offering, from the firstborn of his flock, and from their fat. The Lord respected Abel and his offering, but he did not respect Cain and his offering.

The text does not fully explain why. Readers have wondered about it for thousands of years. Some point out that Abel brought the firstborn and the best, and Cain brought simply some of what grew. Others say that it is not about the gift at all but about the heart that gave it. Whatever the reason, Cain was very angry, and his face fell. And here the Lord does something gentle. He speaks to Cain before anything has happened. Why are you angry? Why has the expression of your face fallen? If you do well, will it not be lifted up? And if you do not do well, sin crouches at the door. Its desire is for you, but you are to rule over it.

Sin crouching at the door, like an animal waiting outside a tent. That is the picture. And the promise that it can be ruled over. Cain was warned. He was given the chance to walk away from the thing that was waiting for him.

He did not take it. Cain spoke to Abel his brother, and when they were in the field, Cain rose up against Abel and killed him. The first death in the Bible is not from old age or illness. It is a brother's hand.

Here are the old words that follow.

The Lord said to Cain, "Where is Abel, your brother?" He said, "I don't know. Am I my brother's keeper?" The Lord said, "What have you done? The voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the ground."

Again a question. Where is Abel, your brother. It is the second question God asks in the Bible, and it echoes the first. Where are you. Where is your brother. The two questions belong together. The story is already telling you, this early, that you cannot answer one without the other.

Cain is sent out to wander, a fugitive on the earth, further east, away from the presence of the Lord. But even here, there is a mercy folded into the judgment. Cain is afraid that anyone who finds him will kill him. And the Lord puts a mark on Cain, not to shame him, as people sometimes think, but to protect him. Whoever harms Cain, the Lord says, will face vengeance seven times over. The murderer is sheltered. It is a strange grace, and the Bible is full of strange graces.

Cain went away and built a city, and had children, and his children had children. The story names a few of them, and tells us what they did. One was the father of those who live in tents and keep livestock. One was the father of all who handle the harp and the pipe. One forged instruments of bronze and iron. Even in the line of Cain, music and craft and shelter were born. Nothing human was wasted.

And Adam and Eve had another son, and named him Seth, which sounds like the word for appointed, because, Eve said, God has appointed me another child instead of Abel, whom Cain killed. And Seth had a son named Enosh. And then comes a line that the whole chapter has been quietly walking toward. At that time, men began to call on the name of the Lord.

The fifth chapter of Genesis is a list of names. The generations from Adam to Noah. I am going to read some of it to you, slowly, because a list of names and ages, read in the dark, is one of the oldest lullabies there is. You do not need to follow it. Just let the names go by like fence posts along a road at night.

Adam lived one hundred thirty years, and became the father of a son in his own likeness, after his image, and named him Seth. The days of Adam after he became the father of Seth were eight hundred years, and he became the father of sons and daughters. All the days that Adam lived were nine hundred thirty years, then he died.

Seth lived one hundred five years, and became the father of Enosh. Seth lived after he became the father of Enosh eight hundred seven years, and became the father of sons and daughters. All the days of Seth were nine hundred twelve years, then he died.

And so it goes. Enosh, who lived nine hundred five years. Kenan, nine hundred ten. Mahalalel, eight hundred ninety-five. Jared, nine hundred sixty-two. Each one lived, and became a father, and had sons and daughters, and then died. The refrain repeats like a slow tide. He lived. He had children. He died.

And then the list pauses on one name, and the rhythm breaks. Enoch. Enoch lived sixty-five years and became the father of Methuselah. And Enoch walked with God after he became the father of Methuselah three hundred years, and became the father of sons and daughters. All the days of Enoch were three hundred sixty-five years. And then, where every other name in the list has the words, then he died, Enoch's line says something different. Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him.

He walked with God, and he was not. The old readers loved this verse. They imagined a man who went out walking with God in the evenings, the way the first man had done in the garden, and one evening simply did not come back, because the walk had gone on too far and too happily to turn around. That is all the Bible says about Enoch. It is enough.

The list goes on. Methuselah, who lived longer than anyone, nine hundred sixty-nine years. Lamech. And then Lamech had a son, and named him Noah, saying, this one will comfort us in our work and in the toil of our hands, because of the ground which the Lord has cursed. Noah. The name sounds like the word for rest.

That is where the long list stops. On a baby named rest, in a tired world. We will meet him again when he is grown, and there is a great deal of rain coming. But not yet. For now, let the names settle around you like dust in a sunbeam. Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, Noah. Ten generations, breathing in and out, and then quiet.
