## Chapter 4: The Cool of the Day

Now the serpent was more subtle than any animal of the field which the Lord God had made. That is how the third chapter begins, and it begins quietly, the way trouble often does.

The serpent spoke to the woman. It did not shout or threaten. It asked a question, and the question was slightly wrong on purpose. Has God really said, you shall not eat of any tree of the garden? Of course God had said no such thing. He had said the opposite. Every tree, freely, except one. But the question made the one forbidden tree seem bigger than the whole garden around it, and that is the trick, then and now.

The woman answered honestly enough. We may eat the fruit of the trees of the garden, she said, but of the fruit of the tree which is in the middle of the garden, God has said, you shall not eat of it, neither shall you touch it, lest you die. And the serpent said, you will not surely die. God knows that in the day you eat it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.

The story does not make the woman foolish. It says she looked at the tree and saw that it was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that it was to be desired to make one wise. Those are reasonable things to want. Food, beauty, wisdom. She took the fruit and ate, and gave some to her husband, who was with her, and he ate too. He had been standing there the whole time. The story is careful about that.

And the eyes of both of them were opened, just as the serpent had said. But what they saw was not what they had been promised. They saw that they were naked. And the two people who a page earlier had been unashamed sewed fig leaves together to cover themselves.

Then comes one of the most tender and most sorrowful lines in all of scripture. I will read it in the old words.

They heard the voice of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the man and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God among the trees of the garden. The Lord God called to the man, and said to him, "Where are you?" The man said, "I heard your voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself."

The cool of the day. That is the evening breeze, the hour when the heat breaks and a person goes walking. The story lets you understand, without saying it outright, that this walk was a habit. God walking in the garden in the evening, and the man and the woman there to meet him. And on this evening, for the first time, no one came to meet him. He had to call out. Where are you?

The old teachers noticed that God asks the question even though he knows the answer. It is not a question for gathering information. It is the question of someone standing in a familiar place, calling for someone who has always been there. Where are you. It is the first question God asks a human being in the Bible, and it is a question you can hear love in, if you listen for it.

What follows is the long sad unwinding. The man blames the woman, and even a little bit blames God, the woman you gave me. The woman blames the serpent. And then the consequences are spoken, one by one. To the serpent, that it will go on its belly and eat dust, and that there will be enmity between it and the woman, between its offspring and hers. To the woman, that childbirth will come with pain, and that there will be hardness between her and her husband. To the man, that the ground itself is cursed because of him, that it will bring forth thorns and thistles, that he will eat bread by the sweat of his face until he returns to the ground. For you are dust, the Lord says to him, and to dust you shall return.

It is a heavy passage. I am not going to make it lighter than it is. But I want you to notice two small things that are easy to miss, because they are the sort of things a tired person might take comfort in.

The first is that the man gives his wife a name right at this moment, after everything has gone wrong. He called her Eve, which means life, because she was the mother of all living. In the middle of the sentence about returning to dust, he looks at her and names her life. That is hope, planted in the worst hour.

The second thing is even smaller. Here are the old words.

The Lord God made coats of skins for Adam and for his wife, and clothed them.

Before they are sent out of the garden, God dresses them. They had made themselves fig leaves, which would have dried and crumbled by morning. He makes them proper clothes, warm ones, that will last. It is such a practical kindness. It is the kind of thing a parent does, kneeling down to button a coat on a child who has done something terrible and is about to face the cold because of it. The sending out still happens. But they go out dressed by his own hands.

And then the Lord God sent them out of the garden of Eden, to till the ground from which they were taken. And he placed cherubim at the east of the garden, and the flame of a sword which turned every way, to guard the way to the tree of life. The gate closed behind them. And the whole long story of the Bible, from this page to its last one, is the story of how that gate gets opened again.

But that is a long way off. For tonight, picture them walking out together, in the clothes that were made for them, into a world that is harder than the one they knew but still, in all its thorns and thistles, very good. The sun still rises on it. The rivers still run. And somewhere behind them, in the cool of the day, someone is still walking in the garden, and still asking where they are.

Let that question rest in the room with you. You do not have to answer it tonight. It is enough to know that it is being asked.
