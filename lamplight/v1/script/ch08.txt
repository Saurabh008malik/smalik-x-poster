## Chapter 8: The Bow in the Cloud, and the City with a Tower

After the flood, God blessed Noah and his sons, and said to them the same words that had been said to the first people in the garden. Be fruitful, and multiply, and fill the earth. The world was starting over, and it was starting with a blessing.

Then God made a covenant. A covenant is a solemn promise, binding on the one who makes it. This one was made not only with Noah but, the text says, with every living creature, the birds and the livestock and every animal of the earth, all who came out of the ship. I will establish my covenant with you, God said. All flesh will not be cut off any more by the waters of the flood. Neither will there ever again be a flood to destroy the earth.

And God gave the promise a sign. Here are the old words.

God said, "This is the token of the covenant which I make between me and you and every living creature that is with you, for perpetual generations: I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be for a sign of a covenant between me and the earth. It will happen, when I bring a cloud over the earth, that the rainbow will be seen in the cloud, and I will remember my covenant, which is between me and you and every living creature of all flesh, and the waters will no more become a flood to destroy all flesh. The rainbow will be in the cloud. I will look at it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth."

The Hebrew word for rainbow is simply the word for a bow, the weapon. Some of the old readers noticed that the bow is hung in the sky pointing upward, away from the earth, the way a warrior hangs up his bow when the fighting is over. Whether or not that was the intention, it is a gentle thought. The storm is finished. The bow is put away. And notice who the sign is for. I will look at it, God says, that I may remember. The rainbow is not mainly a reminder for us. It is a reminder God sets for himself. Every time the clouds come, he says, I will see this, and I will remember.

Noah lived a long time after the flood, three hundred fifty years, the story says, and all his days were nine hundred fifty years. His three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth, had children, and their children had children, and the tenth chapter of Genesis is another long list, the table of the nations, telling how the families of the sons of Noah spread out over the earth, each with its own language, in their lands, by their nations. Peoples with names you half recognize. Javan, and Tarshish, and Kittim. Cush, and Mizraim, and Canaan. Elam, and Asshur, and Aram. The islands and the coastlands and the great rivers all getting their first inhabitants.

And then, in the eleventh chapter, a short story, only nine verses long, that explains how all those languages came to be.

The whole earth, it says, was of one language and of one speech. And as people traveled east, they found a plain in the land of Shinar, which is the flat river country of Babylon, and they lived there. They learned to make bricks, and to bake them hard, and to use tar for mortar. And then they had an idea.

They said, "Come, let's build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top reaches to the sky, and let's make ourselves a name, lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of the whole earth."

Listen to what they wanted. A tower whose top reaches to the sky. A name for themselves. And not to be scattered. They were afraid of being spread out and small, so they wanted to build something enormous and stay together around it. There is nothing strange about that. It is a very human wish.

The Lord came down to see the city and the tower, the story says. There is a quiet humor in that line that the old readers enjoyed. The tower was meant to reach the sky, and God still had to come down to get a look at it. And the Lord said, behold, they are one people, and they have all one language, and this is what they begin to do. Now nothing will be withheld from them which they intend to do. Come, let us go down, and there confuse their language, that they may not understand one another's speech.

So the Lord scattered them abroad from there on the surface of all the earth. They stopped building the city. And its name was called Babel, because there the Lord confused the language of all the earth.

Babel. The name sounds like the Hebrew word for confusion, and it also sounds like Babylon, the great city of the plain. The people wanted a name, and they got one, but not the one they were hoping for.

It can sound like a punishment, and in a way it is. But look at what the people were refusing to do. They had been told, twice now, once in the garden and once after the flood, to fill the earth. To spread out. To go everywhere. And instead they huddled on one plain and built upward. The scattering at Babel is God doing for them what they would not do for themselves. Sending them out into the whole wide world, into the mountains and the islands and the far coasts, with new words in their mouths.

Every language you have ever heard spoken, in an airport, or a market, or a prayer, came down from that plain. Every lullaby in every tongue. The scattering gave the world all of its songs.

And the chapter ends, as these early chapters like to do, with another list. The generations of Shem. Arpachshad, Shelah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, Nahor, Terah. The names are getting closer to someone now. You can feel it. The list is a road, and the road is leading to a particular tent, in a particular city, where an old man named Terah lives with his sons. One of them is called Abram.

Terah took Abram his son, and Lot the son of Haran, his grandson, and Sarai his daughter-in-law, and they went out from Ur of the Chaldees to go into the land of Canaan. But they only got as far as a place called Haran, and they settled there. And Terah died in Haran.

That is where the first eleven chapters of Genesis end. In a town along the road, halfway to somewhere, with a journey begun and then abandoned, and an old man buried, and a son named Abram who has not yet heard his name called.

He is about to. But for now, rest in Haran a while, in the pause between the first world and the next one. The bow is in the cloud. The languages are scattered like seed. And the road south is waiting, quiet under the stars.
