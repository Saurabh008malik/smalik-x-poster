## Chapter 10: Count the Stars

Years went by in the hill country, and the promise did not arrive. Abram grew older. Sarai grew older. There was no child.

One night, the word of the Lord came to Abram in a vision. Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward. And Abram, for the first time in the story, answered back. Lord God, what will you give me, since I go childless? He said it twice, in two different ways, the way a person does when something has been pressing on them for a long time. You have given me no children. A servant born in my house will be my heir.

And God took him outside. Here are the old words.

The Lord brought him outside, and said, "Look now toward the sky, and count the stars, if you are able to count them." He said to Abram, "So shall your seed be." He believed in the Lord; and he reckoned it to him for righteousness.

Picture the two of them. An old man led out of his tent into the open, at night, in a country with no cities near and no lamps, under a sky so thick with stars that it looks like spilled salt. Count them, if you are able. And then the promise. So shall your offspring be. And Abram believed. He had no evidence. He had an empty tent and a wife past the age of bearing children. He had the stars, and a voice. And he believed, and the story says that this was counted to him as righteousness. Not his building of altars. Not his generosity to Lot. Just that, standing under the sky, he took God at his word.

If you cannot sleep tonight, you are in good company. One of the most important moments in the Bible happened to a man who was awake in the dark, looking up.

But believing a promise and living inside its delay are two different things, and the next chapter is about the delay. Sarai had borne Abram no children. She had an Egyptian servant whose name was Hagar. And Sarai said to Abram, see now, the Lord has restrained me from bearing. Please go in to my servant. It may be that I will obtain children by her. This was a custom of the time and place. A child born to a servant could be counted as the mistress's own. Abram listened to Sarai.

Hagar conceived. And when she saw that she had conceived, her mistress was despised in her eyes. And Sarai, in her hurt, dealt harshly with Hagar, and Hagar ran away into the wilderness.

What happens next is one of the quiet wonders of Genesis. The angel of the Lord found Hagar by a spring of water in the wilderness, on the way to Shur. Not Abram. Not Sarai. Hagar. The Egyptian servant, the one with no promise and no standing, pregnant and alone by a desert spring. And the angel called her by name. Hagar, Sarai's servant, where did you come from, and where are you going? And he told her to go back, and that her child would be a son, and that she should name him Ishmael, which means God hears, because the Lord had heard her affliction.

And Hagar gave God a name. She is the only person in the whole Bible who does that. She called the name of the Lord who spoke to her, You are a God who sees. Have I even stayed alive after seeing him, she said. And the well was called Beer Lahai Roi, the well of the living one who sees me.

The God who sees me. A runaway servant girl in the desert said that first. Hold on to it, if you are someone who feels unseen.

Hagar went back and bore a son, and Abram named him Ishmael. Abram was eighty-six years old.

Thirteen more years passed. Thirteen. Then, when Abram was ninety-nine, the Lord appeared to him again and made the covenant more solemn than before. I am God Almighty, he said. Walk before me, and be blameless. And then he changed Abram's name. No longer Abram, which means exalted father, but Abraham, father of a multitude. And Sarai would be Sarah, which means princess. I will bless her, God said, and I will give you a son by her. She will be a mother of nations. Kings of peoples will come from her.

And Abraham fell on his face and laughed. He said in his heart, will a child be born to him who is one hundred years old? Will Sarah, who is ninety years old, give birth? And he said out loud, oh that Ishmael might live before you. He was asking God, gently, to settle for the son he already had. And God said no. Sarah your wife will bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac, which means he laughs. And as for Ishmael, I have heard you. I have blessed him, and will make him fruitful. Twelve princes will come from him. But my covenant I will establish with Isaac, whom Sarah will bear to you at this set time next year.

At this set time next year. After twenty-four years of waiting, the promise finally had a date.

And now we come to the oaks of Mamre, on a hot afternoon. Here are the old words.

The Lord appeared to him by the oaks of Mamre, as he sat in the tent door in the heat of the day. He lifted up his eyes and looked, and saw that three men stood opposite him. When he saw them, he ran to meet them from the tent door, and bowed himself to the earth, and said, "My lord, if now I have found favor in your sight, please don't go away from your servant. Now let a little water be fetched, wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree. I will get a morsel of bread so you can refresh your heart. After that you may go your way, now that you have come to your servant." They said, "Very well, do as you have said."

A morsel of bread, he calls it, and then he runs into the tent and tells Sarah to knead three measures of fine meal and make cakes, and runs to the herd and picks out a tender calf and gives it to a servant to prepare, and brings curds and milk and the calf, and stands by them under the tree while they eat. The little morsel becomes a feast. Abraham is ninety-nine years old and he is running. The whole scene is full of hurry and hospitality.

And then one of the guests says, where is Sarah your wife? In the tent, Abraham says. And the guest says, I will certainly return to you about this time next year, and behold, Sarah your wife will have a son.

Sarah was listening at the tent door behind him. And she laughed within herself. After I have grown old, she thought, will I have pleasure, my lord being old also? And the Lord said to Abraham, why did Sarah laugh? Is anything too hard for the Lord? And Sarah, frightened, said, I did not laugh. And he said, no, but you did laugh.

It is such a human moment. An old woman, listening behind a tent flap, laughing at the absurdity of it, and then caught, and denying it, and being told, gently, no, but you did. The story does not scold her. Her laugh is going to become her son's name. God was not offended by the laughter. He was going to turn it into joy.

That is where we leave them tonight, under the oaks, in the heat of the day, with the meal finished and the promise spoken and a woman in the tent who cannot quite believe it. The three visitors are about to rise and look toward Sodom, and a harder story is coming. But it can wait for the next chapter. For now, there is shade, and curds and milk, and the echo of a laugh that is going to turn out to be the right response after all.
