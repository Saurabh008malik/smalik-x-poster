## Chapter 11: Laughter in the Tent

The three visitors rose from the meal and looked out toward Sodom, down in the plain where Lot had gone to live. And Abraham walked with them a little way, to see them on their road, as a good host does.

What happens next is a conversation, and it is one of the boldest conversations in the Bible. The Lord told Abraham that the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah was great, and their sin very grievous, and that he was going down to see. And Abraham, the old man who had run to fetch water for strangers an hour before, stepped closer and began to bargain.

Will you consume the righteous with the wicked, he asked. What if there are fifty righteous within the city? Will you not spare the place for the fifty? Far be it from you to do such a thing. Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right? And the Lord said, if I find in Sodom fifty righteous within the city, I will spare the whole place for their sake.

And Abraham, who knew exactly how it sounded, kept going. Behold now, I have taken it on myself to speak to the Lord, although I am dust and ashes. What if there lack five of the fifty? Will you destroy the city for lack of five? I will not destroy it, if I find forty-five there. What if there are forty? For the forty's sake I will not do it. Oh do not let the Lord be angry, and I will speak. What if thirty? I will not do it if I find thirty there. Behold now, I have taken it on myself to speak. What if twenty? I will not destroy it for the twenty's sake. Oh do not let the Lord be angry, and I will speak just once more. What if ten are found there? And he said, I will not destroy it for the ten's sake.

Fifty, forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten. Each time Abraham apologizes, and each time he asks again, and each time God says yes. The old readers were struck by this. God lets himself be argued with. More than that, he seems to want it. He told Abraham what he was going to do, as if inviting the argument. And a man made of dust and ashes stood on a hillside and pleaded for a city full of strangers, and was heard every single time.

Ten righteous were not found. You probably know the rest. Two of the visitors went down to Sodom as evening fell, and Lot met them at the gate and brought them into his house, and the men of the city surrounded the house with evil intent, and the visitors struck them blind and told Lot to gather his family and run. In the morning, Lot and his wife and two daughters were led out by the hand, because they lingered, and told to flee to the mountains and not look back. Fire fell on the cities of the plain. And Lot's wife looked back from behind him, and became a pillar of salt.

I am going to let that story stay in the distance tonight, the way the text itself does. Abraham saw it from far off. He got up early in the morning and went to the place where he had stood before the Lord, and looked out toward Sodom and Gomorrah, and saw the smoke of the land going up like the smoke of a furnace. And the story adds one line that matters. God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the middle of the overthrow. The pleading on the hillside was not wasted. It saved the one family that could be saved.

And then, the very next chapter, the long-promised thing happens, so simply that you could almost miss it.

The Lord visited Sarah as he had said, and the Lord did to Sarah as he had spoken. Sarah conceived, and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him. Abraham called his son Isaac. Abraham was one hundred years old when his son Isaac was born to him.

Here are Sarah's own words, in the old translation.

Sarah said, "God has made me laugh. Everyone who hears will laugh with me." She said, "Who would have said to Abraham, that Sarah would nurse children? For I have borne him a son in his old age."

God has made me laugh. The same woman who laughed behind the tent flap in disbelief now laughs in the open, holding a baby. And she invites everyone to laugh with her. The child's name, Isaac, means he laughs, and the household must have said it a hundred times a day. Isaac. Laughter. Twenty-five years after a voice in Haran, a ninety-year-old woman is nursing a child, and the whole camp is laughing.

But the story is honest, and honesty includes the hard parts. There was still another son in the camp. Ishmael, Hagar's boy, was a teenager now. And on the day Isaac was weaned, Abraham made a great feast, and Sarah saw Ishmael mocking, and something in her hardened. Cast out this servant and her son, she said to Abraham, for the son of this servant will not be heir with my son Isaac. And the thing was very grievous in Abraham's sight, because of his son. He loved Ishmael.

God told Abraham to listen to Sarah, and promised that Ishmael too would become a nation, because he was Abraham's offspring. So Abraham rose up early in the morning, and took bread and a container of water, and gave it to Hagar, putting it on her shoulder, and gave her the child, and sent her away.

She wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba. The water ran out. She put the boy under one of the shrubs, and went and sat down a good way off, about a bowshot, because she said, do not let me see the death of the child. And she lifted up her voice and wept.

And God heard the voice of the boy. Of course he did. The boy's name was Ishmael. God hears. The angel of God called to Hagar out of the sky and said, what troubles you, Hagar? Do not be afraid. God has heard the voice of the boy where he is. Get up, lift up the boy, and hold him with your hand, for I will make him a great nation. And God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. She went and filled the container with water and gave the boy a drink.

She saw a well. It had been there. Her eyes were opened to it. The God who sees had seen her twice now, in two different deserts, and both times the answer was water.

And God was with the boy, the story says, and he grew, and lived in the wilderness, and became an archer. His mother found him a wife from the land of Egypt. Ishmael's story goes on, outside the main road of the book, but not outside the care of the one who named him.

So the camp at Beersheba has two stories in it tonight. A child of laughter, growing up in the tents, and a child of hearing, growing up in the wild, each one blessed, each one seen. Let both of them sleep. Let Sarah sleep, and Hagar, and the old man who loved both his sons and had to let one go.

There is one more hard hill to climb before this night is over. But after it, there is a well, and a girl with a water jar, and a love story. Rest until then.
