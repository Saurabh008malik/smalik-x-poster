## Chapter 7: The Dove and the Olive Leaf

The rain fell for forty days and forty nights. The waters rose, and lifted the ship, and it floated. The waters prevailed on the earth, the story says, until all the high mountains under the whole sky were covered. And everything that breathed on the dry land died. Only Noah was left, and those who were with him in the ship.

The old text does not dwell on the drowning. It says it plainly and moves on, and I am going to do the same. What it dwells on instead is the waiting. The waters prevailed on the earth one hundred fifty days. Five months of grey water in every direction, and no land, and no sign.

And then the eighth chapter begins with four words that are among the most comforting in scripture. God remembered Noah.

Not that God had forgotten him. The old word for remember means something closer to turning toward, attending to, acting on behalf of. God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the livestock that were with him in the ship. And God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters began to go down. The fountains of the deep were stopped. The windows of the sky were closed. The rain from the sky was restrained.

You may notice that this is the second chapter of Genesis to begin with wind, or spirit, moving over the waters. In the beginning the Spirit of God hovered over the face of the deep, and a world came out of it. Now a wind passes over the flood, and a world comes out of it again. The flood is not only an ending. The story tells it as a second beginning. The earth is being made new out of the same deep water it was made from the first time.

The ship came to rest, in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, on the mountains of Ararat. The waters kept going down. In the tenth month, the tops of the mountains were seen. And then Noah did something careful and patient, which I will read to you in the old words, because it is one of the loveliest passages in the whole book.

It happened at the end of forty days, that Noah opened the window of the ship which he had made, and he sent forth a raven. It went back and forth, until the waters were dried up from the earth. He sent forth a dove from him, to see if the waters were abated from the surface of the ground, but the dove found no place to rest her foot, and she returned to him into the ship; for the waters were on the surface of the whole earth. He put forth his hand, and took her, and brought her to him into the ship. He stayed yet another seven days; and again he sent forth the dove out of the ship. The dove came back to him at evening, and, behold, in her mouth was an olive leaf plucked off. So Noah knew that the waters were abated from the earth. He stayed yet another seven days, and sent forth the dove; and she didn't return to him any more.

Look at how much tenderness is packed into that passage. The dove comes back the first time with nothing, tired, and Noah puts out his hand and takes her and brings her in. He does not just open the window. He reaches out and gathers her up. Then he waits a week. He is in no hurry. The second time she comes back at evening, and in her beak is a single olive leaf, freshly plucked. One leaf. The first green thing anyone had seen in the better part of a year. And from one leaf, Noah knew. The third time, she did not come back at all, because she had found somewhere to live.

An olive leaf, carried in at evening. There are worse pictures to fall asleep on. It is the smallest possible proof that the world is going to be all right.

Noah removed the covering of the ship and looked, and the surface of the ground was dry. And God spoke to him. Go out of the ship, you and your wife and your sons and your sons' wives with you. Bring out with you every living thing that is with you, birds and livestock and every creeping thing, that they may breed abundantly in the earth, and be fruitful, and multiply. So Noah went out. And every animal, every creeping thing, every bird, went out of the ship by their families. By their families. The old words even give the animals families.

The first thing Noah did on the new earth was build an altar, and offer a sacrifice. And the Lord smelled the sweet savor, the story says, and said in his heart, I will not again curse the ground any more for man's sake, because the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth. Notice what that means. God does not say that people have become good and so deserve a second chance. He says that they are what they are, and he will keep the world going anyway. The promise is not based on us. That is what makes it a promise you can rest on.

And then this, which I will read slowly.

While the earth remains, seed time and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.

Seed time and harvest. Cold and heat. Summer and winter. Day and night. The rhythm of the whole world, promised. Every spring that has ever come after a winter has been the keeping of that promise. Every morning after a night. Including the one that is coming toward you right now, slowly, from the far side of the earth.

That is where we will leave the ship for tonight, empty on the mountain, its door open, the animals going down the slopes by their families, the smoke from the altar rising, and the sky, for the first time in a long time, blue.

In a moment, there will be a rainbow. But you can close your eyes before it comes. It will still be there.
