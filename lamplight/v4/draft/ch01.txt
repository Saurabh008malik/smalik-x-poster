## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Boy with the Sheep

Good evening, and welcome. Settle in. Let the pillow take the weight of your head, and let your hands lie open and loose. The day is over now. Whatever it asked of you, it cannot ask anything more tonight.

This is Lamplight Scripture. Tonight we are going to walk, slowly, through the life of David. A shepherd boy on the hills above Bethlehem. A young musician playing the harp for a troubled king. A stone, a sling, and a giant in a valley. A friendship that the old writers said was knit together soul to soul. Years of hiding in caves and deserts. A crown, a city, a great wrong and a great sorrow, and, at the very end, an old man's song. His story fills the two books of Samuel and the opening of the first book of Kings, and many of the Psalms carry his name.

I will tell it in plain words, gently, the way you might tell a story to someone half asleep beside you. And now and then I will read the old words themselves, from a public domain translation called the World English Bible. Where the old text uses the holy name of God, I will say the Lord, as many readers do. You do not have to follow every step. If you drift away somewhere on the road, the story will keep walking without you, and it will be here when you come back.

Let us begin, not with David, but with an old man and a heavy heart.

Long ago, after the people of Israel had come out of Egypt and settled in the hill country of Canaan, they lived for many generations without a king. Leaders called judges rose up in times of trouble, and then the land would rest again. The last of these great leaders was a prophet named Samuel. He had been given to God as a small child, by a mother who had prayed for him with tears, and he had grown up in the house of the Lord at Shiloh. All his life, people came to him for guidance.

But when Samuel was old, the people asked him for a king. They wanted to be like the nations around them, with a king to lead them and fight their battles. Samuel was grieved, but in the end he did as they asked. He anointed a tall young man from the tribe of Benjamin, a man named Saul, who stood head and shoulders above everyone else. For a time Saul did well. He gathered the tribes, and he led them against their enemies.

But Saul did not listen. More than once, when the word of the Lord came to him through Samuel, he went his own way. And the old writers tell us that the Lord rejected Saul from being king, and that Samuel mourned for him. Saul would go on sitting on the throne for many years, but in the story, something had already ended.

And so we come to a quiet morning, and an old prophet with a horn of oil.

{{1SA 16:1}}

Bethlehem was a small town in the hills of Judah, a few hours' walk south of where Jerusalem stands today. It sat on a ridge, with terraced slopes falling away below it, olive trees and fig trees and patches of barley, and beyond them the long brown folds of the wilderness running down toward the Dead Sea. Long before David was born, this was where Ruth the Moabite had gleaned in the barley fields and married Boaz. Their son was Obed. And Obed's son was Jesse. So Jesse was a grandson of Ruth and Boaz, a farmer and a keeper of sheep, with a family of eight sons.

Samuel was afraid. If Saul heard that the old prophet had gone to anoint someone else, it could cost him his life. So the Lord told him to take a heifer with him, and to say that he had come to offer a sacrifice. And he was to invite Jesse to the sacrifice.

When Samuel came into Bethlehem, the elders of the town came out to meet him, trembling a little, and asked whether he came in peace. Peace, he said. I have come to sacrifice to the Lord. And he invited Jesse and his sons.

Let us picture it. A small gathering in the evening light. The smoke of the offering rising straight up into a still sky. And Jesse's sons, one by one, coming forward to stand before the prophet.

The first was Eliab, the eldest. He was tall, and fine to look at, and Samuel thought, surely this is the one. Surely the Lord's anointed is standing in front of me.

Here are the old words.

{{1SA 16:7}}

So Eliab stepped back. Then Jesse called Abinadab, the second son, and the Lord had not chosen him either. Then Shammah. Then another, and another. Seven sons passed before Samuel, and to each one Samuel said, quietly, the Lord has not chosen this one.

And then the old prophet looked at Jesse and asked a simple question.

{{1SA 16:11}}

Imagine the pause while someone ran out of the town and down the slope to the pastures, calling the boy's name. Imagine the youngest son, out among the sheep, perhaps in the long evening shadows, perhaps sitting on a rock with a little harp across his knees, looking up to see a brother running toward him. Come home. Come now. There is a prophet here, and they will not sit down to eat until you come.

And then he came in.

{{1SA 16:12-13}}

That is all. No crowd, no crown, no trumpet. Just a small family in a small town, a boy with oil running down through his hair, and an old man who saddled his donkey and went home to Ramah. The boy went back to the sheep. Nothing had changed that anyone could see.

But the old writers say that from that day on, the Spirit of the Lord came upon David.

It is worth staying with that young shepherd for a moment, before the story takes him away from the hills. Later in his life, David would tell a king how he had kept his father's sheep, and how when a lion or a bear came and took a lamb from the flock, he went after it and struck it and rescued the lamb from its mouth. Shepherding in those hills was not a gentle hobby. It meant long days and cold nights, finding water and grass in dry country, leading the flock along narrow paths, counting them in at night, sleeping beside the fold under the stars.

Many people, reading the Psalms, have imagined that young shepherd somewhere behind the most famous song of all. The Psalm carries the heading "A Psalm by David", and whatever the full story of how it was written, it sounds like a man who has spent his nights with sheep. Let us hear it now, slowly, as the first evening of our story settles over Bethlehem.

{{PSA 23:1-6}}

The sheep are folded now. The fire is low. Somewhere on a hillside above a little town, a boy who does not yet know what he will become is lying on his back, looking at the stars.

Let us leave him there for a while.
