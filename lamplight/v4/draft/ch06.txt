## Chapter 6: The Cave at En Gedi, and a Wise Woman

Welcome back. Let your shoulders drop. Tonight's road takes us down to the shore of the Dead Sea, to one of the loveliest places in all that dry country.

En Gedi means something like the spring of the young goat. Even today it is a green surprise in the desert. Water comes out of the cliffs high above the sea and falls in thin white waterfalls into pools among the rocks. There are reeds and shrubs and trees around the springs, and wild ibex, the mountain goats with long curved horns, still climb the cliffs there and come down in the evening to drink. Below, the Dead Sea lies flat and still and silver, and across it, the mountains of Moab turn pink and then purple at sunset. The limestone cliffs above the spring are full of caves.

When Saul came back from fighting the Philistines, someone told him that David was in the wilderness of En Gedi. So he took three thousand chosen men and went to look for him on the rocks of the wild goats.

And then something happened that sounds almost like a parable.

On the way there were sheepfolds, built against the cliff, and a cave. And Saul went into the cave alone, to relieve himself. He did not know that David and his men were sitting in the dark at the back of that very cave.

Imagine it. The king, alone, unguarded, his back to the darkness. The men at the back of the cave whispering. This is the day, they said. This is the day the Lord spoke of, when he said, I will deliver your enemy into your hand, and you shall do to him as it seems good to you.

David crept forward, silently, through the dark. And he did not strike. He took his knife and quietly cut off a corner of the king's robe. Then he went back to his men. And even that, the old writers say, troubled his heart afterwards. He said to his men, the Lord forbid that I should do this thing to my lord, the Lord's anointed, to put out my hand against him. And he would not let them attack Saul.

Saul got up, and left the cave, and went on his way. And then David came out after him, and called down from the rocks.

{{1SA 24:8-12}}

Picture the king turning around. Below him, the path and his soldiers. Above him, on a ledge outside the cave, the young man he had been hunting for so long, bowed down to the ground, holding up a piece of cloth from his own robe.

{{1SA 24:16-17}}

For a moment, the hard old king was weeping. He asked David to promise that when he became king, he would not wipe out Saul's family. And David promised. Then Saul went home, and David and his men went back up to the stronghold. They both knew, perhaps, that it would not last. But for that one afternoon, by the springs of En Gedi, there was peace between them.

Around this time, the old prophet died.

{{1SA 25:1}}

The man who had anointed both Saul and David, the last of the judges, the boy who had once heard his name called in the night in the house of the Lord, was gone. All Israel mourned for him.

And then comes a story that has nothing to do with kings, and everything to do with how anger can be turned aside by wisdom.

In the wilderness of Maon, near a place called Carmel, which is a village in Judah and not the famous mountain in the north, lived a very rich man named Nabal. He had three thousand sheep and a thousand goats. His name, in Hebrew, sounds like the word for a fool. His wife was named Abigail, and the old writers give us a very quick portrait of them both. She was of good understanding, and beautiful. He was harsh and evil in his doings.

During all those months in the wilderness, David's men had camped near Nabal's shepherds and protected them. They had taken nothing. They had been, as one of Nabal's own servants said later, a wall around them by night and by day. Now it was sheep-shearing time, which was a time of feasting. So David sent ten young men to Nabal, with a polite greeting, to ask for whatever he could spare for them.

Nabal answered them with contempt. Who is David, he said? Who is the son of Jesse? There are many servants these days who break away from their masters. Shall I take my bread and my water and my meat that I have killed for my shearers, and give it to men I do not know?

When the young men told David, he was furious. Every man, he said, put on your sword. And four hundred of them set out to take revenge on Nabal's household.

But one of Nabal's servants ran to Abigail and told her everything. And Abigail did not wait.

{{1SA 25:18-19}}

She rode down a hidden path on the mountain, and there, coming the other way, were David and his men. She got down quickly from her donkey, bowed to the ground before him, and spoke. It is a long and wise speech. She took the blame on herself. She asked him not to pay any attention to her foolish husband. And then she reminded him of who he was, and who he would be, and asked him not to stain his future with blood shed in anger.

{{1SA 25:32-33}}

So David turned back. He took her gifts, and said to her, go up in peace to your house.

When Abigail got home, Nabal was holding a feast like the feast of a king, and he was very drunk. So she told him nothing until the morning. And when she told him, his heart failed within him, and he became like a stone. About ten days later, he died. And when David heard of it, he sent word to Abigail, asking her to be his wife. She rose, and went with his messengers, and became David's wife.

It is a small story, tucked between the larger ones, but it is worth keeping. Twice, in these two chapters, David had the chance to take what he wanted by force. Once in a dark cave, and once on a mountain path. Once he held himself back. And once, a wise and courageous woman held him back. And both times, the old writers seem to say, it was the better way.

Let the evening come down over En Gedi now. The waterfall is soft in the dusk. The ibex have come down to the pools to drink. The sea is grey and still, and the mountains across it are fading into the dark.
