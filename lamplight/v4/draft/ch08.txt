## Chapter 8: A King in Hebron, and a City on a Hill

Welcome back. Let your hands grow heavy. The long years of running are over now, and the shepherd is about to become a king.

After the mourning, David did not rush. The old writers tell us that he asked the Lord, shall I go up into any of the cities of Judah? And the answer came, go up. Where shall I go, he asked? To Hebron.

Hebron is an ancient town in the hills south of Bethlehem, older than almost any other place in our story. Abraham had lived near there, under the great oaks of Mamre, and he had bought a cave there to bury Sarah, and in time he was buried there too, and Isaac and Jacob after him. It was the heart of the tribe of Judah. So David went up to Hebron, with his wives, Ahinoam and Abigail, and his six hundred men and all their households, and they settled in the towns around it.

{{2SA 2:4}}

But it was only Judah, his own tribe. In the north, Saul's old commander Abner took one of Saul's surviving sons, a man named Ish-bosheth, and made him king over the rest of Israel. And so for some years there were two kingdoms, and a long, weary war between them. The old writers sum it up in one line.

{{2SA 3:1}}

This part of the story is full of hard men. On David's side were the sons of his sister Zeruiah: Joab, his fierce and clever commander, Abishai, and young Asahel, who was as light on his feet as a wild gazelle. On the other side was Abner, old and battle-hardened. In one skirmish, Asahel chased Abner and would not turn aside, and Abner killed him. Later, Joab killed Abner in revenge, at the gate of Hebron, even though Abner had come in peace to make terms with David.

David was grieved and angry. He made the people mourn, and he himself walked behind Abner's coffin, and wept aloud at his grave. Do you not know, he said to his servants, that a prince and a great man has fallen this day in Israel? And all the people saw it, and it pleased them. They understood that the king had had no part in the killing.

Not long afterwards, Ish-bosheth too was killed, by two of his own captains, who came to David expecting a reward. They received none. David would not build his throne on murder.

And then the long war was over. All the tribes of Israel came to Hebron.

{{2SA 5:3}}

{{2SA 5:4-5}}

Thirty years old. If you remember the boy in the sheepfold at Bethlehem, and the young man with the harp, and the long years in the caves, it is a little startling. David had spent his whole youth waiting. Now his reign would last forty years.

And the first thing he did as king of all Israel was to look for a capital.

There was a city in the hills between Judah and the northern tribes that no Israelite had ever held. It sat on a narrow ridge, with steep valleys on three sides, and a spring at the foot of its eastern slope. It was held by a people called the Jebusites, and its walls were so strong that its defenders mocked David. You will not come in here, they said. Even the blind and the lame could keep you out.

The city was called Jerusalem.

The account of how David took it is very brief, and some of its words are hard to translate. It mentions a water shaft, or a channel, which David's men went up. For a long time, people have connected it with an ancient tunnel and shaft near the Gihon spring, at the foot of the old city, which can still be seen today, though archaeologists now think the visible shaft may be later than David's time. However it was done, the city fell.

{{2SA 5:7}}

{{2SA 5:9-10}}

Jerusalem was a good choice, and a wise one. It belonged to no single tribe, so neither the north nor the south could claim it. It stood near the border between them. It had water, and strong walls, and a high place for a king's house. A king of the coastal city of Tyre, named Hiram, sent cedar trees from Lebanon, and carpenters and stonemasons, and they built David a house.

{{2SA 5:12}}

Let us stand for a moment on that ridge, in the evening, the way David might have stood. Below, the Kidron valley falling away to the east, already in shadow. Beyond it, the long slope of the Mount of Olives, catching the last light. To the south, the hills rolling down toward Bethlehem, where a boy once kept his father's sheep. The smell of fresh cedar wood from the new house. Smoke rising from the evening fires in the little city. Swallows wheeling over the walls.

It had been a long road from the sheepfold to this place. A harp, a stone, a cave, a spear, a song for the dead. Now the shepherd was a king, in a city of his own, and for the first time in many years, he could sleep without listening for footsteps in the dark.

Let us let him sleep tonight. And let us sleep a little too.
