## Chapter 7: A Spear, a Water Jar, and the Mountains of Gilboa

Welcome back. Let your breathing find its own slow pace. This chapter carries some sorrow in it, but we will walk through it gently.

Once more, the people of Ziph went to Saul and told him where David was hiding. And once more Saul came, with his three thousand chosen men, and camped on a hill in the wilderness.

That night, David went out quietly to look at the camp. He could see where Saul lay asleep, inside a ring of baggage and wagons, with Abner, his commander, lying beside him, and the whole army sleeping in a circle around them. David asked who would go down with him into the camp. A young warrior named Abishai, the son of David's sister Zeruiah, said, I will go down with you.

So in the deep of the night, two men crept down the hill and walked into the middle of the sleeping army.

{{1SA 26:7-9}}

And David added, the Lord will strike him, or his day will come to die, or he will go down into battle and perish. But the Lord forbid that I should stretch out my hand against the Lord's anointed. Then he said, take the spear that is at his head, and the jar of water, and let us go.

{{1SA 26:12}}

A deep sleep. Let us linger on that for a moment, since it is what we are reaching for ourselves tonight. Three thousand men, their armour laid aside, their fires burned down to embers, all of them breathing slow and deep under the stars. Not one of them stirring. Two shadows moving among them, silent as the night wind. A spear standing upright in the ground beside a sleeping king's head, and a clay water jar beside it. And then the shadows were gone, back up the hill, the spear and the jar with them.

From the top of the far hill, a long way off, David called out across the valley to Abner. Are you not a valiant man? Why have you not kept watch over your lord the king? See where the king's spear is, and the jar of water that was at his head.

Saul knew his voice. Is this your voice, my son David? And David said, it is my voice, my lord, O king. Why does my lord pursue his servant? What have I done?

And Saul said, I have sinned. Come back, my son David. I will not harm you any more, because my life was precious in your eyes today. Behold, I have played the fool, and have erred exceedingly.

David sent back the spear. And they parted, for the last time. Saul went home. David went his way. They would never meet again.

David did not trust the peace. He said in his heart, one of these days I will perish by the hand of Saul. So he did something surprising. He took his six hundred men and their families and went over to the Philistines. He went to Achish, the king of Gath, and Achish received him, and gave him a small town of his own called Ziklag, on the southern edge of the land. David lived there for a year and four months. Saul stopped looking for him.

In those years, the old writers tell us, David led raids against desert tribes to the south, while letting Achish believe he was raiding his own people. It is not a story that the book of Samuel tries to make pretty. David was a man in a hard place, making hard choices, and not all of them were good.

Then the Philistines gathered all their armies for a great war against Israel. Achish wanted David to march with him. But the other Philistine lords would not have it. Is this not David, they said, the one of the song? So David was sent back to Ziklag. And when he and his men got there, on the third day, they found the town burned and empty. Raiders from the desert had come while they were away, and had carried off all the women and children, David's own wives among them.

The men wept until they had no more strength to weep. They were so bitter that they spoke of stoning David. And then the text gives us a short line that many people have carried with them through hard days of their own.

{{1SA 30:6}}

David and his men followed the raiders south, found them, and brought back every person who had been taken, small and great, and nothing was lost.

But far to the north, on that same day or near it, the great battle was being fought.

The mountains of Gilboa are a long ridge on the edge of a wide valley in the north of the land. The Philistines came against Israel there with all their strength, and Israel fled before them. Jonathan was killed, and two of his brothers. The battle went hard against Saul, and the archers found him, and he was badly wounded. And rather than be taken by his enemies, the old king fell upon his own sword. The text tells it simply and without blame. The next day, when the Philistines found the bodies, they took them to a town called Beth Shan and fastened them to its wall. And the men of Jabesh Gilead, a town across the Jordan that Saul had rescued in the first days of his reign, many years before, walked all night, took the bodies down, and carried them home, and buried them under a tamarisk tree, and fasted seven days.

They remembered what Saul had once done for them.

The news reached David at Ziklag, by a messenger with torn clothes and earth on his head. And David tore his own clothes, and he and all his men mourned and wept and fasted until the evening, for Saul, and for Jonathan, and for the people of the Lord.

Let us be quiet for a little while, after that song. Somewhere across the Jordan, under a tamarisk tree, a king and his son are sleeping. Somewhere in the south, a man who loved them both is sitting alone outside a burned town, with his head in his hands, as the evening comes on.

Rest now. The sorrow does not have to be carried all at once.
