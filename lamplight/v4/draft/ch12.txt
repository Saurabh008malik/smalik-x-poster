## Chapter 12: Last Words, a Song, and Goodnight

Welcome back, for the last part of our walk tonight. Let everything go soft now. The story is coming home.

The book of Samuel ends, not with a final battle, but with a song. In the twenty-second chapter of second Samuel, David sings a long song of thanks, on the day that the Lord delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul. The same song appears again in the book of Psalms, as Psalm eighteen. It is full of rock and fortress and shield, of storms and deep waters, of a God who reaches down from on high. Here are a few of its lines.

{{2SA 22:2-3}}

{{2SA 22:17}}

{{2SA 22:29}}

You are my lamp. That is a good line for a channel called Lamplight. A lamp in the dark, not to light up the whole world, only the next step, and the room you are in, and the face of the one beside you.

Then the text gives us something it calls the last words of David. They are short, and they look back across his whole life, from the shepherd's field to the throne.

{{2SA 23:1}}

{{2SA 23:3-4}}

Like the light of the morning when the sun rises, a morning without clouds. Like the tender grass springing out of the earth, through clear shining after rain. Those are the words of someone who had spent many dawns on the hills with his sheep, watching the light come up over the ridges, and the grass shining after the night rain.

In that same chapter, there is a little story from David's younger days, set among the lists of his mighty men. Once, when David was in the cave of Adullam, and the Philistines held Bethlehem, he was thirsty, and he said, with a sigh, oh that someone would give me water to drink from the well of Bethlehem, which is by the gate. He was only longing for home. But three of his mighty men heard it. They broke through the Philistine camp, drew water from the well by the gate of Bethlehem, and carried it back to him. And David would not drink it. He poured it out on the ground, as an offering to the Lord. Is this not the blood of the men who went in jeopardy of their lives, he said? It was too precious to drink.

That small story says a great deal. About loyalty, and about longing for home, and about a man who knew, at least on his better days, that some gifts are too precious to keep for yourself.

The opening of the first book of Kings shows us David at the very end of his life. King David was old, and well advanced in years, and they covered him with clothes, but he could not get warm. There was a last struggle in his household over who would be king after him. Another of his sons, Adonijah, tried to take the throne. But Bathsheba, and Nathan the prophet, came to the old king and reminded him of his promise. And David gave his command.

The young prince Solomon was set on the king's own mule, and taken down to the spring of Gihon, at the foot of the city, the same spring that had watered the old Jebusite town. There Zadok the priest took a horn of oil from the tent of the Lord and anointed him. They blew the trumpet, and all the people said, long live King Solomon. And the people went up after him, playing on pipes and rejoicing so loudly that the earth seemed to shake with the sound.

A horn of oil, poured over a young man's head. That is where David's story began, long ago in a small town in the hills, with an old prophet and a shepherd boy who had been called in from the field. And now it ends the same way, with the old shepherd king lying in his bed, hearing the trumpets and the pipes in the distance, and knowing that his son had been anointed.

When the news was brought to him, the old king bowed himself upon the bed. And he said, blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, who has given one to sit on my throne this day, my eyes even seeing it.

Then David spoke to his son, and gave him his charge. Here is part of it.

{{1KI 2:1-3}}

{{1KI 2:10-11}}

Forty years. Seven in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem. A shepherd, a musician, a soldier, a fugitive, a poet, a king. A man of great courage and great faith, and great failures too, who never stopped turning back to God. His son would build the temple he had dreamed of. And for a thousand years and more, the people of Israel would remember him as the shepherd king, and look for a son of David to come.

So let us go back, one last time, to where we began. Let us leave the palace and the city behind, and walk south, a few hours along the ridge road, to the little town of Bethlehem. The night is very clear. The terraced hills are dark and quiet. Somewhere out on the slopes, a young shepherd is sitting by a small fire, his flock folded in for the night, a little lyre across his knees. He is plucking a few soft notes, the same few notes, again and again, the way you might hum to a child who is nearly asleep.

And maybe the words he is thinking, as he looks up at the stars, are words that he or someone like him would one day write down, words that have been read beside sickbeds and cradles and gravesides for three thousand years. Let us hear them one more time, and then let them carry you into sleep.

{{PSA 23:1-6}}

Thank you for walking with me tonight, all the way from the sheepfold to the throne and back again. Let your breathing slow. Let your hands rest. You have done enough today. The fire is low. The flock is safe. The shepherd is watching, and the night is long and quiet.

The Lord bless you, and keep you.

Goodnight. Sleep well.
