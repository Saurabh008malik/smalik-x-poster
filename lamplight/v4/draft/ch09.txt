## Chapter 9: Dancing Before the Ark, and a Promise of a House

Welcome back. Let your eyes rest. This chapter is about joy, and about kindness, and about a promise.

For many years, the ark of the covenant had been far from the centre of Israel's life. The ark was a wooden chest overlaid with gold, made in the days of Moses in the wilderness. Inside it were the tablets of stone with the ten words from Mount Sinai. On its lid were two golden cherubim, with their wings spread out, and the people believed that the Lord was present there in a special way. It had once been captured by the Philistines in battle, and then sent back, and for many years since then it had rested quietly in the house of a man named Abinadab, in a village called Kiriath Jearim, in the hills west of Jerusalem.

Now David wanted it brought to his new city. He gathered thirty thousand chosen men of Israel, and they went to bring it up.

They set it on a new cart, pulled by oxen, and two sons of Abinadab, Uzzah and Ahio, walked with it. And David and all the people danced before the Lord with all their might, with songs and harps and lyres and tambourines and cymbals.

The story does not hide what happened next. When they came to a threshing floor, the oxen stumbled, and Uzzah reached out his hand and took hold of the ark. And he died there, beside it. In the law of Moses, the ark was not to be carried on a cart at all, but on poles on the shoulders of the Levites, and no one was to touch it. It is a hard and strange moment, and the old text does not soften it. David was angry, and afraid. How can the ark of the Lord come to me, he said? And he would not take it any further. He left it at the house of a man named Obed-edom, from Gath, and it stayed there three months.

And in those three months, the old writers say, the Lord blessed Obed-edom and all his household.

When David heard that, he went down again. This time it was carried properly, on the shoulders of men, and after they had gone six steps, David offered a sacrifice. And then the procession went on up the road toward Jerusalem.

{{2SA 6:14-15}}

Let us picture it. A long, joyful procession winding up through the hills. Trumpets and singing and the beat of the drums. Crowds lining the road. And at the front, not walking in royal robes, but dancing in a simple linen garment like a priest, the king himself, whirling and leaping with all his strength, with his face turned up to the sky.

But the day itself ended well. The ark was set in its place, inside a tent that David had pitched for it. David blessed the people in the name of the Lord, and gave every one of them, men and women, a loaf of bread, a portion of meat, and a cake of raisins. And all the people went home.

Some time later, when the land was at rest, David was sitting in his house of cedar, and he spoke to a prophet named Nathan.

{{2SA 7:2}}

David wanted to build a temple, a proper house for the Lord. And Nathan at first said, go, do all that is in your heart. But that night, the word of the Lord came to Nathan, and the answer was different. David would not build the Lord a house. Instead, the Lord would build David a house. Not a house of stone, but a house of people. A family, a line of kings that would go on after him. His son would build the temple. And the old words say this.

{{2SA 7:8-9}}

{{2SA 7:16}}

David went in and sat before the Lord, and prayed. It is a humble prayer. Who am I, he began, and what is my house, that you have brought me this far?

And now, one more small story, because it is one of the kindest in the whole of David's life.

One day David asked a question. Is there anyone still left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness for Jonathan's sake? He remembered the promise he had made in the field, years before, to his friend.

There was a servant of Saul's household named Ziba, and he told the king that yes, there was a son of Jonathan still living. His name was Mephibosheth. When he was five years old, on the day the news came from Gilboa, his nurse had picked him up and fled, and in her haste he had fallen, and he had been lame in both feet ever since. He was living quietly across the Jordan, in a place called Lo-debar.

David sent for him. And Mephibosheth came, and fell on his face before the king, no doubt afraid. In those days, a new king often did away with the family of the old one.

{{2SA 9:7}}

Mephibosheth bowed down and said, what is your servant, that you should look on such a dead dog as I am? But David gave him back all of his grandfather Saul's land, and Ziba and his sons to farm it for him. And Mephibosheth ate at the king's table, like one of the king's sons, for the rest of his days.

Let us picture that table, one evening in Jerusalem. The lamps lit. The king at the head. His sons around him. And among them, a quiet man who cannot walk well, the son of a friend who died long ago, sitting in a place of honour, because of a promise made in a field.

Rest now. Kindness keeps.
