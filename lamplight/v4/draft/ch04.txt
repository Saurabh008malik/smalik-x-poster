## Chapter 4: Jonathan, and a Song That Went Wrong

Welcome back. Get comfortable. Let your jaw unclench, and your forehead go smooth. We are back in the king's house now, the evening after the valley of Elah, and two young men are meeting for the first time.

Jonathan was Saul's eldest son, the prince, the one everyone expected to be king after his father. He was a soldier, and a brave one. Years before, he and his armour-bearer had climbed alone up a rocky cliff at a place called Michmash, and surprised a whole Philistine garrison. He was older than David, perhaps by a good many years. And when he heard the shepherd from Bethlehem speaking to his father, something happened between them that the old writers describe in a very tender way.

{{1SA 18:1-4}}

A prince giving a shepherd his own robe, and his own sword and bow and belt. In the world of that time, these were not just gifts. They were almost like handing over a part of himself. Jonathan, the heir to the throne, was saying to David, in the language of things, what I have is yours. It is one of the most loyal friendships in all of literature, and it will run like a quiet thread through the next part of our story.

David went out wherever Saul sent him, and he did well. The king set him over the men of war, and the people loved him, and so did Saul's servants. For a little while, everything seemed good.

And then, one day, came a song.

When the army came home from fighting the Philistines, the women came out of all the towns of Israel to meet King Saul, singing and dancing, with tambourines and other instruments. It was a celebration. And they sang a little refrain, back and forth, the way people sing at a festival.

{{1SA 18:7-9}}

It was only a song. The women were not trying to start anything. But Saul heard it, and something in him turned. They give David ten thousands, he thought, and me only thousands. What more can he have, except the kingdom?

From that day, Saul kept his eye on David.

The darkness came back over the king. And this time, when David played the harp for him, as he always did, Saul had a spear in his hand. The old text tells us that twice Saul threw the spear, meaning to pin David to the wall, and twice David slipped out of the way.

It is a hard thing to picture, after the gentle evenings of the harp. The same room, the same lamplight, the same music. But now the king was afraid of the musician, because, as the text says, the Lord was with David, and had departed from Saul. Fear has a way of turning love into suspicion, and suspicion into anger.

Saul tried other ways. He made David a commander of a thousand and sent him out into battle, perhaps hoping the Philistines would do what he could not. But David came home again and again, and the people loved him more and more. Saul offered him his daughter in marriage. His younger daughter Michal loved David, and Saul thought that through her, David might be caught. The price he set for the marriage was the death of a hundred Philistines in battle, still hoping David would fall. David came back with twice that number. So David married Michal, the king's daughter. And Saul, the old writers say, was still more afraid of him.

Then the king spoke openly to Jonathan, and to all his servants, about killing David.

Jonathan went to his friend and told him to hide. Then he went to his father, and spoke up for David. He has never done you wrong, Jonathan said. He put his life in his hand and killed the Philistine, and the Lord gave a great victory to all Israel. You saw it, and you rejoiced. Why would you sin against innocent blood? And Saul listened to his son. As the Lord lives, he said, he shall not be put to death. For a little while, David was back at the king's side.

But it did not last. Once more the darkness came, once more the spear, and this time David fled to his own house. That night Saul sent men to watch the house and kill him in the morning. It was Michal who warned him. She let him down through a window, and he ran away into the dark. Then she took a household image, laid it in the bed, put a pillow of goat's hair at its head, and covered it with a cloth. When the messengers came, she told them David was sick. By the time they found out, he was far away.

David went first to Samuel at Ramah, and then, in secret, he came back to find Jonathan. He was frightened and bewildered. What have I done, he asked? What is my sin against your father, that he seeks my life?

Jonathan could hardly believe it. His father did nothing, he said, great or small, without telling him. Surely he would know. But David said, your father knows very well that you love me, and he will not tell you. As the Lord lives, there is but a step between me and death.

So the two friends made a plan. The next day was the new moon, a feast day, when David was expected to sit at the king's table. He would hide in a field instead. If Saul was angry at his absence, then David would know. And Jonathan would come out into the field, as though to practise with his bow, and shoot three arrows. If he called to the boy fetching the arrows that they were on this side, David was safe. If he called that the arrows were beyond, David must go.

Before they parted, they made a promise to each other, and to each other's children after them, before the Lord.

At the feast, on the second day, Saul noticed the empty seat and asked about it. When Jonathan answered for his friend, his father's anger burst out against him. He called him names, and told him that as long as the son of Jesse lived on the earth, Jonathan would never be king. And then he threw his spear at his own son. Jonathan rose from the table in fierce anger and did not eat, because he was grieved for David, and because his father had shamed him.

In the morning, he went out to the field with a small boy.

{{1SA 20:35-42}}

Let us stay in that field for a moment. The morning light low over the grass. A stone marker, a heap of rocks where a man had been hiding. A small boy trotting back to town with a bow and a quiver, knowing nothing. And two grown men, a prince and a fugitive, holding on to each other and weeping, and then letting go.

David went one way, into the wilderness. Jonathan went the other way, back to the city, back to his father.

Breathe out slowly. Let the field go quiet behind them.
