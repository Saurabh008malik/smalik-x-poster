## Chapter 10: A Rooftop in the Evening, and a Prophet's Story

Welcome back. Let yourself be still. This part of David's story is one that the Bible does not hide, and so we will not hide it either. But we will tell it softly, and we will not stay in the dark places longer than we need to.

By now David's kingdom was strong. His armies had won wars on every side. And the text gives us a small, telling detail. In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle, David sent Joab and the army out to fight, and David himself stayed at home in Jerusalem.

At evening, David arose from his bed and walked on the roof of the king's house. From the roof, he saw a woman bathing, and the woman was very beautiful to look at. David sent and inquired after the woman. One said, Isn't this Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, Uriah the Hittite's wife?

The story that follows is a sad one, and it is told plainly. The king sent messengers and took her. Bathsheba was the wife of Uriah, one of David's own soldiers, one of his mighty men, who was away with the army at that very moment. And when Bathsheba sent word that she was with child, David tried to cover what he had done. He called Uriah home from the battle, and told him to go down to his house and rest. But Uriah would not. He slept at the door of the king's house with the servants. The ark and Israel and Judah are living in tents, he said, and my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camping in the open field. Shall I then go into my house, to eat and drink and lie with my wife? As you live, I will not do this thing.

He was a loyal man, more loyal than his king.

And so David did the worst thing in his whole life. He sent Uriah back to the army with a letter for Joab, telling him to put Uriah in the front of the fiercest fighting, and then to draw back from him, so that he would be killed. And Joab did it, and Uriah died. When the time of mourning was over, David brought Bathsheba to his house, and she became his wife, and bore him a son.

And the old text says, with a quiet and terrible plainness, that the thing David had done displeased the Lord.

Then the Lord sent Nathan to David.

The prophet did not come with an accusation. He came with a story. It is one of the most famous stories in the Bible, and it is gentle on the surface, like a story you might tell a child at bedtime.

The Lord sent Nathan to David. He came to him, and said to him, There were two men in one city: the one rich, and the other poor. The rich man had very many flocks and herds, but the poor man had nothing, except one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and raised. It grew up together with him and with his children. It ate of his own food, drank of his own cup, and lay in his bosom, and was like a daughter to him. A traveler came to the rich man, and he didn't want to take of his own flock and of his own herd to prepare for the wayfaring man who had come to him, but took the poor man's lamb and prepared it for the man who had come to him.

Imagine the king, listening. Imagine him seeing it all in his mind. The poor man's little lamb, drinking from his cup, sleeping in his arms. A shepherd king would know exactly what that meant. And his anger burned. As the Lord lives, he said, the man who has done this deserves to die. And he shall restore the lamb four times over, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity.

And Nathan looked at him and said four words.

Nathan said to David, You are the man! This is what the Lord, the God of Israel, says: 'I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you out of the hand of Saul.

You are the man.

There is a long silence in that moment, even on the page. Nathan went on, and spoke the word of the Lord. I gave you everything, and if that had been too little, I would have given you more. Why have you despised the word of the Lord? And he told David that the sword would never depart from his house.

And this is where David is different from so many kings in the old stories. He did not order the prophet killed. He did not make excuses. He said only this.

David said to Nathan, I have sinned against the Lord. Nathan said to David, The Lord also has put away your sin. You will not die.

And Nathan answered that the Lord had put away his sin, and he would not die. But the child that had been born would not live.

The child became very ill. And David pleaded with God for the boy. He fasted, and went in, and lay all night on the ground. The elders of his house stood beside him and tried to raise him up, but he would not get up, and he would not eat with them. On the seventh day the child died. And the servants were afraid to tell him, because they thought, if he was like this while the child was alive, what will he do now?

But David saw them whispering, and understood. Is the child dead, he asked? And they said, he is dead.

Then David got up from the ground. He washed, and put on fresh clothes, and went into the house of the Lord and worshipped. Then he went home and asked for food, and he ate. His servants did not understand. And David said, while the child was still alive, I fasted and wept, for I thought, who knows whether the Lord will be gracious to me, that the child may live? But now he is dead. Why should I fast? Can I bring him back again?

But now he is dead. Why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I will go to him, but he will not return to me.

It is a very human moment. A grieving father, at the end of hoping, choosing to stand up and keep living.

David comforted Bathsheba. And in time she bore another son, and they named him Solomon. And the old writers say the Lord loved him, and sent word through Nathan the prophet, and gave him a second name, Jedidiah, which means beloved of the Lord.

Many readers have heard David's own heart, after all this, in Psalm fifty-one. Its heading says that it is a Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet came to him. It is the prayer of a man who knows exactly what he has done, and asks to be made new. Let me read you a few of its lines, slowly.

Have mercy on me, God, according to your loving kindness. According to the multitude of your tender mercies, blot out my transgressions. Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity. Cleanse me from my sin.

Create in me a clean heart, O God. Renew a right spirit within me. Don't throw me from your presence, and don't take your Holy Spirit from me. Restore to me the joy of your salvation. Uphold me with a willing spirit.

A clean heart. A right spirit. The joy of salvation, given back. Those are not the words of a man who has earned anything. They are the words of someone asking, very simply, to begin again.

Let us leave the story here for tonight. The king's house is quiet. Somewhere inside, a mother is rocking a newborn boy. The lamps are low. And whatever weighs on your own heart tonight, you can set it down for now. Morning will come, and with it, the chance to begin again.
