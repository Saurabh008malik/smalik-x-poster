## Chapter 11: Absalom, and the Road Up the Mount of Olives

Welcome back. Let your breathing go long and slow. This is the saddest chapter in our story, and the most tender. We will walk it together, gently.

Nathan had said that the sword would not depart from David's house. And in the years that followed, there was trouble among David's own children. One of his sons, Amnon, did a grievous wrong to his half-sister Tamar. David was very angry, the text says, but he did nothing. And Tamar's full brother, Absalom, waited two whole years, and then had Amnon killed at a sheep-shearing feast. Then Absalom fled, far to the north, to his mother's family in a little kingdom called Geshur, and stayed there three years. And the old text says that David longed to go out to Absalom.

In the end, Joab brought Absalom home. But for two more years, the king would not see his son's face. And when at last they met, Absalom bowed to the ground, and the king kissed him. But something was still broken.

Absalom was the most admired young man in all Israel. The old writers say that from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head, there was no blemish in him. His hair was famously thick and long. Once a year, when it grew too heavy, he would have it cut, and they would weigh it.

And Absalom began, quietly, to steal the hearts of the people.

He would rise early and stand beside the road that led to the city gate. Whenever someone came with a dispute for the king to judge, Absalom would call out to him, and ask where he was from, and listen to his case. Your cause is good and right, he would say, but there is no one appointed by the king to hear you. Oh, that I were made judge in the land! Then anyone with a cause could come to me, and I would give him justice. And when people bowed to him, he would take their hands and kiss them.

Absalom did this sort of thing to all Israel who came to the king for judgment. So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.

Then Absalom asked his father for leave to go to Hebron, to keep a vow. And the king said, go in peace. And in Hebron, the city where David himself had first been made king, Absalom had trumpets blown, and messengers ran through all the tribes, saying, Absalom is king.

When the news reached Jerusalem, David did not prepare for a siege. He did not want the city destroyed, or its people killed in a battle between father and son. Arise, he said to his servants, and let us flee.

And so, in his old age, David left Jerusalem on foot.

The procession went out of the city and down into the Kidron valley. The king's household. His bodyguard. Six hundred men from Gath, Philistines who had followed him, led by a man named Ittai. David turned to Ittai and said, why are you coming with us? You are a foreigner, and you only came yesterday. Go back, and stay with the new king. And Ittai answered with words as loyal as any in the Bible.

Ittai answered the king and said, As the Lord lives, and as my lord the king lives, surely in what place my lord the king is, whether for death or for life, your servant will be there also.

The priests Zadok and Abiathar came out carrying the ark of the covenant. But David told them to take it back into the city. If I find favour in the eyes of the Lord, he said, he will bring me back, and show me both the ark and his dwelling. But if he says, I have no delight in you, then here I am. Let him do to me as seems good to him.

And then the king and all his people climbed up the long slope on the other side of the valley.

David went up by the ascent of the Mount of Olives, and wept as he went up; and he had his head covered and went barefoot. All the people who were with him each covered his head, and they went up, weeping as they went up.

Let us walk beside him for a moment. An old man, barefoot on the stones, his head covered, weeping. The people behind him weeping too. The olive trees on either side of the path, grey-green and silver in the light. And behind them, across the valley, the city he had made his home, where his own son was about to ride in through the gates.

Some of the Psalms are linked with this flight. Psalm three is headed "A Psalm by David, when he fled from Absalom his son". It has one line in it that fits this night especially well.

I laid myself down and slept. I awakened, for the Lord sustains me.

I lay down and slept. Even on the worst night of his life, with everything lost, the old king found that he could lie down, and sleep, and wake again, and that he was still held.

The rebellion did not last. Absalom took the advice of those who told him to wait and gather all Israel, and David's friends in the city sent him word. David crossed the Jordan and gathered his loyal men at a town called Mahanaim. And there, generous people of the land brought beds and bowls and wheat and barley and beans and honey and cheese for the king and his weary company, because they said, the people are hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness.

When the battle came, it was fought in a great forest called the forest of Ephraim. And before the armies went out, the old king stood by the gate, and as the companies marched past him by hundreds and by thousands, he gave one command to his three captains, Joab and Abishai and Ittai, in the hearing of all the people. Deal gently, for my sake, with the young man, with Absalom.

Deal gently. That was all he asked.

The battle went against Absalom's men, and the forest, the text says, devoured more people that day than the sword. And Absalom, riding his mule under the thick branches of a great oak, was caught by the head in the tree, and the mule went on from under him, and he was left hanging between heaven and earth. A soldier saw it and told Joab. And Joab, against the king's command, took three darts in his hand and went and thrust them into Absalom's heart. Then Joab blew the trumpet, and the fighting stopped.

At Mahanaim, David was sitting between the two gates, waiting. A watchman on the roof saw a runner coming, and then another. The first reached the king, and said, all is well, but he would not say what had happened to Absalom. Then the second runner came, a man from Cush, and the king asked him, is it well with the young man Absalom? And the messenger told him.

The king was much moved, and went up to the room over the gate and wept. As he went, he said, My son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom! I wish I had died instead of you, Absalom, my son, my son!

Let us be very quiet now, and let that cry fade away. It is one of the most famous cries of grief in all of literature. A king who had won every battle, and lost his son.

The king came home. But part of him, perhaps, stayed behind under an oak in a forest across the Jordan.

Rest now. Grief is heavy, and you do not have to carry it tonight. Let it be set down beside the road, under the olive trees, for someone stronger to carry.
