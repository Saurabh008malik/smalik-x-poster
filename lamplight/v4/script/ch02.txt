## Chapter 2: A Harp in the King's House

Welcome back. Let your breathing slow a little more. We are leaving the quiet hills of Bethlehem now, and going north, to the house of a king.

Saul's home was at a place called Gibeah, in the land of his own tribe, Benjamin, only a short way north of where Jerusalem stands today. It was not a palace of marble. Archaeologists have argued for a long time about exactly what stood there, but we can picture a stone fortress on a hilltop, thick walls, a courtyard, a few rooms where the king sat with his family and his commanders. Around it, the farms and villages of the hill country.

And in that house, the king was not well.

Now the Lord's Spirit departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the Lord troubled him.

The old text tells it in the language of its own time. A darkness had come over Saul. Some days he was the king everyone remembered, tall and strong and generous. Other days a heaviness fell on him, a fear and a restlessness that his servants could see in his face. We do not need to name it in modern words. It is enough to know that the man who had been chosen to lead a nation was now troubled in his own mind, and that the people around him were worried.

His servants had an idea. They said, let our lord command us to look for a man who knows how to play the harp. When the darkness comes on you, he will play, and you will be well.

Saul agreed. Find me someone who plays well, he said, and bring him to me.

And one of the young men in the court said this.

Then one of the young men answered and said, Behold, I have seen a son of Jesse the Bethlehemite who is skillful in playing, a mighty man of valor, a man of war, prudent in speech, and a handsome person; and the Lord is with him.

So messengers went south to Bethlehem, to Jesse's house, and said, the king wants your son David, the one who is with the sheep.

Jesse did what a father does when his son is going a long way to a strange place. He loaded a donkey with bread, and a skin of wine, and a young goat, and sent them with David as a gift for the king. And the shepherd boy walked north, with the hills of home falling away behind him, to stand in front of the man whose crown he had been anointed to receive.

It is one of the quiet ironies of the whole story. Saul did not know. David's brothers knew, and Samuel knew, but the king himself had no idea that the young man with the harp was the one the Lord had chosen to follow him. And in that first meeting, there was no fear and no rivalry. The old words say simply that Saul loved him greatly.

David came to Saul and stood before him. He loved him greatly; and he became his armor bearer. Saul sent to Jesse, saying, Please let David stand before me, for he has found favor in my sight. When the spirit from God was on Saul, David took the harp and played with his hand; so Saul was refreshed and was well, and the evil spirit departed from him.

The instrument David played is called a kinnor in Hebrew. It was not the tall harp of later paintings, the kind that stands on the floor. It was more like a small lyre, a wooden frame with two arms and a crossbar, and strings, perhaps eight or ten of them, made of gut. You held it against your chest or under your arm, and you plucked it with your fingers or a small pick. Pictures of instruments like this have been found carved and painted from many parts of the ancient world. It made a soft, bright sound, the kind of sound that fills a small stone room without filling it too much.

Let us picture an evening in Gibeah. The lamps are lit. The king sits on his couch with his head in his hands. The young man from Bethlehem sits a little way off on a low stool, the lyre against his shoulder, and he begins to play. Not loudly. Just a simple melody, the kind of tune a shepherd might play to settle a restless flock at dusk. The strings hum. The notes fall one after another into the quiet room. And slowly, the king's shoulders begin to loosen. His breathing slows. The heaviness lifts, at least for tonight.

For a while, David seems to have gone back and forth between the king's house and his father's sheep. He would come when he was needed, and return to Bethlehem when he was not. Meanwhile the kingdom had another trouble, one that would not go away. To the west, on the coastal plain near the sea, lived a people called the Philistines.

The Philistines had come to that coast from somewhere across the sea, perhaps from the islands and shores of the Aegean, some generations before. They lived in five strong cities, Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron and Gath, and they were skilled with iron. The book of Samuel says that in Saul's early days there was no blacksmith in all the land of Israel, and farmers had to go down to the Philistines even to sharpen their ploughs. The Philistines held the plain, and they pushed up again and again into the valleys that led toward the hill country.

Those valleys ran from west to east like the fingers of a hand, wide and green near the coast and narrowing as they climbed into the hills. Whoever held the valleys could reach the heart of the land. And one of those valleys, a few hours' walk west of Bethlehem, had a name that meant the valley of the terebinth tree. In Hebrew, the valley of Elah.

The story is about to go there.

But not yet. For now, let us stay a little longer in the king's house at Gibeah, in the lamplight, with a tired king resting at last and a young man's fingers moving softly over the strings. Outside, the night wind moves over the hilltop. Inside, the music goes on, slow and patient, the same few notes, again and again, until the king is asleep.
