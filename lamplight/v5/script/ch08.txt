## Chapter 8: The Hand by the Lamp

Many years have passed. Nebuchadnezzar is gone, and the kings who came after him have come and gone quickly too. Daniel is no longer a young man. If he came to Babylon as a youth in the days of Jehoiakim, he must now be in his eighties, an old man with a long memory, perhaps living quietly, perhaps half forgotten by the court.

The book now brings us to a king named Belshazzar. For a long time, readers outside the Bible knew nothing about him, and some doubted he had ever existed, because the ancient lists of the kings of Babylon did not name him. Then, in the eighteen hundreds, clay tablets and cylinders were found that mention him. The last king of Babylon was a man named Nabonidus, and Belshazzar was his son. For about ten years, Nabonidus lived far away in the Arabian desert, at the oasis of Teima, and he left Belshazzar in charge of Babylon, ruling in his place. So Belshazzar was not the official king, but he was the man who sat on the throne in the city.

The book calls Nebuchadnezzar his father. In the languages of that time, the word father could also mean ancestor, or predecessor, the king who came before. Scholars discuss what exactly the book means by it. We will simply let the story unfold.

Belshazzar the king made a great feast to a thousand of his lords, and drank wine before the thousand. Belshazzar, while he tasted the wine, commanded that the golden and silver vessels which Nebuchadnezzar his father had taken out of the temple which was in Jerusalem be brought to him, that the king and his lords, his wives and his concubines, might drink from them. Then they brought the golden vessels that were taken out of the temple of God's house which was at Jerusalem; and the king and his lords, his wives and his concubines, drank from them. They drank wine, and praised the gods of gold, and of silver, of bronze, of iron, of wood, and of stone.

A great feast, for a thousand lords. We can imagine the banquet hall, long and high, lit by many lamps. The tables crowded with dishes. Musicians playing. The wine flowing. And then, at the height of the feast, the king does a careless thing. He sends for the gold and silver vessels that had been taken from the temple in Jerusalem all those years before, the cups and bowls that had once been used in the worship of the Lord, and he and his guests drink wine from them, and praise their own gods of gold and silver, bronze and iron, wood and stone.

It was the kind of thing a man does to show that nothing is sacred to him. And just at that moment, something happened that no one in the hall would ever forget.

In the same hour, the fingers of a man's hand came out and wrote near the lamp stand on the plaster of the wall of the king's palace. The king saw the part of the hand that wrote.

Near the lampstand, on the plaster of the wall. That is such a precise little detail. The hall was full of light, and in the brightest place, where the great lamp stood and the wall was white with plaster, the fingers of a hand appeared, and wrote.

The laughter stopped. The music stopped. The king saw the hand, and the book tells us that his face went pale, his thoughts troubled him, and his knees knocked against each other.

He called for the enchanters and the astrologers and the wise men of Babylon, and he promised that whoever could read the writing and tell him its meaning would be clothed in purple, with a chain of gold around his neck, and would be the third ruler in the kingdom. Third, because, as we now know, his father Nabonidus was the first, and he himself was the second. But none of the wise men could read the writing.

Then the queen came into the hall. She may have been Belshazzar's mother, or perhaps his grandmother, a queen of the older generation, because she seems to remember things from long ago. She spoke calmly, in the middle of the panic.

The queen by reason of the words of the king and his lords came into the banquet house. The queen spoke and said, O king, live forever; don't let your thoughts trouble you, nor let your face be changed. There is a man in your kingdom in whom is the spirit of the holy gods; and in the days of your father, light and understanding and wisdom, like the wisdom of the gods, were found in him. The king, Nebuchadnezzar, your father—yes, the king, your father—made him master of the magicians, enchanters, Chaldeans, and soothsayers, because an excellent spirit, knowledge, understanding, interpreting of dreams, showing of dark sentences, and dissolving of doubts were found in the same Daniel, whom the king named Belteshazzar. Now let Daniel be called, and he will show the interpretation.

There is a man in your kingdom. In the days of your father, light and understanding and wisdom were found in him. Let Daniel be called.

So the old man was sent for. We can picture him coming slowly into the bright hall, an old man among the young lords and their wine cups, a man who had lived in Babylon for more than sixty years and had seen kings rise and fall. The king spoke to him, a little uncertainly, as if he had only heard of him by rumor. And he repeated his offer, the purple, the gold chain, the third place in the kingdom.

And Daniel's answer is one of the most characteristic things he ever says.

Then Daniel answered before the king, Let your gifts be to yourself, and give your rewards to another. Nevertheless, I will read the writing to the king, and make known to him the interpretation.

Let your gifts be to yourself. Daniel was old now, and he did not need anything from this king. He would read the writing, but not for a reward.

Before he read it, he spoke to the king plainly, as he had once spoken to Nebuchadnezzar. He reminded Belshazzar of the great king who had reigned before him, how he had been lifted up and then humbled, until he learned that the Most High rules the kingdom of men. And you, Daniel said, have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this. You have lifted yourself up against the Lord of heaven, and drunk from the vessels of his house, and praised gods that cannot see or hear or know. But the God in whose hand your breath is, you have not honored.

Then he read the writing.

This is the writing that was inscribed: MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN. This is the interpretation of the thing: MENE: God has counted your kingdom, and brought it to an end. TEKEL: you are weighed in the balances, and are found wanting. PERES: your kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians.

Mene, mene, tekel, upharsin. These were Aramaic words for weights or measures, like a merchant's terms in a market. A mina, a shekel, and halves. But Daniel read them as verbs. Counted. Weighed. Divided. Your days are counted, and the kingdom is at an end. You have been weighed in the balance, and found too light. Your kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and the Persians. And in the last word, peres, there is even a play on the name of the Persians themselves.

The king did what he had promised. He had Daniel clothed in purple, with a chain of gold around his neck, and proclaimed him third ruler in the kingdom. And then the book tells the end in one quiet sentence.

In that night Belshazzar the Chaldean King was slain. Darius the Mede received the kingdom, being about sixty-two years old.

That same night. History tells us that in the autumn of five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ, the armies of Cyrus the Persian took Babylon. A Babylonian record called the Nabonidus Chronicle says the city was taken without a battle. The Greek historian Herodotus, writing about a century later, tells a story that the Persians lowered the river and came in along its bed while the people of the city were celebrating a festival. We cannot be sure of every detail. But the great city of Nebuchadnezzar passed into other hands, and its long reign as the head of gold was over.

As for Darius the Mede, whom the book names as the one who received the kingdom, historians have not yet found him by that name in the records outside the Bible. Some think he was a governor appointed by Cyrus, known by another name. Some think the name refers to Cyrus himself. It is one of the old puzzles of the book. We will leave it gently with the scholars, and follow Daniel into the new kingdom.

The banquet hall is dark now. The lamps have burned down. The cups lie where they were dropped. And on the plaster of the wall, near the lampstand, four words are still written, waiting for the morning.
