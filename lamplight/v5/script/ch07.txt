## Chapter 7: The Great Tree and the Humbled King

The fourth chapter of Daniel is unlike any other part of the book. It is written as a letter, from King Nebuchadnezzar himself, to all the peoples of his empire. The king tells his own story, in his own voice, and it is a story of how he lost his mind, and found it again. It begins with a greeting.

Nebuchadnezzar the king, to all the peoples, nations, and languages, who dwell in all the earth: Peace be multiplied to you. It has seemed good to me to show the signs and wonders that the Most High God has worked toward me. How great are his signs! How mighty are his wonders! His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom. His dominion is from generation to generation.

Peace be multiplied to you. It is a gentle opening, from a man who was not always gentle. And in the letter he looks back on something that happened to him, as a man looks back on an illness he has recovered from.

He tells us that he was at rest in his house, and flourishing in his palace. All was well. And then, once again, he had a dream that frightened him. He called the wise men of Babylon, as before, and this time he told them the dream, but they could not explain it. And at last Daniel came in, Belteshazzar, as the king calls him, the one in whom, he says, is the spirit of the holy gods. And the king told Daniel what he had seen.

These were the visions of my head on my bed: I saw, and behold, a tree in the middle of the earth; and its height was great. The tree grew and was strong. Its height reached to the sky and its sight to the end of all the earth. Its leaves were beautiful, and it had much fruit, and in it was food for all. The animals of the field had shade under it, and the birds of the sky lived in its branches, and all flesh was fed from it.

It is a beautiful picture. A great tree in the middle of the earth, so tall it reached the sky, so wide it could be seen from the ends of the world. Leaves full and green. Branches heavy with fruit. Wild animals resting in its shade, birds nesting in its branches, and food in it for everyone.

The people of Babylon knew a great deal about trees, because they had so few of them. Their land was flat and dry, and the great timbers for their palaces and temples had to be brought from far away, from the cedar forests of the mountains of Lebanon. Nebuchadnezzar himself, in one of his inscriptions, boasts of the cedars he brought down from those mountains. A great tree, in that land, was a picture of wealth and shelter and power.

But then, in the dream, a holy watcher came down from heaven, and cried out that the tree must be cut down. Its branches lopped, its leaves shaken off, its fruit scattered. The animals must flee from under it, and the birds from its branches. But the stump was to be left in the ground, bound with a band of iron and bronze, out in the tender grass of the field, wet with the dew of the sky. And then the dream changed, and spoke not of a tree, but of a man.

Let his heart be changed from man's, and let an animal's heart be given to him. Then let seven times pass over him. The sentence is by the decree of the watchers and the demand by the word of the holy ones, to the intent that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will, and sets up over it the lowest of men.

When Daniel heard the dream, the king says, he was silent for a while, and troubled. He did not want to say what it meant. And the king, kindly, told him not to be afraid. And Daniel answered with words that show how much he had come to care for this proud and difficult man. My lord, he said, I wish this dream were for those who hate you, and its meaning for your enemies.

But it was not for his enemies. The tree, Daniel said, was the king himself. He had grown great and strong, and his greatness reached to the sky, and his rule to the ends of the earth. And he would be cut down. He would be driven from among people, and would live with the animals of the field, and eat grass like an ox, until seven times passed over him, until he knew that the Most High rules the kingdom of men.

And yet the stump would remain. The roots would stay in the ground. The kingdom would be waiting for him when he came back. And then Daniel gave the king some advice, the kind of advice a friend gives.

Therefore, O king, let my counsel be acceptable to you, and break off your sins by righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the poor. Perhaps there may be a lengthening of your tranquility.

Show mercy to the poor. Perhaps there may be a lengthening of your tranquility. It is a simple and practical word. Daniel did not tell the king to perform great religious rituals. He told him to be just and kind to those who had little.

The king does not tell us whether he took that advice. He only tells us what happened a year later.

At the end of twelve months he was walking in the royal palace of Babylon. The king spoke and said, Is not this great Babylon, which I have built for the royal dwelling place by the might of my power and for the glory of my majesty?

Let us picture it gently. A warm evening, perhaps. The king walking alone on the flat roof of his palace, the way people in that land walked on their rooftops in the cool hours. Below him, the city spreads out in every direction. The great walls. The blue gate of Ishtar. The temple tower rising up above the houses. The river, wide and slow, with the lights of boats moving on it. All of it built, or rebuilt, by his command. And his heart swells with pride, and he says, out loud, is not this great Babylon, which I have built, by the might of my power, and for the glory of my majesty?

And while the words were still in his mouth, a voice came from heaven.

The book tells what followed in only a few lines, and we can tell it gently too. The king's mind left him. He was driven out from among people, and he lived out in the fields, like an animal, in the open air. He ate grass like the oxen. The dew of the night fell on him. His hair grew long and wild, and his nails long. For a time, the greatest king in the world did not know who he was.

Some modern scholars have noticed that among the Dead Sea Scrolls there is a fragment called the Prayer of Nabonidus, in which a later king of Babylon, Nabonidus, speaks of being struck with an illness for seven years in the desert town of Teima, and being healed when he prayed to the true God. Some think the story in Daniel and that fragment are connected in some way. We cannot be sure. We will simply let the book tell its own story, which ends, as all good stories of illness should, with healing.

At the end of the days I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted up my eyes to heaven, and my understanding returned to me; and I blessed the Most High, and I praised and honored him who lives forever, for his dominion is an everlasting dominion, and his kingdom from generation to generation.

I lifted up my eyes to heaven, and my understanding returned to me. What a simple and beautiful sentence. Out in the field, in the grass and the dew, under the open sky, the king looked up. And as he looked up, his mind came back.

His counselors came looking for him. His kingdom was given back to him, as the dream had promised, like a stump that sends up green shoots again in spring. And the king ends his letter, not with a boast about his buildings, but with praise.

Now I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise and extol and honor the King of heaven; for all his works are truth, and his ways justice; and those who walk in pride he is able to abase.

Those who walk in pride, he is able to abase. The king who once stood on his rooftop and boasted of what he had built now looks up, and speaks of the King of heaven. And with that, he leaves our story. Nebuchadnezzar does not appear in the book of Daniel again. The next time we enter the palace of Babylon, many years will have passed, there will be another king on the throne, and a great feast will be lit by many lamps.
