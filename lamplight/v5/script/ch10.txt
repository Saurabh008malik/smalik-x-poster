## Chapter 10: The Night in the Lions' Den

There are two places in the story tonight, and we can move between them softly, like a lamp being carried from one room to another.

The first is the palace. The book tells us exactly how the king spent that night.

Then the king went to his palace, and passed the night fasting. No musical instruments were brought before him; and his sleep fled from him.

He fasted. He would not eat. He sent away the musicians who usually played for him in the evening. And his sleep fled from him. Darius lay awake in the dark, in the great palace, with all its rooms and corridors and courtyards silent around him, and thought about the old man in the pit.

Many people know that kind of night. The night when someone you care about is in danger, and there is nothing you can do but wait. The hours go very slowly. You turn over, and turn over again. You listen to every small sound. You wait for the first grey light in the window, so that the waiting can be over.

The second place is the den. And about the den, the book tells us almost nothing until the morning. We do not know what Daniel did through those long hours. Perhaps he prayed, as he had prayed in his upper room only that afternoon. Perhaps he sat quietly in the dark, listening to the breathing of the great animals around him. Perhaps, after a lifetime of trusting, he simply lay down and slept. Many artists over the centuries have painted him in that den, an old man sitting calmly among the lions, sometimes looking up toward a small square of light far above, sometimes with his hands folded in prayer, and the lions lying around him like large, tired cats in the straw.

Let us leave him there in that quiet for a while, and let the night pass.

The stars turn slowly over Babylon. The moon moves across the sky. Along the river, the boats are tied up and still. In the streets, the lamps go out one by one. Somewhere a dog barks, and then is quiet. The great city sleeps, all except one king in his palace, lying with his eyes open in the dark.

And then, at last, the sky begins to grow pale in the east.

Then the king arose very early in the morning, and went in haste to the den of lions. When he came near to Daniel in the den, he cried with a troubled voice. The king spoke and said to Daniel, Daniel, servant of the living God, is your God, whom you serve continually, able to deliver you from the lions?

He arose very early in the morning, and went in haste. We can picture him hurrying through the cold grey streets, perhaps without even waiting for his attendants. He comes to the den. The seals are still on the stone. Everything is silent. And he calls down, in a voice the book describes as troubled. Daniel, servant of the living God, is your God, whom you serve continually, able to deliver you from the lions?

There is a moment of silence. And then, from below, out of the dark, comes an old man's voice, calm and courteous, as if nothing at all were the matter.

Then Daniel said to the king, O king, live forever! My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions' mouths, and they have not hurt me, because innocence was found in me before him; and also before you, O king, I have done no harm.

O king, live forever. Even now, after a night among the lions, Daniel greets the king with the old polite words of the court. And then he tells him. My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions' mouths.

Shut the lions' mouths. Such a simple picture. Not a battle, not a great flash of fire, just the great jaws closed, quietly, all through the night, as if the lions too had decided to sleep.

Then the king was exceedingly glad, and commanded that they should take Daniel up out of the den. So Daniel was taken up out of the den, and no kind of harm was found on him, because he had trusted in his God.

No kind of harm was found on him, because he had trusted in his God. They let down ropes, perhaps, and drew him up, blinking, into the early morning light. An old man, dusty from the floor of the den, but whole.

The book goes on to tell, briefly and sternly, how the men who had plotted against Daniel were themselves thrown into the den, and that this time the lions were not gentle. It is a hard ending, of the kind the ancient stories often have, and we will not dwell on it. Then the king wrote a letter to all his kingdom, and it echoes, very closely, the letter that Nebuchadnezzar had written long before.

I make a decree that in all the dominion of my kingdom men tremble and fear before the God of Daniel. For he is the living God, and steadfast forever. His kingdom is that which will not be destroyed. His dominion will be even to the end. He delivers and rescues. He works signs and wonders in heaven and in earth, who has delivered Daniel from the power of the lions.

His kingdom is that which will not be destroyed. Again and again, in these stories, the kings of the earth come to the same conclusion. Nebuchadnezzar after the dream of the statue. Nebuchadnezzar after the furnace. Nebuchadnezzar after his long illness in the fields. And now Darius, after a sleepless night. The kingdoms of the earth rise and fall. Babylon, the head of gold, has already fallen. But there is a kingdom that does not pass away.

And then, at the end of all the stories of the book, comes one more short, calm sentence.

So this Daniel prospered in the reign of Darius and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian.

So this Daniel prospered. The young man who came to Babylon with nothing but his name and his faith, and was given a new name, and a new language, and a place at a foreign king's table, has lived through it all. Through the dream and the furnace and the feast and the den. Through Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar and Darius. And now he is living in the time of Cyrus the Persian, the king whose coming the book had mentioned in its very first chapter.

The stories of the book of Daniel end here. The second half of the book is something different. It is a book of visions, of dreams in the night, of strange beasts and shining figures, of prayers and of angels. Those chapters are harder to understand, and people have argued about them for a very long time. But in among them there are some of the most tender passages in the whole book. And there is one more thing to happen that the old man has been waiting for all his life.

So let the morning light fade again, gently, back into evening. In the next chapter, we will find Daniel once more in his upper room, with his windows open, reading an old scroll by the light of a lamp.
