## Chapter 11: Seventy Years, and a Prayer at Evening

The second half of the book of Daniel is made of visions. Daniel, who spent so much of his life explaining other people's dreams, now has dreams of his own. They are strange and vivid, and the book tells us that they often left him tired and troubled. We will not try to explain them all tonight. People have read them in many different ways for more than two thousand years. But we can listen to a little of the first one, the way we might listen to a piece of old music, without needing to understand every note.

In the first year of Belshazzar king of Babylon, Daniel had a dream and visions of his head while on his bed. Then he wrote the dream and told the sum of the matters. Daniel spoke and said, I saw in my vision by night, and, behold, the four winds of the sky broke out on the great sea. Four great animals came up from the sea, different from one another.

The four winds of the sky broke out on the great sea. Out of the churning water come four great beasts, one after another, each stranger and more frightening than the last. Many readers have seen in them the same four kingdoms as the four metals of Nebuchadnezzar's statue. But then the vision turns, and the noise and the churning water fall away, and Daniel sees something very different.

I watched until thrones were placed, and one who was Ancient of Days sat. His clothing was white as snow, and the hair of his head like pure wool. His throne was fiery flames, and its wheels burning fire. A fiery stream issued and came out from before him. Thousands of thousands ministered to him. Ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him. The judgment was set. The books were opened.

One who was Ancient of Days. Clothing white as snow, hair like pure wool. Thrones placed, books opened. It is a picture of great calm after great storm, of judgment and order after chaos. And then, coming with the clouds of the sky, there is another figure.

I saw in the night visions, and behold, there came with the clouds of the sky one like a son of man, and he came even to the Ancient of Days, and they brought him near before him. Dominion was given him, and glory, and a kingdom, that all the peoples, nations, and languages should serve him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion, which will not pass away, and his kingdom one that will not be destroyed.

One like a son of man. In Jewish tradition, readers have understood this figure in more than one way, sometimes as standing for God's faithful people. Christians, in later centuries, heard in this phrase an echo of the way Jesus spoke of himself in the Gospels. Whatever else the vision means, it ends where the dream of the statue ended, with a kingdom that will not pass away and will not be destroyed.

Now let us come back to Daniel's own room, to a quieter scene. It is the first year of Darius, the new king. The old man is sitting with a scroll, reading by lamplight. And the scroll is the book of the prophet Jeremiah.

In the first year of Darius the son of Ahasuerus, of the offspring of the Medes, who was made king over the realm of the Chaldeans— in the first year of his reign I, Daniel, understood by the books the number of the years about which the Lord's word came to Jeremiah the prophet for the accomplishing of the desolations of Jerusalem, even seventy years.

Jeremiah was the prophet who had lived in Jerusalem in the last years before it fell, the one who warned and wept, and who was not believed. And among his words, there was a letter he had sent to the first exiles in Babylon, long ago, in the days when Daniel himself was young. It is a surprising letter. You might expect a prophet to tell his people to resist, or to keep themselves apart, or simply to wait. But Jeremiah told them something else.

The Lord of Armies, the God of Israel, says to all the captives whom I have caused to be carried away captive from Jerusalem to Babylon: Build houses and dwell in them. Plant gardens and eat their fruit. Take wives and father sons and daughters. Take wives for your sons, and give your daughters to husbands, that they may bear sons and daughters. Multiply there, and don't be diminished. Seek the peace of the city where I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray to the Lord for it; for in its peace you will have peace.

Build houses, and live in them. Plant gardens, and eat their fruit. Seek the peace of the city where you have been carried, and pray for it, because in its peace you will find your peace.

That is exactly how Daniel had lived, all those years. He had sought the good of Babylon. He had served its kings honestly. He had prayed, and kept faith, and been kind to people who were not his own. And now, reading by lamplight, he came to another part of the same letter.

For the Lord says, After seventy years are accomplished for Babylon, I will visit you and perform my good word toward you, in causing you to return to this place. For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, says the Lord, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you hope and a future.

Seventy years. Thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you hope and a future. Daniel counted the years. The time Jeremiah had spoken of was nearly complete. And so the old man put down the scroll, and turned his face toward the Lord, and prayed.

His prayer is long, and most of it is a confession. Not of his own private wrongs, but of the wrongs of his whole people, the kings and the princes and the ordinary families, generation after generation. He does not make excuses. He does not say that his people deserved better. He asks only for mercy. And his prayer ends with some of the most heartfelt words in the whole Bible.

My God, turn your ear and hear. Open your eyes and see our desolations, and the city which is called by your name; for we do not present our petitions before you for our righteousness, but for your great mercies' sake. Lord, hear. Lord, forgive. Lord, listen and do. Don't defer, for your own sake, my God, because your city and your people are called by your name.

Lord, hear. Lord, forgive. Lord, listen and do. Not because we are good, Daniel says, but because you are merciful.

And then, the book says, while he was still praying, something happened.

While I was speaking, praying, and confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel, and presenting my supplication before the Lord my God for the holy mountain of my God— yes, while I was speaking in prayer—the man Gabriel, whom I had seen in the vision at the beginning, being caused to fly swiftly, touched me about the time of the evening offering. He instructed me and talked with me, and said, Daniel, I have now come to give you wisdom and understanding. At the beginning of your petitions the commandment went out, and I have come to tell you, for you are greatly beloved. Therefore consider the matter and understand the vision.

About the time of the evening offering. There had been no temple in Jerusalem for nearly fifty years. No evening offering had been made there in all that time. And yet, far away in Babylon, the old man still kept the hours of the temple in his heart. When the sun was going down, and the time came when, long ago, the evening sacrifice would have been offered on the holy hill, Daniel was praying. And it was at that hour, the quiet hour of the evening, that the answer came.

For you are greatly beloved. Of all the things said to Daniel in his long life, by kings and queens and officials, this may be the most beautiful. He was greatly beloved.

The words of the vision that followed are hard and mysterious, and we will leave them for another night. But the hope that Daniel had been praying for did come. The book of Ezra, which tells what happened after the exile, opens like this.

Now in the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, that the Lord's word by Jeremiah's mouth might be accomplished, the Lord stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia, so that he made a proclamation throughout all his kingdom, and put it also in writing, saying, Cyrus king of Persia says, The Lord, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth; and he has commanded me to build him a house in Jerusalem, which is in Judah. Whoever there is among you of all his people, may his God be with him, and let him go up to Jerusalem, which is in Judah, and build the house of the Lord, the God of Israel (he is God), which is in Jerusalem.

In the first year of Cyrus. There is a famous clay cylinder from the reign of Cyrus, now kept in the British Museum, which speaks of him allowing peoples who had been carried away to return to their homes and restore their shrines. It does not mention Judah by name, but it shows the kind of policy that the book of Ezra describes. And so, after all the long years, the first families began to make the journey west, back across the desert, back to the stony hills, carrying with them the very vessels of gold and silver that had once been taken from the temple, and that a careless king had once drunk from at his feast.

We do not know whether Daniel went with them. He was very old, and the book does not say. But in the last chapters of the book, we find him once more, alone, beside a great river.
