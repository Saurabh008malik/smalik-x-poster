## Chapter 2: Four Young Men and the King's Table

The road from Jerusalem to Babylon was very long. Travelers did not usually cross the open desert in a straight line. They went north first, up through the hills and valleys, then along the great bend of the Euphrates, and then down the river toward the south. It was a journey of many weeks, perhaps three months or more on foot. The book of Daniel does not tell us anything about that journey, so we can only imagine it, the dust and the heat of the day, the cold of the nights, the slow change of the land under their feet, from stony hills to long plains and then to the green and the palms along the river.

When they arrived, the young men of Judah were given into the care of an official of the king. The book calls him Ashpenaz, the master of the king's eunuchs, the man in charge of the young people who were being trained for service in the palace. And Nebuchadnezzar had given him a clear instruction about which young people to choose.

The king spoke to Ashpenaz, the master of his eunuchs, that he should bring in some of the children of Israel, even of the royal offspring and of the nobles: youths in whom was no defect, but well-favored, skillful in all wisdom, endowed with knowledge, understanding science, and who had the ability to stand in the king's palace; and that he should teach them the learning and the language of the Chaldeans. The king appointed for them a daily portion of the king's delicacies and of the wine which he drank, and that they should be nourished three years, that at its end they should stand before the king.

So this was a kind of royal school. The king wanted capable young men from the conquered nations, well-born and well-taught, healthy and clever, who could be trained in the language and the learning of Babylon and then serve in his court. It was a wise policy, from a king's point of view. A young man raised at the king's table might become loyal to the king, and might help to govern his own people for Babylon.

The learning of the Chaldeans was rich and very old. The scribes of Babylon wrote with a reed stylus on tablets of soft clay, pressing little wedge-shaped marks into it, a writing we call cuneiform. They kept long records of the movements of the moon and the planets, night after night, for centuries. They had lists of omens, and hymns, and stories, and mathematics. A young man from Jerusalem would have had a great deal to learn.

Among the young men chosen were four from the tribe of Judah. The book gives us their names.

Now among these of the children of Judah were Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. The prince of the eunuchs gave names to them: to Daniel he gave the name Belteshazzar; to Hananiah, Shadrach; to Mishael, Meshach; and to Azariah, Abednego.

It is worth pausing for a moment on those names, because names meant a great deal. Daniel means something like God is my judge. Hananiah means the Lord has been gracious. Mishael may mean who is what God is. And Azariah means the Lord has helped. Every one of their Hebrew names carried the name of their God inside it.

And the new names they were given in Babylon were different. Daniel became Belteshazzar, and the others became Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Scholars are not completely sure of the meanings of all of them, but they seem to point toward the gods of Babylon. It was as if the empire were saying, gently but firmly, you belong to us now. You will speak our language, eat our food, learn our books, and answer to our names.

The young men accepted a great deal of this. They learned the language. They studied the books. They answered to their new names, and the book itself often uses those names. But there was one place where Daniel quietly drew a line.

But Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the king's delicacies, nor with the wine which he drank. Therefore he requested of the prince of the eunuchs that he might not defile himself.

The book does not explain exactly why the king's food would have defiled him. Perhaps some of it was food that the law of Moses forbade. Perhaps the meat and wine had been offered to the gods of Babylon first. Perhaps eating from the king's table was a sign of belonging to the king completely. Whatever the reason, Daniel decided in his heart that this was a thing he would not do.

What is lovely about this part of the story is how he went about it. He did not make a scene. He did not shout or refuse in front of everyone. He went to the official and asked, politely, whether there might be another way. And the book tells us something kind about that official.

Now God made Daniel find kindness and compassion in the sight of the prince of the eunuchs. The prince of the eunuchs said to Daniel, I fear my lord the king, who has appointed your food and your drink. For why should he see your faces worse looking than the youths who are of your own age? Then you would endanger my head with the king.

The official liked Daniel. But he was afraid, and his fear was reasonable. If the young men in his care grew thin and pale, the king would notice, and it would be his own head that paid for it.

So Daniel tried again, with the man who looked after them day to day, the steward who brought them their meals. And this time he offered a small, careful experiment.

Then Daniel said to the steward whom the prince of the eunuchs had appointed over Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah: Test your servants, I beg you, ten days; and let them give us vegetables to eat and water to drink. Then let our faces be examined before you, and the face of the youths who eat of the king's delicacies; and as you see, deal with your servants. So he listened to them in this matter, and tested them for ten days.

Ten days. Only vegetables to eat, and water to drink. Then look at us, and look at the others, and decide.

We can imagine those ten days. The other young men at their tables, with roasted meat and fine bread and cups of the king's wine, and the four friends from Jerusalem at their own simple meal, bowls of beans and lentils and greens, and cups of water. Perhaps the others laughed at them a little. Perhaps they did not notice at all. The book does not say. It only tells us how the experiment ended.

At the end of ten days, their faces appeared fairer and they were fatter in flesh than all the youths who ate of the king's delicacies. So the steward took away their delicacies and the wine that they were given to drink, and gave them vegetables.

So the steward kept on bringing them their vegetables, and they kept on eating their simple meals, and the matter was settled quietly, without a quarrel and without anyone being punished.

And then the book gives us a summary of the years that followed, the three years of their training in the royal school.

Now as for these four youths, God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom; and Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams.

Three years is a long time, when you are young and far from home. Long enough to learn to think in another language. Long enough for the faces of home to grow a little blurred in your memory. Long enough for the great city to stop seeming strange, and to start feeling, in some ways, like the place where you live. And yet the four friends kept something of their own. They kept their prayers, and their simple table, and each other.

At the end of the three years, the day came when they were brought before the king himself.

At the end of the days which the king had appointed for bringing them in, the prince of the eunuchs brought them in before Nebuchadnezzar. The king talked with them; and among them all was found no one like Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. Therefore they stood before the king. In every matter of wisdom and understanding concerning which the king inquired of them, he found them ten times better than all the magicians and enchanters who were in all his realm.

Ten times better. It is the kind of phrase people use when they are proud of someone. The king tested them with questions, and he found the four young men from Judah wiser than all the learned men of his kingdom.

And then the chapter closes with a single sentence that reaches a very long way ahead.

Daniel continued even to the first year of King Cyrus.

The first year of King Cyrus. That was the Persian king who would one day take Babylon, many years later. That one short line tells us that Daniel would live in that foreign land through the whole long reign of Nebuchadnezzar, and through the kings after him, until the empire of Babylon itself had passed away. The young man who arrived dusty from the road would become an old man there.

For now, though, he is young. He has finished his schooling. He has a place in the court of the greatest king in the world, and three good friends beside him, and a quiet faith that has already been tested in a small way, at the table, and has held.

The nights in Babylon are warm. On the flat roofs of the city, people sleep outside in summer, under a sky full of stars. Somewhere in the palace, the king is sleeping too. But his sleep is not peaceful. Soon he will wake in the dark, troubled by a dream, and he will ask his wise men for something no one has ever asked of them before.
