## Chapter 9: An Open Window Toward Jerusalem

A new kingdom had come to Babylon, but the business of ruling went on much as before. Someone had to collect the taxes, and settle the quarrels, and keep the roads and canals in order. And so the new king, Darius, organized his realm.

It pleased Darius to set over the kingdom one hundred twenty local governors, who should be throughout the whole kingdom; and over them three presidents, of whom Daniel was one, that these local governors might give account to them, and that the king should suffer no loss. Then this Daniel was distinguished above the presidents and the local governors, because an excellent spirit was in him; and the king thought to set him over the whole realm.

An excellent spirit was in him. Daniel was very old by now, but he was still the man he had always been, careful, honest, wise. He had served the Babylonians, and now he served the Persians, and in both courts he was trusted. The king even thought of setting him over the whole kingdom.

That was too much for some of the other officials. They began to look for something they could use against him. But they could find nothing. No bribes. No carelessness. No hidden dealings. And so they came to a conclusion that is, in its way, the highest compliment anyone in the book ever pays to Daniel.

Then these men said, We won't find any occasion against this Daniel, unless we find it against him concerning the law of his God.

The only fault they could find in him was his faithfulness to his God. And so they made a plan. They went to the king together, all of them at once, and flattered him, and asked him to sign a decree.

All the presidents of the kingdom, the deputies and the local governors, the counselors and the governors, have consulted together to establish a royal statute and to make a strong decree, that whoever asks a petition of any god or man for thirty days, except of you, O king, he shall be cast into the den of lions. Now, O king, establish the decree and sign the writing, that it not be changed, according to the law of the Medes and Persians, which doesn't alter. Therefore King Darius signed the writing and the decree.

For thirty days, no one could ask anything of any god or any man, except of the king himself. And the decree was written according to the law of the Medes and Persians, which could not be changed.

The book often notices how kings can be trapped by their own words, and their own pride. Darius seems not to have thought very hard. Perhaps he was pleased to be honored. Perhaps he did not see who the decree was really aimed at. He signed it.

And now comes one of the quietest and most beautiful verses in the whole story.

When Daniel knew that the writing was signed, he went into his house, now his windows were open in his room toward Jerusalem, and he kneeled on his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did before.

Let us picture it slowly. An old man walks home through the streets of Babylon. He knows that the decree has been signed. He knows what it means. He goes into his house, and climbs the stairs to his upper room, the way he has done for years. The windows of that room are open toward Jerusalem, toward the west, toward the hills he left so long ago, the city that lies in ruins, the temple he has not seen since he was a boy. He kneels down, as he always does, three times a day. And he prays, and gives thanks to his God, as he did before.

As he did before. He did not make a show of it. He did not close the windows to hide. He did not open them wider to make a point. He simply did what he had always done, at the same times, in the same place, with the same open windows, facing home.

There is something very peaceful about that picture, even with danger waiting outside. A lifetime of small, faithful habits, kept day after day for decades, and now, at the hardest moment, those habits holding him steady, like a well-worn path that the feet can follow even in the dark.

His enemies came, all together, and found him praying. And they went straight to the king. They reminded him of the decree he had signed. And then they told him that Daniel, one of the captives from Judah, did not respect the king or his decree, but prayed to his God three times a day.

And the king understood, too late, what he had done.

Then the king, when he heard these words, was very displeased, and set his heart on Daniel to deliver him; and he labored until the going down of the sun to rescue him.

He labored until the going down of the sun. We can imagine Darius, all that long afternoon, sending for his lawyers, searching for some way out, some exception in the law. He liked Daniel. He did not want to lose him. But the law of the Medes and Persians could not be changed, and the officials came back at evening to remind him of it.

So, as the sun went down over Babylon, the king gave the order. And then he said something that shows he had come, in his own way, to share a little of Daniel's hope.

Then the king commanded, and they brought Daniel and cast him into the den of lions. The king spoke and said to Daniel, Your God whom you serve continually, he will deliver you.

Your God, whom you serve continually, he will deliver you. It was half a prayer and half a hope, from a king who had been caught by his own decree and did not know what else to say.

The den of lions was likely a pit, or an enclosure below the ground, with an opening at the top. Kings in that part of the world had long kept lions. The kings of Assyria, before Babylon, had famously hunted them, and their carvings of lion hunts can still be seen in museums today. Daniel was let down into the dark, and a stone was rolled over the mouth of the den, and the king sealed it with his own signet ring, pressing it into clay, so that nothing could be changed.

And then the king went back to his palace, alone, as the night came down.

We will stay with that night in the next chapter. For now, let the stone settle into place. Let the footsteps of the officials fade away into the streets. Let the evening come down over the city, the lamps being lit in the windows, the river moving quietly in the dark. Somewhere below the ground, an old man is sitting very still. And somewhere in the palace, a king is lying awake, unable to sleep.
