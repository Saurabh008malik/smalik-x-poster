## Chapter 6: The Fourth Man in the Fire

The answer of the three friends had been calm and gentle. But it did not make the king calm. The book says that his face changed, the way a face changes when anger takes hold of it completely. He ordered the furnace to be heated seven times hotter than usual. He commanded some of the strongest men in his army to bind the three friends, just as they were, in their clothes and cloaks, and to throw them into the fire.

The book tells this part of the story quickly, almost breathlessly, and we will not linger over it. The king's command was urgent, and the furnace was so fiercely hot that the soldiers who carried the three friends to its mouth were themselves overcome by the flames. And Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, still bound, fell down into the middle of the fire.

And then everything changes. The pace of the story slows. The king, who a moment before was shouting orders, is suddenly on his feet, staring.

Then Nebuchadnezzar the king was astonished and rose up in haste. He spoke and said to his counselors, Didn't we cast three men bound into the middle of the fire? They answered the king, True, O king. He answered, Look, I see four men loose, walking in the middle of the fire, and they are unharmed. The appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods.

Four men, loose, walking in the middle of the fire, and unharmed. The ropes that had bound them were gone. They were not lying where they had fallen. They were walking, the way people walk in a garden in the cool of the evening. And there was a fourth with them.

The king said the fourth looked like a son of the gods. That is the way a man of Babylon would naturally describe a heavenly being. Later in the chapter, the king himself calls this figure an angel. Christian readers over many centuries have seen in the fourth man something more, and have wondered whether he was a sign of God's own presence walking with his people in their suffering. The book does not explain. It simply tells us that in the very worst place, in the middle of the fire, the three friends were not alone.

That is a thought worth carrying into the night. Whatever fire a person passes through, the old story says, there may be someone walking beside them in it, whom they did not expect.

The king came near to the mouth of the furnace, as close as he could bear the heat. And he called to them, and for the first time, he called their God by a new name.

Then Nebuchadnezzar came near to the mouth of the burning fiery furnace. He spoke and said, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, you servants of the Most High God, come out, and come here! Then Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego came out of the middle of the fire.

You servants of the Most High God, come out, and come here.

And they came out. Slowly, we might imagine, stepping out of the glow into the evening light. And all the officials of the empire, the same governors and deputies and counselors who had been listed so carefully at the beginning of the chapter, crowded around them to look. The book tells us what they saw. The fire had no power over their bodies. Not a hair of their heads was singed. Their clothes were not scorched. And there was not even the smell of smoke on them.

That last detail is a lovely one, and very true to life. Anyone who has sat near a campfire knows how the smell of smoke gets into your hair and your clothes, and stays there for days. But these three men had walked in the middle of a great furnace, and they came out smelling only of themselves, as if they had been taking a quiet walk in the open air.

Then Nebuchadnezzar spoke again. And this time, it was not a threat.

Nebuchadnezzar spoke and said, Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who has sent his angel and delivered his servants who trusted in him, and have changed the king's word, and have yielded their bodies, that they might not serve nor worship any god except their own God.

They yielded their bodies, the king said, rather than serve any god except their own. He was moved, not only by the miracle, but by their faithfulness. These were men who would not bow even to him, the greatest king on earth, and he found that, in spite of everything, he admired them for it.

The king then made a new decree, and as with many of Nebuchadnezzar's decrees, it was very severe. Anyone, he said, who spoke against the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, would be punished. He had swung from one extreme to the other, from trying to force them to bow to his image to threatening anyone who insulted their God. He was still a king who thought that the way to deal with anything was by command. He had seen something wonderful, but he had not yet learned what it meant.

And then the chapter ends very simply.

Then the king promoted Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego in the province of Babylon.

He promoted them. They went back to their work, in the province of Babylon. They went on keeping the accounts and settling the disputes and doing all the ordinary business of the empire. The great image presumably stayed standing on the plain, glinting in the sun. And the three friends went on living in the land where they had been so nearly destroyed, and serving the king who had tried to destroy them.

There is something quietly remarkable about that. They did not leave. They did not take revenge. They did not demand that the image be pulled down. They simply went on being faithful, in small ways and in large ones, day after day, in a foreign land.

This is the last time the three friends appear in the book of Daniel. We do not hear any more about them after this chapter. But their story has traveled a very long way. It was told by Jewish families in the long centuries afterward, and it was painted on the walls of the catacombs of Rome by early Christians, who drew three small figures with their hands raised, standing in the flames. In the old Greek version of the book of Daniel, there is even a long song of praise that the three are said to have sung in the fire, calling on the sun and moon, the stars of heaven, the showers and dew, the nights and days, light and darkness, to bless the Lord. That song is not in the Hebrew and Aramaic text that we are following tonight, but it has been sung in churches for many centuries.

Let us leave the plain now. The crowds are going home. The furnace is cooling. The sun has gone down behind the palms, and the first stars are coming out over the flat land, one by one.

Back in the city, the king goes up to his palace. And in the years that follow, he will have another dream, and this time, the dream will be about himself.
