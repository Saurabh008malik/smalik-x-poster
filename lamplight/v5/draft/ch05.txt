## Chapter 5: The Golden Image on the Plain

Outside Babylon, the land is very flat. On a clear day, you can see a long way across it, to the line of palms along a canal, or the walls of a distant town, or the blue smudge of the river. The book of Daniel says that the king set up his image on the plain of Dura, in the province of Babylon. No one today knows exactly where that plain was. There are several places in Iraq with names like Dura, and scholars have suggested more than one of them. But we can picture a wide, open place, where a great crowd could gather and a tall image could be seen from far away.

{{DAN 3:1}}

Sixty cubits high and six cubits wide. A cubit was about the length of a man's forearm, from the elbow to the tip of the fingers, about a foot and a half. So the image was something like ninety feet tall and nine feet wide, a tall, narrow shape, more like a pillar than a statue. It may have stood on a high base. It may have been made of wood or brick and covered with gold, as many great images were in those days. The book does not say. It only tells us that it was golden, and that it shone.

Then the king sent messengers through his whole empire, to call all its officials to the dedication. And here the book does something it will do several times in this chapter. It gives us long lists, repeated over and over, almost like a chant. The governors, the deputies, the judges, the treasurers, the counselors, the sheriffs, and all the rulers of the provinces. And then the instruments of the royal orchestra, the horn, the flute, the zither, the lyre, the harp, the pipe, and all kinds of music.

Many readers think those repeated lists are a little bit humorous, on purpose. The whole great machinery of the empire, every official in his robes, every instrument in its place, all lined up on the plain, all doing exactly the same thing at exactly the same moment, because the king has told them to. Let us hear how the herald cried it out.

{{DAN 3:4-7}}

We can imagine the scene, the way a painter might. The flat plain, the heat of the sun, the dust lifting in the air. Thousands of people in their bright clothes, from all the lands of the empire, speaking many languages. The tall golden image catching the light. And then the music begins, the horns and the pipes and the harps all at once, and the whole great crowd goes down on its knees like a field of barley bending in the wind.

All except three.

Somewhere in that crowd, three men stayed on their feet. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who had come to Babylon as boys, who had learned its language and served its king faithfully for years, stood quietly while everyone else bowed. The book does not tell us where Daniel was that day. He does not appear in this chapter at all. Perhaps he was away on the king's business. The story belongs to his three friends.

It did not take long for someone to notice. Certain Chaldeans came to the king and told him. There are certain Jews, they said, whom you have appointed over the affairs of the province of Babylon, and they do not serve your gods, and they do not worship the golden image.

The king was furious. He sent for the three men, and they were brought before him. And yet, even in his anger, he gave them one more chance. Perhaps he valued them. Perhaps he remembered the morning when Daniel had told him his dream. He said to them, if you are ready, when you hear the music, to fall down and worship the image, good. But if you will not, you will be thrown, that same hour, into a burning, fiery furnace.

{{DAN 3:15}}

Who is that god who will deliver you out of my hands? It was the question of a man who had never yet met a power greater than his own.

And now comes the answer of the three friends, which is one of the bravest and quietest answers in all of scripture.

{{DAN 3:16-18}}

Let those words settle. Our God is able to deliver us. And he will deliver us out of your hand. But if not.

But if not. Those three small words have meant a great deal to many people over the centuries. The three friends were not sure what would happen. They believed their God could save them. They hoped he would. But they did not make their faithfulness depend on being rescued. Even if he does not save us, they said, we will still not bow.

There is no anger in their answer, and no pride. They do not insult the king, or his gods. They simply say, calmly, that there is a line they will not cross. It is the same quiet courage that Daniel showed at the king's table, all those years before, when he asked for vegetables and water. Only now the stakes are much, much higher.

Let us leave them standing there for a moment, before the king, in the bright light of the plain. Three men in their tunics and cloaks, the clothes of officials of Babylon. Behind them, the crowd, still murmuring. Before them, the angry king. And not far away, the furnace, a great kiln of the kind used for baking bricks or smelting metal, its fire roaring, its heat shimmering in the air.

Babylon was a city of brick, and brick kilns were everywhere in that land. The people of the plain would have known exactly what a furnace like that looked like, its open mouth, the glow inside, the way the heat struck your face from many paces away. We do not need to imagine it too closely tonight. It is enough to know that it was there, and that the three friends had chosen to stand anyway.

In the next chapter, we will see what happened when the king's anger reached its height. But for now, let the music on the plain fall silent. Let the crowd grow still. Let the three friends stand quietly, as the sun begins to sink lower over the flat land, and the long shadows stretch out across the plain.
