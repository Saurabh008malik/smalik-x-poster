## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a City by the River

Good evening, and welcome. Find the place where your body feels heavy and supported, and let it rest there. Loosen your shoulders. Unclench your jaw. The day has done what it came to do, and now it is going away, like a cart rolling slowly down a long road. You do not have to follow it.

This is Lamplight Scripture. Tonight we are going to travel a long way from home, to a great city on a great river, and we are going to stay there for most of a lifetime. We will follow a young man named Daniel, who was carried away to Babylon when he was still a youth, and who lived there, far from the hills where he was born, under one king after another. We will meet his three friends, and a king who had strange dreams. We will stand on a wide plain beside a burning furnace, and in a banquet hall where a hand writes on the wall beside a lamp. And we will sit for one long night beside a den of lions, and wait for morning.

Their story is told in the book of Daniel, near the end of the Old Testament, with a few threads from Jeremiah, from the Psalms, and from the book of Ezra. I will tell it in plain words, slowly. And now and then I will read the old words themselves, from a public domain translation called the World English Bible. Where the old text uses the holy name of God, I will say the Lord, as many readers do.

You do not need to keep up. If you fall asleep in the king's palace, or somewhere out on the plain, that is perfectly all right. The story will go on walking, and it will be here whenever you come back.

Let us begin with the opening lines of the book.

That is how the book of Daniel begins, very simply, with a siege and a journey. But behind those few lines is a whole world, and it may help, before we go any further, to see that world from a little height, the way a bird might see it, drifting high over the land in the evening light.

Far to the west, near the edge of the great sea, there is a small hill country. Its hills are stony and green in spring, brown and gold in late summer. There are olive trees on the terraces, and vineyards, and flocks on the slopes. In the middle of those hills stands Jerusalem, a city on a ridge, with a temple at its top. For hundreds of years it had been the royal city of the kings of Judah, the descendants of David. The temple was where the people went up to pray, and to sing, and to bring their offerings at the great feasts.

Now let the bird turn east and fly. Below it the hills fall away into desert, and the desert goes on for a very long way, and then, at last, there is a broad, flat land between two rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates. The old Greeks called it Mesopotamia, the land between the rivers. It is flat as a table, and the rivers wind across it slowly, and men have dug canals from them for thousands of years, so that in the heat of summer the fields are green with barley and the date palms stand in long rows along the water. The old Bible calls the southern part of this land Shinar, and later the land of the Chaldeans.

And there, on the Euphrates, sat Babylon.

In the days of our story, Babylon was perhaps the greatest city in the world. Its king, Nebuchadnezzar, the second of that name, reigned for more than forty years, from about six hundred and five before Christ to about five hundred and sixty-two. He was a soldier, and he was also a builder. His workmen made bricks by the millions, baked bricks, many of them stamped with his name, and they raised walls and palaces and temples along the river. One of his gates, the gate of the goddess Ishtar, was covered in glazed bricks of a deep, shining blue, with rows of bulls and dragons in yellow and white marching across it. Pieces of that gate were dug up by archaeologists in the early nineteen hundreds, and part of it has been rebuilt in a museum in Berlin, so that people can stand in front of it and look up at those blue walls.

Later writers told of the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, terraces of trees and flowers rising one above another, and they counted them among the wonders of the world. We should hold that story lightly. The Greek writers who describe the gardens lived long after Nebuchadnezzar, and no Babylonian record that has been found so far mentions them. Some scholars wonder whether they were really somewhere else, or whether they were a beautiful legend. But the walls were real, and the gate was real, and the river was real, flowing slowly through the middle of the city under the stars.

Between the small hill country and the great city by the river, there was trouble. For years, the kingdom of Judah had been caught between powers much larger than itself, Egypt to the south and Babylon to the east. Its kings tried to bend this way and that, paying tribute here, making alliances there. And the prophets of the time, especially a prophet named Jeremiah, warned again and again that the way the people were living, and the way their kings were ruling, would end in ruin.

The book of Daniel begins in the third year of a king named Jehoiakim. In those years, Babylon's armies came west, and Judah became a servant kingdom. Some of the vessels of the temple were taken away, cups and bowls of gold and silver that had been used in worship, and they were carried hundreds of miles across the desert and placed in the treasure house of Babylon's god. And with them, some of the young men of Jerusalem were taken too.

Over the next twenty years, the story grew darker. More people were carried away, in more than one deportation. The historians usually date one of them to about five hundred and ninety-seven before Christ, when a young king named Jehoiachin, with his household and many craftsmen, was taken to Babylon. And about ten years after that, in five hundred and eighty-seven or five hundred and eighty-six, after a long siege, Jerusalem itself was broken, and the temple was burned. The book of Second Kings tells of it, and the book of Lamentations mourns it.

So the people of Judah became exiles. A great many of them lived the rest of their lives in Babylonia, by rivers that were not their own. One of the Psalms remembers how that felt. It is a sad song, and we will not read all of it, only the beginning, which many people know.

That is the world into which our story walks. A small people, far from home. A great city, shining with blue brick. A river, and willow trees, and harps hung up and silent.

But the book of Daniel is not only a sad book. It is, in many ways, a book about how to live well in a place that is not your home. How to keep your heart when everything around you is pulling it somewhere else. How to be kind and faithful and wise in a foreign court, under kings who could be generous one day and frightening the next. And it is a book with a deep, quiet confidence running all the way through it, that the God of that small hill country was not left behind in Jerusalem, but was there in Babylon too, and that he was still the one who gives kingdoms and takes them away.

Before we go on, there is one more thing worth saying gently. Scholars have discussed the book of Daniel for a very long time. Some read it as written by Daniel himself in the time of the exile. Many modern scholars believe it was put into its final form much later, around the second century before Christ, as a book of encouragement for people living through a later time of persecution. Tonight we will not try to settle that. We will simply listen to the stories as the book tells them, the way people have listened to them for many centuries, by lamplight, at the end of the day.

And so let us go down, out of the sky, onto a long dusty road. A line of travelers is walking east, and among them, if we look closely, there are a few young men from Jerusalem, walking toward a city they have never seen.
