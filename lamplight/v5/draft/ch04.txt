## Chapter 4: The Statue and the Stone

Daniel stood before the king, and the court was quiet. Before he told the dream, he said one more thing, gently, about where the dream had come from.

{{DAN 2:29-30}}

Your thoughts came on your bed. It is a very human beginning. The king had been lying awake, the way people do, wondering about what would come after him. What would happen to his kingdom, and to all the great walls and gates he had built, when he was gone? And in his sleep, an answer had come to him, in a picture he could not understand.

Then Daniel told him the dream.

{{DAN 2:31-35}}

Let that picture rise in front of you for a moment, the way it rose in front of the king. A great figure, taller than any building, shining in the dark. A head of fine gold, catching the light. A chest and arms of silver. A belly and thighs of bronze. Legs of iron, grey and strong. And feet that were partly iron and partly clay, the soft clay of the riverbank that the brickmakers of Babylon used every day.

And then a stone. Not thrown by anyone. Cut out, the dream says, without hands. It strikes the feet, the weakest part, where the iron and clay are mixed. And the whole great figure comes down, all at once, gold and silver and bronze and iron and clay, broken into pieces as fine as the chaff that blows from a threshing floor at the end of summer. The wind carries it away, and there is nothing left. And the small stone grows, and grows, until it becomes a mountain that fills the whole earth.

The threshing floor would have been a familiar picture to everyone in that room. At harvest, the farmers spread the cut barley on a flat, hard floor and beat it or drove animals over it to loosen the grain. Then, on an evening when the wind was right, they tossed it into the air with wooden forks. The heavy grain fell back to the floor, and the light chaff floated away on the breeze, like dust in the low sun. So the great statue, for all its gold and iron, became as light as chaff.

Then Daniel gave the meaning. He began by speaking to the king directly, and with great respect. You, O king, are the head of gold. The God of heaven has given you a kingdom, and power, and strength, and glory. After you, he said, there would come another kingdom, lesser than yours, and then a third kingdom, of bronze, that would rule over all the earth, and then a fourth kingdom, as strong as iron, that would break and crush. And at the end, a divided kingdom, part strong and part brittle, like iron mixed with clay, that does not hold together.

Readers over the centuries have tried to name these kingdoms. Many have seen in them Babylon, and then the Medes and the Persians, and then the Greeks who came after Alexander, and then later empires. Others have counted them differently. The book itself names only the first, the head of gold, Nebuchadnezzar's own Babylon. And then it moves to the part of the dream that matters most.

{{DAN 2:44-45}}

A kingdom which will never be destroyed. That is the heart of the dream. All the great empires of the earth, shining and strong as they are, will pass away like chaff on the wind. But there is another kingdom, set up not by human hands but by the God of heaven, and it will stand forever.

It is a strange thing to say to a king. Daniel was telling Nebuchadnezzar, to his face, that his empire would not last. That it was gold, yes, but that it would come down. And yet he said it so carefully, with so much honor for the king, that the king did not become angry. Instead, something remarkable happened.

{{DAN 2:46-49}}

The great king fell on his face before a young foreigner. And he said, of Daniel's God, that he was the God of gods, and the Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets.

We should not imagine that Nebuchadnezzar had suddenly changed his whole life. As we will see, he had a long way still to go. Kings in the ancient world were often happy to honor many gods at once, and he may simply have added the God of Daniel to the list of powers he respected. But something had moved in him. For one morning at least, the most powerful man in the world had been humbled, not by an army, but by a dream and a young man's honest words.

And notice what Daniel did with his new power. The very first thing he asked was for his three friends to be given places too. He did not forget the ones who had prayed with him through the night. So Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego were set over the affairs of the province of Babylon, and Daniel stayed in the king's gate, which in those days meant at the center of the king's court, where judgments were given and business was done.

Let us leave them there for a while, settling into their new work. The years in Babylon go on. The river rises in the spring with snowmelt from the far northern mountains, and falls again in the late summer. The canals are dug and cleaned. Ships come and go along the quays with cargoes of timber and stone and dates. The four friends grow older, and wiser, and more used to the ways of the court.

The book does not tell us how much time passes before the next story, or what the king was thinking. But many readers, over the centuries, have noticed something. In the dream, only the head of the statue was gold. And in the very next chapter, Nebuchadnezzar makes a statue of his own, and it is golden all over. Perhaps, they wonder, the king did not like the idea that his golden kingdom would one day give way to others. Perhaps he wanted an image that was gold from head to foot, set up where the whole world could see it.

Whatever the reason, out on a wide plain beyond the city, the workmen begin to build.
