## Chapter 3: A Dream in the Night

There is a kind of waking that many of us know. You come up out of sleep suddenly, in the deep part of the night, with your heart beating fast. A dream has left something behind, a feeling, a picture, a weight on the chest. You lie in the dark and try to hold on to it, and you cannot quite get back to sleep.

That is how the second chapter of Daniel begins, in the bedroom of the most powerful man in the world.

{{DAN 2:1-3}}

You might notice that this happens in the second year of Nebuchadnezzar's reign, while the first chapter spoke of three years of training. Readers have puzzled over that for a long time, and there are different ways of counting the years of a king's reign in the ancient world. The book does not stop to explain, and we will not stop either. We will simply walk into the throne room, where the wise men of Babylon have gathered in their robes, in the early light.

There is a small, curious thing about the book at this point. Just as the wise men begin to speak to the king, the book itself changes language. Up to here it has been written in Hebrew. But from this verse until the end of the seventh chapter, it is written in Aramaic, the common language of the great empires of that time, the language that traders and officials used from Egypt to Persia. It is as if the book itself has walked into the court of Babylon and begun to speak the language of the court.

The wise men answered the king the way they always answered. Tell us the dream, O king, and we will tell you what it means. That was their craft. They had books of dreams and their meanings, and they could look up the signs and give an answer.

But Nebuchadnezzar would not tell them the dream. Readers have long wondered exactly why. Some think he had truly forgotten it. Others think he remembered it very well, and was testing them. If they really had wisdom from the gods, he seems to say, then they could tell him the dream itself, and then he would believe their interpretation. And he backed his demand with terrible threats, and with promises of great rewards.

The wise men asked again, and again he refused. And finally they said what was true.

{{DAN 2:10-11}}

The gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh. It is an honest answer, and a humble one, from men who were not used to being humble before a king. What you ask, they were saying, is beyond any human being.

But the king was in no mood for honesty. He was angry, the way frightened people sometimes are, and he gave a cruel order, that all the wise men of Babylon should be put to death. And that order reached even the young men from Judah, who had only lately finished their training and become part of the circle of the wise.

So the captain of the king's guard, a man named Arioch, came looking for Daniel. And here we see something about Daniel's character that we will see again and again. He did not panic. He did not run. He asked a calm question.

{{DAN 2:14-16}}

Counsel and prudence. Careful words, at a moment when careless words could have cost his life. Daniel asked why the order was so urgent, and when he understood, he went in to the king himself and asked for a little time. Give me time, he said, and I will tell the king what he wants to know.

And then he did something very simple. He went home, and he told his three friends, and they prayed together.

{{DAN 2:17-19}}

We can picture it, if we like, though the book gives only the outline. A small house somewhere in the city. Four young men far from home, sitting together in the lamplight, the sounds of the city outside growing quieter as the night went on. Perhaps they spoke the old prayers of their people, in Hebrew, the language of their childhood. Perhaps they simply sat in silence for a long time. And at some point in the night, Daniel slept, or half slept, and the secret was shown to him in a vision.

And the first thing Daniel did, before he went running to the king, before he even told his friends, was to give thanks. His prayer is one of the most beautiful passages in the book, and it is worth listening to slowly.

{{DAN 2:20-23}}

He changes the times and the seasons. He removes kings and sets up kings. He knows what is in the darkness, and the light dwells with him.

That last line is a good one to hold onto at night. Whatever is in the darkness, it is known. The light dwells with him.

In the morning, Daniel went to Arioch, and his first words were a plea for the others.

{{DAN 2:24}}

Do not destroy the wise men of Babylon. It would have been easy for Daniel to think only of himself and his friends. The wise men of Babylon were not his people. They served other gods. Some of them may well have looked down on these young foreigners. But Daniel's first thought was to save them all.

Arioch hurried him in to the king, and, as officials will, took a little credit for himself, saying that he had found a man among the captives of Judah who could tell the king the meaning. And the king looked at the young man in front of him, and asked the question that had kept him awake.

{{DAN 2:26-28}}

There is a God in heaven who reveals secrets. Daniel was careful to say, before anything else, that the wisdom was not his own. He did not claim to be cleverer than the magicians and the astrologers. He said, plainly, that there is a God in heaven, and that this God had chosen to show the king something about the days to come.

And then, standing before the throne, with the court watching and the morning light coming in, he began to tell the king his own dream.

We will hear that dream in the next chapter. For now, let the throne room grow quiet. Let the voices fall away. Only the young man is speaking now, in a calm, low voice, and the great king is leaning forward on his seat, listening, the way a child listens to a story it has heard before but cannot quite remember.
