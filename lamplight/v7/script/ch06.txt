## Chapter 6: Joseph's Dream

Until now we have been listening to Luke. But there is a second gospel that tells of the birth of Jesus, the gospel of Matthew, and Matthew tells the story from a different side of the house. Luke stays close to Mary. Matthew stays close to Joseph.

Matthew begins his gospel with a long family line, a genealogy, tracing Joseph's family back through the kings of Judah to David, and from David all the way back to Abraham. Name after name after name, like the slow ticking of a very old clock. We will not read it all tonight, but let us hear the first line, because it tells us what Matthew wants us to notice.

The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham.

The son of David, the son of Abraham. Joseph comes from that royal line. Not that he lived like a prince. By the time of our story, the family of David had long since lost its throne. Joseph was a working man in a small village. Matthew later calls him a tekton, a word usually translated carpenter, though it can mean any kind of builder or craftsman who worked with wood or stone. Picture a man with strong, scarred hands, used to beams and doorframes and ploughs and yokes.

And now, this man receives news that must have struck him like a blow.

Now the birth of Jesus Christ was like this: After his mother, Mary, was engaged to Joseph, before they came together, she was found pregnant by the Holy Spirit. Joseph, her husband, being a righteous man, and not willing to make her a public example, intended to put her away secretly.

Let us go slowly here, because this is a painful moment, and the gospel does not hide it.

Joseph and Mary are betrothed. They are bound to each other by promise, but they have not yet come together as husband and wife. And Mary is found to be with child. Matthew tells us, the reader, that the child is from the Holy Spirit. But Joseph does not know that yet. All Joseph knows is what anyone in the village would have assumed.

Matthew describes Joseph as a righteous man. And it is interesting what that righteousness looks like. Under the customs of the time, a betrothal like this could only be ended by something like a divorce. Joseph could have done it loudly. He could have made a public accusation, before the elders of the village, and let everyone know that he had been wronged. Many men would have.

But Joseph does not want to shame her. He decides to end the betrothal quietly. Privately. With as little harm to Mary as possible.

Think about that. He is hurt. He is confused. He must have felt that the whole careful shape of his life had cracked. And still, his first thought is not how to protect himself, but how to protect her. That is the kind of man Joseph was.

And so, with his mind made up, and his heart heavy, he goes to sleep.

But when he thought about these things, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream, saying, Joseph, son of David, don't be afraid to take to yourself Mary as your wife, for that which is conceived in her is of the Holy Spirit. She shall give birth to a son. You shall name him Jesus, for it is he who shall save his people from their sins.

Matthew tells the story of Joseph through dreams. Four times in these first chapters, Joseph is guided in a dream. It is fitting that we hear about them tonight, at the edge of sleep. A troubled man lies down in the dark, his decision already made, and in the night, a messenger comes.

Joseph, son of David, don't be afraid.

There they are again, those words. Don't be afraid. To an old priest in the temple. To a young woman in her house. And now, to a tired carpenter lying awake, or half awake, in the dark.

Don't be afraid to take to yourself Mary, your wife. The angel does not say your betrothed. He says your wife. As if to say, nothing has been broken. She is still yours. Take her home.

And the angel tells Joseph the name of the child, the same name Gabriel gave to Mary. You shall name him Jesus, for it is he who shall save his people from their sins. In the language they spoke, the name itself carried that meaning. Yeshua. The Lord saves.

Then Matthew steps back, as he often does, to show us how the story connects with the old scriptures.

Now all this has happened that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying, Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and shall give birth to a son. They shall call his name Immanuel, which is, being interpreted, God with us.

These words come from the prophet Isaiah, written some seven centuries earlier, in a time of fear, when the kingdom of Judah was threatened by enemies on its borders. Matthew reads them in the light of this new birth. Immanuel. God with us.

That name is perhaps the gentlest summary of the whole Christmas story. Not God far away. Not God watching from a distance. God with us. In the small village. In the troubled night. In the dark.

And now we come to what Joseph did when he woke.

Joseph arose from his sleep, and did as the angel of the Lord commanded him, and took his wife to himself; and didn't know her sexually until she had given birth to her firstborn son. He named him Jesus.

He did as the angel of the Lord commanded him. That is all. Matthew records no speech from Joseph, not a single word, in the whole of his gospel. Joseph never says anything. He simply wakes up, and does what he has been asked to do.

He takes Mary home as his wife. And with that, he takes on everything that comes with it. The whispering in the village, perhaps. The looks from the neighbors. The weight of raising a child that is not, in the ordinary way, his own, and loving him as if he were. And Matthew tells us, at the very end, that it was Joseph who named the boy Jesus. In that world, to name a child was to claim him as your own.

A quiet man. A righteous man. A man who listened to his dreams, and got up in the morning, and did the hard and faithful thing.

Let us leave the two of them in Nazareth now, in Joseph's house, as the months pass. Mary growing heavier. Joseph at his work, the sound of his tools in the yard. The olive harvest coming and going on the terraces. The evenings growing long. They expect, surely, that the child will be born right there, in Nazareth, among their own people.

But far away, in Rome, an emperor is about to give an order.
