## Chapter 5: A Name Written on a Tablet

In the house in the hill country of Judea, the waiting is over.

Now the time that Elizabeth should give birth was fulfilled, and she gave birth to a son. Her neighbors and her relatives heard that the Lord had magnified his mercy toward her, and they rejoiced with her.

The neighbors and relatives heard, Luke says, and they rejoiced with her. That is a lovely line, after all those years. The same village that once whispered about Elizabeth, that once looked at her with pity, now crowds into her house to rejoice. Word travels quickly in a small town. The old woman has had a son. Imagine the voices at the door, the women bringing bread and oil and small gifts, the children peering in to see the baby, the old men shaking their heads in wonder.

The Lord had magnified his mercy toward her. That is how the neighbors understood it. Not luck. Not chance. Mercy.

Then, on the eighth day, the family gathers again, for the circumcision of the child. This was the old covenant sign, given to Abraham long before, and at that time it was also the moment when a child was formally given his name.

On the eighth day, they came to circumcise the child; and they would have called him Zacharias, after the name of his father. His mother answered, Not so; but he will be called John. They said to her, There is no one among your relatives who is called by this name.

Everyone assumes the boy will be named Zacharias, after his father. That was a common custom. And it seemed especially fitting here. An old priest, given a son at the very end of his life. What better way to honor him than to give the boy his name?

But Elizabeth says no. He will be called John.

And the relatives are puzzled. There is no one in your family who has that name, they say. It was unusual, in a family like theirs, to choose a name from outside the family line.

So they turn to the father. He still cannot speak. And it seems that he may not have been able to hear, either, because Luke says they made signs to him, asking what he wanted the child to be called.

They made signs to his father, what he would have him called. He asked for a writing tablet, and wrote, His name is John. They all marveled. His mouth was opened immediately and his tongue freed, and he spoke, blessing God.

He asks for a writing tablet. In those days that was often a small wooden board, coated with a thin layer of wax, that you could write on with a pointed stylus, and then smooth over and use again. Picture the old man taking it in his hands. The room falls quiet. Everyone leans in to see. And slowly, carefully, he scratches the words into the wax.

His name is John.

Not, his name will be John. Not, I would like to call him John. His name is John. It had been settled long ago, in the holy place, beside the golden altar, when the angel spoke. Zacharias is not deciding anything now. He is only agreeing with what God had already said.

And in that moment, his mouth is opened. His tongue is freed. And the first thing he does with his voice, after all those silent months, is to bless God.

Fear came on all who lived around them, and all these sayings were talked about throughout all the hill country of Judea. All who heard them laid them up in their heart, saying, What then will this child be? The hand of the Lord was with him.

Fear came on all who lived around them. Not a frightened kind of fear, but the hush that falls over people when they feel that something holy has come close. And the story spreads through all the hill country of Judea. People talk about it at the wells, and in the fields, and around the evening fires. What then will this child be?

And now Zacharias, who has been silent for so long, sings his own song. In the old Latin church this one came to be called the Benedictus, from its first word, blessed. For many centuries it has been sung in the morning prayers, at the beginning of the day. Let us hear the first part of it.

His father Zacharias was filled with the Holy Spirit, and prophesied, saying, Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, for he has visited and redeemed his people; and has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David, as he spoke by the mouth of his holy prophets who have been from of old, salvation from our enemies and from the hand of all who hate us; to show mercy toward our fathers, to remember his holy covenant, the oath which he swore to Abraham our father, to grant to us that we, being delivered out of the hand of our enemies, should serve him without fear, in holiness and righteousness before him all the days of our life.

Much of this first part is about the old promises. Zacharias reaches all the way back. To David, the great king. To the holy prophets. To Abraham, the father of his people, and the oath God swore to him, long, long ago. It is as if the old priest is gathering up all the hopes of all the generations who came before him, and saying, now. Now, at last, it is beginning.

And then his song turns, and becomes very personal. He looks down at the tiny child in his arms.

And you, child, will be called a prophet of the Most High; for you will go before the face of the Lord to prepare his ways, to give knowledge of salvation to his people by the remission of their sins, because of the tender mercy of our God, by which the dawn from on high will visit us, to shine on those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death; to guide our feet into the way of peace.

And you, child. You will be called a prophet of the Most High. You will go before the face of the Lord, to prepare his ways.

Imagine an old father, with a voice that has been silent for all those months, singing those words over his newborn son. He knows he is very old. He knows he will likely not live to see the boy become a man. And still he sings over him, blessing him, sending him out into a future that his father will never see.

And notice how the song ends. It ends with light. The dawn from on high will visit us, to shine on those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death, to guide our feet into the way of peace.

For anyone lying awake in the dark, those are good words to hear. The dawn from on high. A light for those who sit in darkness. A guide for our feet, into the way of peace.

Luke then closes the story of John's childhood with a single sentence, covering many years.

The child was growing and becoming strong in spirit, and was in the desert until the day of his public appearance to Israel.

The child grew, and became strong in spirit, and was in the desert until the day of his public appearance. We will not follow John any further tonight. Many years later, he will come out of the wilderness, by the river Jordan, in clothes of camel's hair, calling people to turn back to God. But that is a story for another night.

For now, let us leave the old priest and his wife in their house in the hills, with a baby asleep in a basket by the lamp, and turn back north once more, to Nazareth. Because in Nazareth, a carpenter has just heard some news that he does not know what to do with.
