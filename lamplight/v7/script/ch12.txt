## Chapter 12: Out of Egypt, Home to Nazareth, and Goodnight

The wise men have gone home another way. And on that same night, perhaps, in the small house in Bethlehem, Joseph lies down to sleep. And once more, a dream comes.

Now when they had departed, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream, saying, Arise and take the young child and his mother, and flee into Egypt, and stay there until I tell you, for Herod will seek the young child to destroy him. He arose and took the young child and his mother by night and departed into Egypt,

Arise, and take the young child and his mother, and flee into Egypt.

And Joseph, the quiet man, who never says a word in all of Matthew's gospel, does exactly what he did before. He gets up. And this time he does not even wait for the morning. He takes the young child and his mother by night, and they leave.

Picture it, very gently. A door opening quietly in the dark. A few things gathered up in haste, perhaps the gifts from the magi among them, wrapped in cloth. A sleepy child lifted from his bed. And a little family slipping out of Bethlehem under the stars, heading south and west, toward the coast road and the long way down into Egypt.

Egypt was beyond Herod's reach. It was a Roman province, but not his kingdom. And it was a place where many Jewish families had lived for centuries. In the great city of Alexandria, there was a large Jewish community, with its own synagogues and scholars. It was there, a few centuries earlier, that the Hebrew scriptures had been translated into Greek. So Egypt was a place where a family from Judea might find shelter among their own people.

And there is an old echo here, too. Long ago, another Joseph, the son of Jacob, the boy with the coat, had been carried down into Egypt, and had saved his family there in a time of famine. And later Moses had led the people out of Egypt again, through the sea. Now a new family goes down into Egypt to be safe, and Matthew quotes the prophet Hosea. Out of Egypt I called my son.

We must not pass too quickly over what happened next, because the gospel does not. But we will speak of it gently.

Matthew tells us that when Herod saw that the wise men had not returned, he was furious, and he sent soldiers to Bethlehem and the countryside around it, to kill the little boys, two years old and under. It is the darkest moment in the whole story of the birth.

No other ancient writer records this event. Bethlehem was a small town, and historians suggest that the number of children may have been small, too small, perhaps, to be noticed in the long, grim record of Herod's cruelties. But it is very much the kind of thing the historians tell us Herod did. And to the families of Bethlehem, it was everything.

Matthew remembers them with words from the prophet Jeremiah, about Rachel, the mother who was buried by the road near Bethlehem so long ago, weeping for her children, and refusing to be comforted, because they are no more.

The Christmas story is not only a story of light. It is also a story of a light that came into a dark world, where there were cruel kings, and grieving mothers, and families running for their lives in the night. Perhaps that is part of why it has comforted so many people, in so many hard times. It does not pretend the darkness is not there. It says that the light came into it anyway.

Then, some time later, Herod died. And once again, Joseph dreams.

But when Herod was dead, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared in a dream to Joseph in Egypt, saying, Arise and take the young child and his mother, and go into the land of Israel, for those who sought the young child's life are dead. He arose and took the young child and his mother, and came into the land of Israel. But when he heard that Archelaus was reigning over Judea in the place of his father, Herod, he was afraid to go there. Being warned in a dream, he withdrew into the region of Galilee, and came and lived in a city called Nazareth; that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through the prophets that he will be called a Nazarene.

Arise, the angel says. Go back. Those who sought the young child's life are dead.

So the family makes the long journey home. But when Joseph hears that Archelaus, one of Herod's sons, is now ruling over Judea, he is afraid. Archelaus had a reputation for cruelty, like his father, and the Romans would later remove him from power. So, warned once again in a dream, Joseph turns north. Back to Galilee. Back to the hills. Back to the small village of Nazareth, where the story began.

Matthew's gospel and Luke's gospel tell the story of these early days in different ways, and they do not try to fit every piece together for us. Luke says simply that when they had done everything the law required, they returned to Galilee, to their own town of Nazareth. Matthew tells of the long road through Egypt first. Both end in the same small village. Let us go there with them now.

When they had accomplished all things that were according to the law of the Lord, they returned into Galilee, to their own city, Nazareth. The child was growing, and was becoming strong in spirit, being filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon him.

The child was growing, and was becoming strong in spirit, being filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon him.

Of all the years of Jesus' childhood, the gospels tell us only one more story. Just one. Let us hear it, as our last story tonight.

His parents went every year to Jerusalem at the feast of the Passover. When he was twelve years old, they went up to Jerusalem according to the custom of the feast; and when they had fulfilled the days, as they were returning, the boy Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem. Joseph and his mother didn't know it, but supposing him to be in the company, they went a day's journey; and they looked for him among their relatives and acquaintances. When they didn't find him, they returned to Jerusalem, looking for him.

Every year, at the feast of Passover, Joseph and Mary went up to Jerusalem. Families from the villages traveled together, in long, cheerful caravans, relatives and neighbors walking side by side, the children running between the groups. So it is easy to see how it happened. When the feast was over and the caravan set off for home, they thought the boy was somewhere among the others. Walking with his cousins, perhaps. With friends from the village.

They walk a whole day before they realize he is not there. And then the panic. Every parent knows it. Searching among the relatives. Calling his name. And then the long, anxious walk back to Jerusalem.

After three days they found him in the temple, sitting in the middle of the teachers, both listening to them and asking them questions. All who heard him were amazed at his understanding and his answers.

You can hear all the fear of those three days in her voice. And his answer is gentle, and strange. Why were you looking for me? Didn't you know that I must be in my Father's house?

And Luke says, very honestly, they didn't understand. Even Mary, who had seen the angel, and heard the shepherds, and held the gifts of the magi. Even she did not understand everything.

And he went down with them and came to Nazareth. He was subject to them, and his mother kept all these sayings in her heart. And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men.

And his mother kept all these sayings in her heart.

That is where the story of the birth comes to rest. Not with angels, or kings, or stars, but with a mother keeping things in her heart. And a boy growing quietly, in a small village in the hills, in wisdom, and in stature, and in favor with God and with people.

So tonight we have traveled a long way. From the smoke of the incense in the temple, where an old priest heard an angel. To a small house in Nazareth, where a young woman said yes. To the hill country, where two women sang together. To the dark of Joseph's dreams. Along the long road to Bethlehem. To a manger, and a baby wrapped in cloth. To shepherds on a hillside, and a sky full of singing. To an old man in the temple who could at last go home in peace. To travelers from the east, kneeling in a small house. And back home again, to Nazareth.

And all the way through, we heard those same three words, spoken again and again, to old and young, to the faithful and the frightened. Don't be afraid.

Perhaps they are words for you, too, tonight. Whatever you are carrying. Whatever is waiting for you tomorrow. Don't be afraid.

Now let the story go. Let the lamps of Bethlehem go out, one by one, along the dark ridge. Let the shepherds' fire burn down to a soft red glow. Let the stars come out over the hills of Judea, bright and silent and very old. Let your breathing slow, and your hands rest, and your thoughts grow quiet.

You have done enough today. You can rest now.

And let me leave you with the words that Simeon spoke, at the end of his long watch, as he held the child in his arms.

Now you are releasing your servant, Master, according to your word, in peace; for my eyes have seen your salvation,

Goodnight. Sleep well. And may you have a peaceful Christmas.
