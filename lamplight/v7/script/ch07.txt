## Chapter 7: The Road to Bethlehem

Luke now widens the frame. Until this moment the story has stayed in small places, a temple sanctuary, a house in the hills, a village in Galilee. But now Luke names the most powerful man in the world.

Now in those days, a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be enrolled. This was the first enrollment made when Quirinius was governor of Syria. All went to enroll themselves, everyone to his own city.

Caesar Augustus. He was the first of the Roman emperors, the great-nephew and adopted son of Julius Caesar. He ruled for more than forty years, and in his time Rome brought much of the world around the Mediterranean Sea under one peace, held in place by its legions. Coins carried his face. Temples were built in his honor. Some of his subjects called him a savior, and the bringer of good news.

And from his palace in Rome, a decree went out that the whole empire should be enrolled. A census. A great counting of people, so that taxes could be collected, and the empire would know who it ruled.

Luke mentions Quirinius, the governor of Syria. Here I should be honest with you, because this is one of the places where careful readers have wondered for a long time. The Jewish historian Josephus records a census under Quirinius in Judea, but he places it some years after the death of Herod, while Luke places the birth of Jesus in Herod's days. Scholars have suggested several ways the two accounts might fit together, and none of them has settled the question for everyone. We do not need to solve it tonight. It is enough to know that it is an old puzzle, and that people of good faith still talk about it.

What matters to Luke is the picture. The emperor, far away, gives an order. And a carpenter in a small village in Galilee has to obey it.

Joseph also went up from Galilee, out of the city of Nazareth, into Judea, to David's city, which is called Bethlehem, because he was of the house and family of David, to enroll himself with Mary, who was pledged to be married to him as wife, being pregnant.

Joseph must go to Bethlehem, the city of David, because he is of the house and family of David. And he takes Mary with him. She is pledged to him, Luke says, and she is with child.

Bethlehem was a small town in the hills of Judea, about six miles south of Jerusalem. It was famous for one reason. Long, long ago, a shepherd boy named David had grown up there, tending his father's flocks in those same hills, before he was anointed king. And many centuries before David, it was near Bethlehem that Rachel, the beloved wife of Jacob, had died and been buried by the road. And it was in the fields of Bethlehem that Ruth, a young widow from Moab, gleaned barley and met Boaz, and became the great-grandmother of David himself.

So Bethlehem was an old place, full of old stories. And one of the old prophets had spoken of it, too. Matthew will quote these words later in our story, but let us hear them now, from the prophet Micah.

But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, being small among the clans of Judah, out of you one will come out to me who is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings out are from of old, from ancient times.

But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, being small among the clans of Judah, out of you one will come out to me who is to be ruler in Israel.

Small among the clans. A small town, overlooked. Just like Nazareth. Just like an old woman in the hills. Just like a young woman who called herself a servant.

Now, the journey. From Nazareth to Bethlehem is about seventy miles in a straight line, and more like ninety miles by the longer road through the Jordan valley, close to a hundred and fifty kilometers. On foot, at the pace of a woman late in her pregnancy, it would have taken many days, perhaps a week or more. Luke does not tell us how they traveled, or which road they took.

You may have pictured Mary riding on a donkey, with Joseph walking beside her, holding the rope. That is how the scene has been painted and sung for many centuries, and it may well be true. Donkeys were the ordinary beasts of burden in that land. But the gospel itself does not mention a donkey. So let us hold that picture gently, as one possibility.

Let us imagine the road, as one likely path. They might have walked down from the hills of Nazareth into the broad valley below, and then east and south, down toward the river Jordan, where the air grows warmer, because the valley lies far below the level of the sea. Some travelers from Galilee went that way, perhaps to avoid passing through Samaria, though the historian Josephus tells us that many Galileans did take the road through Samaria when they went up to the festivals. Then south along the river, among the reeds and the palms, for several days. Then, near Jericho, the long, steep climb up through the bare, golden hills of the wilderness, toward Jerusalem. And then a few more miles south, along the ridge, to Bethlehem.

Or they may have gone the more direct way, straight south along the spine of the hills, through the villages of Samaria. We simply do not know.

But whichever road they took, picture them on it, unhurried. A man and a woman, and perhaps a small animal carrying their few things. Bread wrapped in cloth. A skin of water. A blanket or two for the nights. Mornings of cool, pale light, and long afternoons of walking. Other families on the road too, all going to their own towns, because of the emperor's order. Fires at night. Stars overhead, brighter than we ever see them now. Mary resting her hand on her belly, feeling the child move. Joseph glancing at her, again and again, checking that she is all right.

It is a strange thing, when you think about it. The whole great machinery of the Roman empire, all its roads and officials and soldiers and scrolls, set in motion by one man in a palace. And what it does, in the end, without anyone in Rome knowing or caring, is to move one young couple from a village in Galilee to a little town in Judea, so that a child might be born in the city of David, as the old prophet said.

Let your breathing slow, now, to the pace of their walking. One step, and then another. The road is long, but they are almost there. Up ahead, on its ridge, in the evening light, the small town of Bethlehem.
