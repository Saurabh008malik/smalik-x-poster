## Chapter 3: A Young Woman in Nazareth

Six months have passed since the day in the temple. In the hill country of Judea, Elizabeth's secret is no longer a secret. And now Luke turns north, to Galilee, to a village called Nazareth.

{{LUK 1:26-27}}

Nazareth was a very small place. It does not appear anywhere in the old Hebrew scriptures, and it is not mentioned by the historians of that time. Archaeologists who have dug there describe a modest farming village on a hillside, with houses partly built into the soft limestone, with cisterns for water, terraces for olives and grapes, and simple storage pits cut into the rock. Perhaps a few hundred people lived there. Later in the gospels, someone asks, can any good thing come out of Nazareth? It was that sort of place. Quiet, unremarkable, far from the centers of power.

And in that village lived a young woman named Mary. In her own language, her name would have been Miriam, the same name as the sister of Moses, long ago. Luke tells us she was a virgin, and that she was pledged to be married to a man named Joseph, of the house of David.

We do not know Mary's age. Luke does not tell us. Girls in that world were often betrothed young, in their teens, but the gospel itself is silent, and it is best not to pretend we know. What we can say is that a betrothal in that time was more than an engagement is today. It was a binding promise, a legal bond, made before the couple lived together. Usually the bride stayed in her family's home for a time, perhaps a year, before the wedding, when she would move to her husband's house.

So Mary is in that waiting time. Her life is mapped out ahead of her, as clearly as the paths between the terraces. She will marry Joseph. She will keep a house in Nazareth. She will draw water from the spring, and grind grain, and bake bread, and, if God is kind, raise children. An ordinary, good, small life.

And into that ordinary life, an angel comes.

{{LUK 1:28-29}}

Luke says she was greatly troubled at the saying. Not so much at the sight of the angel, but at the words. Rejoicing, you highly favored one. The Lord is with you. She wondered what kind of greeting this could be.

That is a very honest detail. Mary is not presented as someone who expected this, or who felt she deserved it. She is puzzled. She turns the words over in her mind. Why would anyone greet her like that? She is no one in particular.

And then the angel speaks the words we have heard before.

{{LUK 1:30-33}}

Don't be afraid, Mary. Those same words, once again.

And then the angel tells her of a son. She will name him Jesus, which in her own language would have been Yeshua, a form of the name Joshua, meaning, the Lord saves. He will be great, and will be called the Son of the Most High. He will be given the throne of his father David, and of his kingdom there will be no end.

To a young woman in a small village in Galilee, under the rule of Herod and the shadow of Rome, those words must have sounded enormous. The throne of David. A kingdom without end. The old prophets had spoken of a king like that, a son of David who would one day come. Families had told their children about it for centuries, by the lamp in the evenings.

And Mary asks a question. It is not quite the same as the question Zacharias asked. He asked how he could be sure. She asks how it can happen at all.

{{LUK 1:34-35}}

The angel's answer is gentle and mysterious. The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you. That word, overshadow, is the same kind of word the old scriptures used for the cloud of God's presence resting over the tent in the wilderness. Something holy is coming close, the way a cloud's shadow passes softly over a hillside.

And then Gabriel gives her a sign, without her even asking for one.

{{LUK 1:36-37}}

Elizabeth, your relative. The old woman in the hill country, who everyone said could not have children, is now in her sixth month. For nothing spoken by God is impossible.

Luke calls Elizabeth Mary's relative. The old word does not tell us exactly how they were related. Many people have imagined them as cousins, and that may be so, but the text says only that they were kin.

And now comes the moment the whole story turns on. A young woman, alone in a small house in Nazareth, must give her answer.

{{LUK 1:38}}

Behold, the servant of the Lord. Let it be done to me according to your word.

Let us stay with those words for a little while. She does not understand everything. She cannot know what it will cost her. She cannot know what people in the village will say, or what Joseph will think, or where this road will lead, years from now. But she says yes. Quietly. Simply. Let it be done.

Many painters, over many centuries, have tried to paint that moment. Some show her in a grand room with marble columns, or in a garden full of lilies, kneeling at a carved prayer desk with a book open in front of her. One nineteenth century painter, James Tissot, who traveled to the Holy Land to make his pictures, tried instead to paint these scenes in the simple houses and clothing he had seen with his own eyes in that land. But the text itself gives us only a few lines, and an angel who came and then went away.

And then the angel departed from her. That is all. The room is quiet again. The light through the doorway is the same as before. Somewhere outside, a goat bleats, a neighbor calls to a child, the ordinary afternoon goes on.

But for Mary, nothing will ever be ordinary again. And her first thought, Luke tells us, is to go and see Elizabeth.
