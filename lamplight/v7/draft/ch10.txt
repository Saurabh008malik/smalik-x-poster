## Chapter 10: Simeon and Anna in the Temple

The shepherds have gone back to their fields. The town of Bethlehem goes on with its ordinary days. And the young family stays on for a while, in the city of David.

{{LUK 2:21}}

On the eighth day, just as with John in the hill country, the child is circumcised, and given his name. Jesus. The name the angel had spoken to Mary in Nazareth, and to Joseph in his dream. Now it is spoken aloud, among family and neighbors, and it belongs to him.

Then, some weeks later, there is another journey. Not a long one this time. Just six miles or so, north along the ridge, to Jerusalem.

{{LUK 2:22-24}}

The law of Moses had two old customs for a time like this. One was that a mother, some weeks after giving birth, would bring an offering to the temple, and be declared ritually clean again. For a son, that time was forty days. The other was that a firstborn son was considered to belong in a special way to the Lord, and was to be redeemed, presented, given back to God and then received again.

Luke tells us what offering Mary and Joseph brought. A pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons. That small detail says a great deal. In the book of Leviticus, the usual offering was a lamb, along with a bird. But if a family could not afford a lamb, they could bring two birds instead. So without saying it directly, Luke lets us know that Mary and Joseph were poor. They brought the offering of the poor.

Picture them, then, climbing the great stairways up into the temple courts. The temple that Herod had rebuilt was enormous, one of the wonders of that world. Gleaming white stone, and gold catching the morning sun. Wide courts filled with pilgrims, priests, merchants selling animals for sacrifice, teachers sitting with their students in the shade of the long colonnades. And somewhere in that great crowd, a young couple from Galilee, carrying a baby and two small birds in a cage of reeds.

No one noticed them. Why would they? There were families like them every day.

But someone was waiting.

{{LUK 2:25-28}}

Simeon. We know almost nothing about him. Luke does not say he was a priest, or a scholar, or anyone important. He was simply a man in Jerusalem, righteous and devout, looking for the consolation of Israel. He was waiting for comfort, for his people. And the Holy Spirit had shown him that he would not die until he had seen the one he was waiting for.

Many paintings show Simeon as a very old man, with a long white beard, and that is how he is usually imagined. Luke does not actually give his age, but his words suggest a man near the end of his life. How long had he been waiting? Years, perhaps. Decades. Coming up to the temple, day after day, looking at every face.

And on this day, he is led by the Spirit into the temple courts. And he sees them. Among all the families, all the crowds, this one young couple. And he goes to them, and takes the child up into his arms.

Imagine Mary's surprise, as a stranger reaches out for her baby. And then imagine what she sees in the old man's face.

And then Simeon begins to sing. This is the third song in Luke's story of the birth. And of all of them, it is perhaps the one most fitting for the end of the day. For many centuries it has been sung at night prayers, the very last service before sleep. In Latin it is called the Nunc Dimittis, from its first words, now you let depart.

{{LUK 2:29-32}}

Now you are releasing your servant, Master, according to your word, in peace.

It is the song of a man whose long watch is finally over. Like a guard at the end of the night, who sees the dawn coming, and can at last lay down his spear and go home to rest. My eyes have seen your salvation. That is enough. I can go now, in peace.

A light for revelation to the nations. Simeon sees that this child is not only for his own people, but for the whole world. A light for everyone.

And then Simeon turns to Mary, and his voice changes.

{{LUK 2:33-35}}

A sword will pierce through your own soul.

It is the first shadow in the story. Until now, everything has been joy. But Simeon sees further. He sees that this child will be spoken against. That there will be sorrow ahead. And that his mother will feel it, like a blade, in her own heart. Mary, who keeps things and ponders them, must have kept these words too, for a very long time.

And then, as if to soften that shadow, someone else arrives.

{{LUK 2:36-38}}

Anna. A prophetess, Luke calls her, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher. She had been married only seven years when her husband died. And then she had been a widow for a very long time. The old text can be read in two ways. Either she was now eighty-four years old, or she had been a widow for eighty-four years, which would make her more than a hundred. Either way, she was very old.

And she never left the temple. She worshiped there, night and day, with fasting and prayer. Think of her as one of the quiet ones, the people you see in every place of prayer, who seem to have always been there, praying in a corner, known to everyone, noticed by no one.

And at that very hour, she comes up. She sees the child. And she gives thanks to God, and begins to speak about him to everyone who was looking for the redemption of Jerusalem.

Two very old people, a man and a woman. Both of them had waited their whole lives. Both of them had stayed faithful through long, uneventful years. And at the very end, they were the ones who recognized the child, when all the priests and teachers in the great temple walked right past.

Perhaps there is comfort in that, if you are one of the ones who waits. Who keeps faith through long ordinary years, without seeing much happen. Simeon and Anna waited, too. And they were not forgotten.

Let us leave them now, in the temple courts, as the afternoon light grows long and golden across the stones. An old man who can at last go home in peace. An old woman telling everyone she meets. And a young couple, carrying their baby back down the great stairs, wondering at all that had been said.
