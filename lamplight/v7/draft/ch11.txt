## Chapter 11: Wise Men from the East

Now we turn back to Matthew's gospel. Luke gave us the shepherds, the people from nearby, from the fields just outside the town. Matthew gives us visitors from very far away.

{{MAT 2:1-2}}

Wise men from the east. In the old Greek of Matthew's gospel, they are called magoi, magi. The word first belonged to a class of priests and learned men in ancient Persia. By the time of Matthew it was used more widely, for scholars and astrologers, people who studied the stars and the old writings, and who served as advisers to kings. Matthew does not tell us exactly where they came from. The east could mean Persia, or Babylon, where many Jewish families still lived, or the deserts of Arabia. We do not know.

We also do not know how many there were. Matthew never says. Because they brought three gifts, people came to imagine three travelers, and in time they were given names, and crowns, and were sung about as kings. Those are old and lovely traditions, but they came later. The gospel itself simply says, wise men from the east.

And they had seen a star. For we saw his star in the east, they say, and have come to worship him.

People have wondered about that star for many centuries. Was it a comet? A new star, blazing up and then fading? A rare meeting of planets in the night sky? Astronomers, including the great Johannes Kepler four hundred years ago, have tried to work out what the magi might have seen. Kepler thought of a conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn that happened in those years. Others have suggested other things. No one knows for certain. And perhaps it does not matter. What matters is that they saw something, and they set out.

Imagine their journey. Weeks, perhaps months, of travel. Camels or donkeys loaded with supplies, though again the gospel does not say how they traveled. Across deserts and river valleys, by caravan routes and old roads, sleeping in tents or in roadside shelters. Looking up, each clear night, at the sky.

And where do they go, when they arrive? They go to Jerusalem, to the capital, to the king's palace. Where else would you look for a newborn king?

{{MAT 2:3-6}}

King Herod was troubled. Of course he was. Herod was an old man by then, and a very suspicious one. He had built great fortresses and palaces, and he had rebuilt the temple in splendor, but he was always afraid of losing his throne. The ancient historians tell us that he had members of his own family put to death because he feared they were plotting against him. And now strangers from the east arrive at his court, asking, where is he who is born King of the Jews?

And all Jerusalem with him, Matthew adds. When Herod was troubled, everyone was troubled. People knew what an anxious Herod could do.

So Herod gathers the chief priests and scribes, the experts in the old writings, and asks them where the Christ, the anointed one, was to be born. And they know the answer at once. In Bethlehem of Judea. And they quote the words of the prophet Micah, the same words we heard on the road.

{{MAT 2:7-8}}

Herod calls the wise men secretly. He asks them exactly when the star appeared. And then he sends them to Bethlehem, with a request that sounds so pious. Go and search diligently for the young child. When you have found him, bring me word, so that I also may come and worship him.

The reader knows, of course, that Herod has no intention of worshiping anyone. But the wise men set out.

{{MAT 2:9-10}}

The star which they saw in the east goes before them, Matthew says, until it comes and stands over the place where the young child was. And when they saw the star, they rejoiced with exceedingly great joy.

That is a lovely phrase, exceedingly great joy. After all those months of travel, after the anxious court in Jerusalem, after the strange questions of the king, they see their star again in the night sky, leading them the last six miles south, along the dark ridge to Bethlehem. And their hearts lift.

{{MAT 2:11}}

They came into the house. Notice that. Not a stable, not a manger. A house. Matthew does not tell us how much time had passed since the birth, but his story suggests it may have been some while, perhaps many months. And Jesus is now called not a baby, but a young child. So in Matthew's telling, the magi did not arrive on the night of the birth, as they so often do in our nativity scenes, but later, when the family had found a home in Bethlehem.

And there they see the young child, with Mary, his mother. And these learned, travelled, perhaps wealthy men from a far country fall down and worship him.

Then they open their treasures. Gold, frankincense, and myrrh.

Gold was the gift for a king. Frankincense was a sweet resin, from trees that grow in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, burned as incense in the temple, so it was often linked with worship and with God. Myrrh was another precious resin, used in perfumes and medicines, and also for anointing the bodies of the dead. Over the centuries, Christians have seen meanings in each of them. A gift for a king. A gift for God. And a gift that quietly points ahead, toward sorrow, and toward burial.

Imagine Mary, holding those gifts in her hands. The heavy gleam of gold. The sharp, sweet smell of frankincense. The bitter, earthy scent of myrrh. One more thing to keep, and ponder in her heart.

{{MAT 2:12}}

And then, once again, a dream. The wise men are warned not to return to Herod. So they go back to their own country another way.

We never hear of them again. They simply vanish, back into the east, back across the deserts and the river valleys, back under their stars. Let us imagine them, if you like, on their long road home. Riding slowly through the cold desert night, under a vast dark sky full of stars. Talking quietly, perhaps, about the young child they had seen in a small house in Bethlehem. Wondering what would become of him.

But in Jerusalem, an old and frightened king is waiting for them. And they are not coming back.
