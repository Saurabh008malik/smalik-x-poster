## Chapter 8: No Room, and a Manger

They have come at last to Bethlehem. And Luke tells what happened there in only two verses. Two short, plain verses, that have been read aloud on more Christmas nights than anyone could ever count.

{{LUK 2:6-7}}

That is all. While they were there, the day came. She gave birth to her firstborn son. She wrapped him in bands of cloth, and laid him in a feeding trough, because there was no room for them in the inn.

Let us go through those words very slowly, one at a time, the way you might turn a small, precious thing over in your hands.

While they were there. Luke does not say that Mary went into labor the night they arrived, though that is how the story is often told. He says only that while they were there, the days were fulfilled. They may have been in Bethlehem for some days, or even longer, before the child came.

She gave birth to her firstborn son. Luke does not mention a midwife, or any other woman helping her, though in a crowded town someone may well have come. Many painters have filled the scene with angels, or with crowds. But the gospel is very quiet. A young woman, far from her mother and her village, bringing her first child into the world.

She wrapped him in bands of cloth. In that world, newborn babies were wrapped snugly in long strips of cloth, swaddled, so that they felt held and safe, and their arms and legs were kept still and warm. Mary does this herself. It is the most ordinary thing in the world. Every mother in Bethlehem would have done the same.

And laid him in a feeding trough. The older translations say a manger. A manger is a trough or box from which animals eat. Some were carved out of stone, others built of wood, or shaped from clay. So the baby was laid, not in a cradle, but in the place where the animals were fed, on a bed of straw or hay, perhaps.

The gospel does not actually mention any animals. No ox, no donkey, no sheep. But a manger means that animals were near, or had been. And from very early times, Christians remembered words from the prophet Isaiah, the ox knows his owner, and the donkey his master's crib, and so the ox and the donkey found their way into countless paintings and carvings of this night. They are gentle companions to imagine, breathing warm in the cold air. Let us keep them, if you like, as a kind picture, and remember that the text itself is silent.

Because there was no room for them in the inn. This is perhaps the most famous phrase of all. We picture a busy inn, a crowded courtyard, and an innkeeper at the door, shaking his head. Many Christmas plays have given that innkeeper a line or two, and sometimes a kind wife who points the way to the stable.

But here, too, it helps to look closely. The gospel does not mention an innkeeper at all. And the Greek word that is often translated inn, kataluma, can also mean a guest room, a place to lodge. Luke uses the same word later in his gospel for the upstairs room where Jesus would share the Passover meal with his friends.

Many scholars now picture the scene a little differently. In the villages of that land, ordinary family houses were often simple. A main living room where the family ate and slept, sometimes with a guest room built on top or to one side. And at one end, often a little lower, a space where the animals were brought in at night, for safety and for warmth, with mangers cut into the floor or set along the wall. Other traditions, recorded very early, speak of a cave. Even now, in the hills around Bethlehem, there are caves that were used for keeping animals, and some houses were built against the rock, with a cave at the back.

So perhaps the guest room was already full of other relatives, all come for the census. And so the young mother and her baby were given the only space that was left, the lower part of the house, or the cave behind it, where the animals were kept, warm and quiet, out of the crowd.

We cannot know exactly. But either way, the heart of the picture does not change. The child was born at the edge of things. Not in a palace. Not in a priest's house. Not even in the main room of an ordinary home. But in the place where the animals were fed, because there was no other room.

The church later came to celebrate this birth on the twenty-fifth of December, and by the fourth century that date was widely kept in the West. But the gospels themselves do not give us a date, or even a season. We do not know whether it was winter or spring. What we have is the picture. A young mother. A carpenter who had traveled a long way. A baby, wrapped tight in strips of cloth. And a manger, made into a bed.

Let us stay here a moment longer, in the quiet. Picture the low light of a small clay lamp. The smell of straw and animals and woodsmoke. The muffled sounds of the crowded town around them. Mary, exhausted, leaning back against the wall. Joseph, close by, keeping watch. And the child, asleep at last, his small chest rising and falling, rising and falling.

Let your own breathing follow that slow rhythm, if you like. In, and out. Rising, and falling. The whole world going on outside, not knowing. And here, in the quiet, a mother watching her sleeping child.

But not everyone in Bethlehem is asleep tonight. Out in the fields beyond the town, some men are awake, keeping watch over their flocks.
