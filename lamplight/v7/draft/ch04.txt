## Chapter 4: Mary Goes to the Hill Country

Luke tells us that Mary set out with haste. She does not wait. She does not, as far as we know, tell anyone in Nazareth what has happened. She simply packs what she needs, and goes south.

{{LUK 1:39-40}}

It was a long way. From Nazareth to the hills of Judea was a journey of several days on foot. Luke does not tell us whether she traveled with others, though it would have been usual, and safer, to walk with a group of travelers, perhaps pilgrims heading toward Jerusalem. We do not know which road she took. Some travelers went down into the warm Jordan valley and walked south along the river, then climbed up through the wilderness toward Jerusalem. Others went more directly, along the ridge of hills through Samaria.

Let us imagine her walking, gently, without hurrying the picture. A young woman on a dusty road, a bundle on her shoulder. Olive trees, grey and silver, on the terraced hillsides. Shepherds with their flocks on the slopes. Villages of pale stone glimpsed in the distance. The evenings coming early. Nights spent wherever travelers could find shelter. And all the while, carrying a secret she could hardly put into words.

And at last, a town in the hill country. A house. A door. And an old woman inside.

{{LUK 1:41-45}}

Mary has not even had time to explain. She simply greets Elizabeth, the ordinary greeting of one woman arriving at a relative's house. And at the sound of her voice, the child inside Elizabeth leaps.

Elizabeth is filled with the Holy Spirit, and she cries out with a loud voice. It is the first loud voice we have heard in this story. Zacharias is still silent. Mary has kept her secret. But Elizabeth, the old woman who hid herself for five months, now lifts up her voice and calls out a blessing.

Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb. Why am I so favored, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?

Think what that must have meant to Mary. She had traveled all that way, carrying something no one else could understand. Perhaps she was afraid she would not be believed. And before she has said a word about it, Elizabeth already knows. Elizabeth already rejoices with her. The old woman and the young woman, one at the end of her years and one at the very beginning, standing together in a doorway, both of them carrying a child that should not have been possible.

Blessed is she who believed, Elizabeth says. That is a quiet contrast with her husband, sitting somewhere in the house, still unable to speak.

And then Mary answers. Not with ordinary words, but with a song.

{{LUK 1:46-49}}

This song has many names. In the old Latin church it was called the Magnificat, from its first word in Latin, which means, it magnifies. For many centuries it has been sung in evening prayers, as the light fades, in monasteries and cathedrals and small village churches, night after night. So it is a fitting song for us, too, at this hour.

Let us hear the rest of it.

{{LUK 1:50-55}}

It is a remarkable song for a young woman from Nazareth to sing. It sounds a great deal like the old songs of her people, especially the song of Hannah, many centuries earlier, when Hannah, too, was given a child after long sorrow. Mary would have known those songs. She would have heard them all her life.

And notice what the song is about. It is not about palaces or armies. He has scattered the proud. He has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly. He has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty.

It is a song about the world turned gently upside down. About God noticing the people that everyone else overlooks. An old woman whom the village pitied. A young woman from a village no one had heard of. The hungry, the humble, the small.

He has looked at the humble state of his servant, Mary sings. Almost the same thing Elizabeth said, months before, in her quiet house. He has looked at me.

And then Luke adds one short sentence.

{{LUK 1:56}}

Three months. Mary stays with Elizabeth for about three months. Long enough to help the old woman through the heavy last months before her baby comes, though Luke does not say whether Mary was still there on the day of the birth. Long enough, perhaps, for the two of them to talk, in the evenings, about everything that had happened, and everything that might happen still. Long enough for Mary to grow used to her own secret, in a house where it was understood.

Picture those evenings. A lamp on a ledge. Two women sitting close, one with her hands folded over her round belly, the other with the first quiet signs of her own. Zacharias nearby, listening, unable to join in, but perhaps nodding, perhaps smiling. The hills outside growing dark. The stars coming out over Judea.

And then Mary goes home, to Nazareth, to face whatever waits for her there. And in the house in the hill country, Elizabeth's time has come.
