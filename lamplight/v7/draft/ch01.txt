## Chapter 1: Welcome, and an Old Priest in the Temple

Good evening, and welcome. Let yourself sink a little deeper into the place where you are lying. Let your shoulders come down away from your ears. Let your hands rest open and loose. The day is over now, or nearly over, and whatever it asked of you, it has finished asking. There is nothing more you need to do tonight except listen, or not listen, and rest.

This is Lamplight Scripture. Tonight, as the year turns toward its darkest and longest nights, we are going to hear the oldest Christmas story there is. Not the story of snow and bells and shop windows, lovely as those are, but the first telling of it, the one written down nearly two thousand years ago, in the gospels of Luke and of Matthew.

It is a story of very ordinary people. An old priest and his wife who had given up hoping for a child. A young woman in a small village in the hills of Galilee. A carpenter who was asked to trust something he could not fully understand. Shepherds keeping watch in the cold. Two very old people waiting in the temple courts. And travelers from a far country, following a light in the sky.

I will tell the story in plain words, slowly, and in between we will read the old text itself, from a public domain translation called the World English Bible. Where the old text uses the holy name of God, I will say the Lord, as many readers do.

You do not need to follow every name, or remember every place. If you drift away somewhere on the road to Bethlehem, that is perfectly all right. The story will keep traveling without you, and it will be here when you come back.

Luke begins his gospel with a short letter, a few lines addressed to a man named Theophilus. Let us hear them, because they tell us what Luke thought he was doing.

{{LUK 1:1-4}}

So Luke tells us that he was not an eyewitness himself. He gathered what others had handed down, and he tried to set it out in order. That is a good way to think of tonight, too. We are listening to something that has been handed down, carefully, from one generation to the next, a very long time.

And now the story itself begins, not in Bethlehem, and not with Mary, but somewhere else entirely, with an old man and an old woman.

{{LUK 1:5-7}}

Let us stay with those two for a while. The old text calls him Zacharias. Many people today know him by the Hebrew form of the name, Zechariah. His wife's name was Elizabeth. They lived, Luke tells us, in the days of Herod, the king of Judea. That was Herod the Great, a powerful and suspicious ruler who held his throne with the permission of Rome, and who rebuilt the temple in Jerusalem on a grand scale.

Zacharias was a priest. In those days the priests of Israel were divided into twenty-four groups, sometimes called courses or divisions, and each group took its turn to serve in the temple for a week at a time, about twice a year. Zacharias belonged to the division of Abijah. And Elizabeth, Luke adds, was herself from a priestly family, one of the daughters of Aaron.

Luke says something very kind about them. He says they were both righteous before God, walking blamelessly. That does not mean they never made a mistake. It means they were faithful people, quietly keeping the old ways, year after year, without any great reward that anyone could see.

And then comes the sorrow, told in a single plain sentence. They had no child. And they were both well advanced in years.

In that world, to be without children was a deep grief, and often a source of shame as well, as if people wondered what had gone wrong. Elizabeth had carried that grief for a very long time. By now, they had surely stopped praying for a child, or perhaps they still prayed, out of habit, the way you might keep a lamp burning in a window long after you have stopped expecting anyone to come.

Now the time came for the division of Abijah to serve. Zacharias traveled up to Jerusalem, as he had done many times before.

{{LUK 1:8-10}}

Let us picture it, gently. This is an imagined scene, built from what we know of the temple, but Luke gives us the heart of it.

Each day, morning and evening, incense was burned on a golden altar inside the holy place, the sanctuary of the temple, just in front of the curtain. There were so many priests that the honor of burning the incense was decided by lot. A priest might wait his whole life and never be chosen. According to later Jewish tradition, a priest who had been chosen once did not cast the lot for it again.

And on this day, the lot fell to Zacharias. An old man, near the end of his years of service, was given the one honor he had perhaps never expected to receive.

So he went in alone. Outside, in the courts, a great crowd of people stood praying. They would wait for him to come out again and bless them. Inside, all was quiet. The light of the great lampstand. The smoke of the incense rising, sweet and grey, toward the high ceiling.

And then, Zacharias realized that he was not alone.

{{LUK 1:11-17}}

Notice the first words the angel says. Don't be afraid. We will hear those words again and again tonight. They come to an old priest, and to a young girl, and to a carpenter, and to shepherds on a hillside. It is almost as if the whole story is wrapped inside those three words. Don't be afraid.

And then the angel says something tender. Your request has been heard. Zacharias had prayed for a son, perhaps many years ago, perhaps so long ago that he had almost forgotten. And the prayer had not been lost. It had been heard all along.

The child, the angel says, will be called John. He will bring joy, not only to his parents, but to many. And he will go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, and to make ready a people prepared.

Those words reach back to the very last lines of the old prophets, where the coming of Elijah is promised before the great day of the Lord. So in the smoke of the incense, in the quiet of the sanctuary, a very old hope is being stirred awake.

But Zacharias is an old man. And old men, even faithful ones, have learned to be careful with hope. Let us leave him there for a moment, standing by the golden altar, with the incense rising around him, and the angel's words still hanging in the air.
