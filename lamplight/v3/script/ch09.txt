## Chapter 9: A Song by the Sea, and Bread from Heaven

Let us stay a little while longer on the shore of the sea, in the bright morning after the crossing.

When the people understood what had happened, that they were truly safe, and truly free, they did what people have always done when their hearts are too full for ordinary words. They sang.

Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the Lord, and said, I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. He has thrown the horse and his rider into the sea. The Lord is my strength and song. He has become my salvation. This is my God, and I will praise him; my father's God, and I will exalt him.

It is one of the oldest songs in the Bible, and many scholars believe its words are among the most ancient parts of the whole book. It goes on for many verses, telling of the waters standing up like a heap, and of the peoples far away trembling at the news, and of the Lord leading his people like a shepherd to his holy dwelling.

And then Miriam stepped forward. Miriam, the sister who had once hidden among the reeds, watching over a baby in a basket. She was an old woman now. But she took a tambourine in her hand, and all the women followed her, with their own tambourines, dancing.

Miriam answered them, Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously. He has thrown the horse and his rider into the sea.

Let us picture it. The sun shining on the water. The beach full of people. And an old woman dancing in the sand, her grey hair loose, beating her tambourine, and hundreds of women dancing behind her in a long, swaying line, singing the same words over and over, their voices rising over the sound of the waves.

But songs end, and the journey had to go on.

Moses led the people away from the sea, out into the wilderness of Shur. And here the wilderness showed its harder face. For three days they walked across the dry land and found no water. Then they came to a place where there was water at last, but when they knelt to drink, it was bitter. They named the place Marah, which means bitter. And the people grumbled against Moses. What shall we drink?

Moses cried out to the Lord, and the Lord showed him a piece of wood, a tree, and Moses threw it into the water, and the water became sweet. And there God gave them a promise. I am the Lord who heals you.

Then they came to a beautiful place.

They came to Elim, where there were twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees. They encamped there by the waters.

Let us rest at Elim for a while, as they did. After the long dry days, a green oasis. Twelve springs bubbling up out of the ground, clear and cold. Seventy palm trees, their long leaves moving in the breeze, throwing cool shade over the sand. The people filling their water skins, washing their faces, letting the children splash in the pools. The animals drinking deeply. And at night, the stars above the palms, and the sound of water running quietly through the dark.

From Elim they went on, into a wide and empty region called the wilderness of Sin. A month had passed since they left Egypt. The food they had carried was running out. And the people began to complain again, with a bitter kind of homesickness. If only we had died in Egypt, they said, where we sat by the pots of meat and ate all the bread we wanted. You have brought us out here to starve.

It is easy to judge them. But hunger is hard, and fear makes people forget. In their memories, Egypt had grown softer than it really was.

And God did not scold them. He answered with a gift.

Behold, I will rain bread from the sky for you, and the people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day.

That evening, a great flock of quail came, small brown birds flying low over the camp, and settled all around, so that there was meat to eat. And in the morning, there was dew lying around the camp. When the dew dried away, there on the ground lay something small and round and white, fine as frost.

When the children of Israel saw it, they said to one another, What is it? For they didn't know what it was. Moses said to them, It is the bread which the Lord has given you to eat.

In Hebrew, what is it is man hu. And so the bread was called manna. The scripture describes it lovingly. It was like coriander seed, white, and its taste was like wafers made with honey.

There were rules for gathering it. Each family was to gather only as much as they needed for one day. Some greedy or anxious people tried to keep extra until the morning, and it spoiled. But on the sixth day, they were to gather twice as much, because on the seventh day, the day of rest, the Sabbath, no manna would fall. And the manna gathered for the Sabbath did not spoil. It was a lesson repeated every morning, for forty years. Take what you need for today. Trust that there will be enough tomorrow.

Let us leave them there, on a still morning in the wilderness. The sun is just rising over the desert. The dew is drying. And all through the quiet camp, families are walking out with baskets, bending down to gather the small white bread from the ground, enough for one day, and no more, while the children run ahead, laughing, calling to one another, what is it, what is it.
