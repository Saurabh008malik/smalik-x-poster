## Chapter 7: The Night of the Passover

This chapter holds the most solemn night in the whole story. We will go through it quietly and carefully, the way one walks through a house where people are sleeping.

Before the last sign came, God gave Moses instructions for the people of Israel. They were to prepare for a special meal, a meal that their children, and their children's children, would remember for ever.

Here are the old words.

This month shall be to you the beginning of months. It shall be the first month of the year to you. Speak to all the congregation of Israel, saying, On the tenth day of this month, they shall take to them every man a lamb, according to their fathers' houses, a lamb for a household; and if the household is too little for a lamb, then he and his neighbor next to his house shall take one according to the number of the souls.

Each family was to choose a lamb, a young one without any blemish. If a family was too small, they were to share with their neighbours, so that no one would be left out. They were to keep the lamb for four days, and then, at twilight on the fourteenth day of the month, they were to prepare it. And they were to take some of its blood and mark the doorposts and the lintel, the beam above the door, of the house where they ate.

Then they were to eat the meal in a particular way. The lamb roasted over the fire. Flat bread, made without yeast, because there would be no time to wait for bread to rise. And bitter herbs, to remind them of the bitterness of slavery.

This is how you shall eat it: with your belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and you shall eat it in haste: it is the Lord's Passover.

Let us picture one of those houses on that evening. A small room, lit by the fire and a clay lamp. A family gathered close, mothers and fathers, grandparents, children with wide eyes. Everyone dressed for a journey, cloaks on, sandals tied, walking sticks leaning against the wall. The smell of roasting meat and baking bread. Dark marks on the doorposts outside. And everyone eating quickly, quietly, listening.

The Lord had said that he would pass through the land of Egypt that night, and strike the firstborn. But when he saw the mark on the doorposts, he would pass over that house, and no harm would come to it. That is where the name of the feast comes from. Passover.

This day shall be a memorial for you. You shall keep it as a feast to the Lord. You shall keep it as a feast throughout your generations by an ordinance forever.

And so it has been. For more than three thousand years, by tradition, Jewish families have gathered on this night each spring to eat the flat bread and the bitter herbs, and to tell the story again, with the youngest child asking the old questions. Why is this night different from all other nights? It is one of the longest remembered meals in human history.

Then came midnight.

The scripture tells it in a few short, heavy words, and we will not linger over it. At midnight the last plague fell on Egypt. In every Egyptian house, from the palace of Pharaoh to the poorest prisoner's cell, the firstborn died. And a great cry rose up in Egypt, for there was not a house where there was not one dead.

It is one of the hardest passages in the Bible, and it is right to feel the weight of it. The grief of Egyptian mothers and fathers was real grief. Through all the long weeks of the plagues, Pharaoh had been warned again and again, and had chosen, again and again, to keep a whole people in chains. And at last the sorrow he had brought on the Hebrews came home to his own house. The old story does not pretend that freedom came without pain.

In the middle of that night, Pharaoh sent for Moses and Aaron. The proud king who had said, I do not know the Lord, now said something very different.

Rise up, get out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel; and go, serve the Lord, as you have said! Take both your flocks and your herds, as you have said, and be gone; and bless me also!

Bless me also. After everything, the king of Egypt was asking for a blessing.

The Egyptian people, too, urged the Israelites to hurry. They gave them gifts of silver and gold and clothing as they left. And the Hebrews picked up their bread dough before it had risen, still in the kneading bowls, wrapped it in their cloaks, and set it on their shoulders. Then, in the darkness before dawn, they began to walk.

The children of Israel traveled from Rameses to Succoth, about six hundred thousand on foot who were men, in addition to children. A mixed multitude went up also with them, with flocks, herds, and even very much livestock.

A great river of people, flowing out of Egypt. Families with children on their shoulders. Old people leaning on sticks. Flocks of sheep and goats, and herds of cattle, raising dust. And with them, the scripture says, a mixed multitude, people of other backgrounds who chose to go with them. Moses also carried with him the bones of Joseph, as Joseph had asked, long before, so that he could be buried at last in the land promised to his fathers.

Now the time that the children of Israel lived in Egypt was four hundred thirty years. It is a night to be much observed to the Lord for bringing them out from the land of Egypt.

Let us leave them there, at the end of that long night, walking east as the sky begins to grow pale. Behind them, the dark cities of Egypt. Ahead of them, the desert, wide and grey and silent. And over everything, the first soft light of a new morning, the first morning of their freedom.
