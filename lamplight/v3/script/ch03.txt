## Chapter 3: A Stranger in Midian

Let us move forward many years now. The baby from the basket has grown into a man.

The book of Exodus does not tell us about his boyhood at all. It moves straight from the child to the grown man, and later scripture suggests that Moses was about forty years old when the next part of the story happened. He was a prince of Egypt in his clothes and his learning. But in his heart, he had not forgotten his own people.

Here are the old words.

In those days, when Moses had grown up, he went out to his brothers and saw their burdens. He saw an Egyptian striking a Hebrew, one of his brothers.

Let us picture it. Moses walking out to the building sites, in the heat and dust, where long lines of Hebrew workers were carrying bricks and mortar under the burning sun. And there, an Egyptian overseer beating one of the workers. Something in Moses broke. He looked this way and that, and when he thought no one was watching, he struck the Egyptian, and the man died, and Moses hid his body in the sand.

It was a violent, angry, hasty act, and the scripture does not praise it. It simply tells it. Moses had seen injustice, and he wanted to put it right, but he tried to do it with his own hands, in his own way, in secret. And it did not work.

The next day he went out again, and he saw two Hebrew men fighting with each other. He tried to make peace between them. But one of them turned on him and said, Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you plan to kill me, as you killed the Egyptian?

Moses was afraid. His secret was known. And soon Pharaoh heard of it too, and wanted him dead.

So Moses fled. He left the palace, and the river, and the green fields of Egypt, and he went out east, across the desert, far away, to a land called Midian. And there, tired and alone, a stranger with nothing, he sat down beside a well.

Wells are special places in the Bible. Again and again, the great stories of love and new beginnings happen beside them. And so it was here.

Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters. They came and drew water, and filled the troughs to water their father's flock. The shepherds came and drove them away; but Moses stood up and helped them, and watered their flock.

Let us picture the scene. Evening light over the desert hills. Seven young women at the well with their father's sheep and goats, lowering their buckets, filling the long stone troughs. And then a rough band of shepherds arriving, pushing them aside, wanting the water first. And a tired stranger in dusty Egyptian clothes rising up, and standing between them, and driving the shepherds away. Then, instead of leaving, he drew the water himself and watered all the flock.

It is the same Moses as before, the man who cannot bear to see the weak pushed around. But this time he did not strike anyone in secret. He simply stood up, and helped.

The young women hurried home to their father, who was surprised to see them back so early. When he asked why, they said, An Egyptian delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds, and moreover he drew water for us, and watered the flock.

Their father's name was Reuel, and in other places he is called Jethro. He was a priest of Midian, and a wise and hospitable man. Where is he, he asked his daughters. Why is it that you have left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread.

So Moses came in from the dusk to a desert tent, and was given bread and a place by the fire. And he stayed. The old words say that Moses was content to dwell with the man.

Jethro gave him his daughter Zipporah as his wife, and in time they had a son. Moses named him Gershom, which sounds like the Hebrew for a stranger there, for he said, I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land.

And so the prince of Egypt became a shepherd in the desert. For many years, perhaps for forty years, he lived a quiet life in Midian, leading his father-in-law's flocks across the dry hills, looking for grass and water, sleeping under the stars. It was a slow life, a patient life. He learned the paths of the wilderness, where the springs were, where the shade was, how to find his way by the stars at night. He did not know it, but he was being prepared.

Meanwhile, far away in Egypt, nothing had become easier for his people.

In the course of those many days, the king of Egypt died, and the children of Israel sighed because of the bondage, and they cried, and their cry came up to God because of the bondage. God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. God saw the children of Israel, and God understood.

Let us rest on those last words for a moment. God saw. God understood. Sometimes, when life is hard, it can feel as though no one sees, and no one hears. This old story says that the cry of a suffering people does rise up, and that it is heard.

Let us leave Moses there, on a cool evening in Midian, sitting at the door of a tent, with his small son asleep against his shoulder. The sheep are folded for the night. The desert is silent, and above the dark hills, the stars are coming out, one by one, more than anyone could ever count.
