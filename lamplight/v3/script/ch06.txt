## Chapter 6: Hail, Locusts, and a Darkness That Could Be Felt

Let us go on now, slowly, through the long season of the plagues.

After the river of blood, and the frogs, and the gnats, the wonders went on. And from this point in the story, something new appears. The land of Goshen, where the people of Israel lived, began to be spared. The trouble fell on Egypt, but not on them. It was as if a line had been drawn across the land.

Next came swarms of flies, thick clouds of them, filling the houses of the Egyptians and darkening the air. But in Goshen, there were none. Pharaoh called Moses and said the people could make their sacrifice, if they did it inside Egypt. Moses said no, they must go three days into the wilderness. Very well, said Pharaoh, only don't go very far, and pray for me. Moses prayed, and the flies were taken away, every one. And once again Pharaoh changed his mind, and would not let the people go.

Then a sickness came on the animals of Egypt, on the horses and donkeys, the camels, the cattle and the sheep in the fields. But the animals of Israel were not touched. Pharaoh sent servants to see, and they found that not one of the Israelites' animals had died. And still his heart was hard.

Then Moses and Aaron took handfuls of soot from a furnace, and Moses threw it up into the air in front of Pharaoh, and it became a fine dust over all the land, and painful boils broke out on people and animals. Even the magicians could not stand before Moses because of them.

Then came the hail.

Before it came, Moses gave a warning. Bring your servants and your animals in from the fields, he said, because tomorrow a great hailstorm is coming. And here is something worth noticing. Some of Pharaoh's own servants listened. They feared the word of the Lord, the scripture says, and they hurried their servants and their cattle into shelter. Others paid no attention, and left them out in the fields.

Moses stretched out his rod toward the heavens, and the Lord sent thunder and hail; and lightning flashed down to the earth. The Lord rained hail on the land of Egypt. So there was very severe hail, and lightning mixed with the hail, such as had not been in all the land of Egypt since it became a nation.

Egypt is a land of very little rain. A storm like this would have been terrifying. The thunder rolling across the flat land, the white hail hammering down on the fields, flattening the flax and the barley, stripping the trees. Only in the land of Goshen, where the children of Israel were, there was no hail.

For the first time, Pharaoh said the words. I have sinned this time, he told Moses. The Lord is righteous, and I and my people are wicked. Pray for us, and I will let you go. So Moses went out of the city and spread out his hands toward the sky, and the thunder and hail stopped, and the rain no longer poured on the earth. But when Pharaoh saw that the storm was over, he hardened his heart once more.

Then Moses warned of locusts.

By now even Pharaoh's own officials were pleading with him. How long will this man be a snare to us, they said. Let the men go. Don't you know that Egypt is ruined? So Pharaoh called Moses back, and began to bargain. Who exactly will go, he asked. Everyone, said Moses. Young and old, sons and daughters, flocks and herds. No, said Pharaoh, only the men may go. And he had Moses and Aaron driven out of his presence.

So Moses stretched out his staff, and the Lord brought an east wind, all day and all night, and in the morning the wind had brought the locusts. They came in a great cloud, so thick that they covered the face of the ground and darkened the land, and they ate every green thing that the hail had left. Not a green leaf remained on any tree or plant in all Egypt.

Again Pharaoh called Moses in a hurry, and said he had sinned, and asked him to pray. And the Lord turned the wind into a strong west wind, which lifted the locusts and carried them into the sea, until not one was left. But still Pharaoh would not let the people go.

Then came the ninth sign, the strangest and perhaps the quietest of them all.

Moses stretched out his hand toward the sky, and there was a thick darkness in all the land of Egypt for three days. They didn't see one another, and nobody rose from his place for three days; but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings.

A darkness which may be felt, the scripture calls it. Let us imagine it, very gently. Three days of deep black, as if the sun itself had gone out. People sitting still in their houses, unable to see their own hands, waiting. The great temples silent. The sun, which the Egyptians honoured as one of their greatest gods, simply gone.

And yet, in Goshen, in the small brick houses of the Hebrew families, there was light. The children could see their mothers' faces. Bread could be baked. Lamps glowed in the windows.

Pharaoh called Moses one last time. You may go, he said, even your children. But leave your flocks and herds behind. Moses said no, not a hoof would be left behind. And Pharaoh, in a fury, told him, Get away from me. Make sure you never see my face again, for on the day you see my face, you will die. Moses answered him quietly. You have spoken well. I will see your face again no more.

Let us leave the story there, in that long darkness. Egypt lies silent and still, wrapped in shadow. And in Goshen, a scattering of small lights glows warm in the windows, like stars that have come down to rest among the houses, while the people inside wait, and wonder what will come next.
