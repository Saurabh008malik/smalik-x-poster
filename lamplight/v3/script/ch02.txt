## Chapter 2: A Basket Among the Reeds

Let us go now to that small house in Goshen, where a mother and father were waiting for a child.

The scripture tells it very simply. A man of the tribe of Levi married a woman of the same tribe. Later in the story we learn their names. The father was called Amram, and the mother Jochebed. They already had a daughter, Miriam, and a little son, Aaron, who was about three years old. And now, in the worst of times, with Pharaoh's cruel order hanging over every Hebrew home, Jochebed gave birth to another boy.

Here are the old words.

The woman conceived and bore a son. When she saw that he was a fine child, she hid him three months.

Let us imagine those three months. A baby is not easy to hide. Babies cry in the night. They need feeding and washing and rocking. Every time footsteps passed in the lane, or an Egyptian voice was heard nearby, his mother must have held her breath and held him close. She hushed him, and sang to him very softly, and kept him in the back room, in the shadows. For three whole months she kept him safe.

But babies grow. His cries grew louder, and stronger. And at last she knew she could not hide him any longer.

So she made a plan. It was a plan of great love, and great faith, and perhaps of desperation too.

When she could no longer hide him, she took a papyrus basket for him, and coated it with tar and with pitch. She put the child in it, and laid it in the reeds by the river's bank. His sister stood far off, to see what would be done to him.

The basket was woven from papyrus, the tall green reed that grew along the Nile, the same reed the Egyptians used to make paper. She sealed it with tar and pitch, so that no water could come in. It became a tiny boat, a little ark. And it is worth noticing that the Hebrew word used here for the basket is the very same word used for Noah's ark, long before. Once again, a small vessel would carry a life safely through the water.

Let us picture that morning by the river. The early light on the water. Tall reeds swaying gently, higher than a person's head, rustling in the breeze. Herons standing still in the shallows. And a mother, kneeling at the water's edge, setting down a small basket among the reeds, so carefully, and then standing, and walking away without looking back, because if she looked back, she might not be able to leave.

But she did not leave him entirely alone. His big sister, Miriam, stayed behind, hidden at a distance, watching.

And then someone came down to the river.

Pharaoh's daughter came down to bathe at the river. Her maidens walked along by the riverside. She saw the basket among the reeds, and sent her servant to get it. She opened it, and saw the child, and behold, the baby cried. She had compassion on him, and said, This is one of the Hebrews' children.

Of all the people who might have found him, it was the daughter of the very king who had ordered the Hebrew boys to be drowned. She knew at once what this baby was, and what her father's law said. But when she saw him crying, her heart was moved. She had compassion on him.

And now Miriam, the watching sister, did something very brave and very quick-witted. She stepped out from the reeds and came to the princess.

Then his sister said to Pharaoh's daughter, Should I go and call a nurse for you from the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for you? Pharaoh's daughter said to her, Go. The young woman went and called the child's mother. Pharaoh's daughter said to her, Take this child away, and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages. The woman took the child, and nursed him.

It is one of the sweetest moments in all of scripture. The mother who had laid her baby in the river, expecting perhaps never to hold him again, was given him back. And not only given him back, but paid to care for him, under the protection of Pharaoh's own daughter.

Let us imagine Jochebed walking home that evening, with her baby in her arms, and Miriam running ahead, unable to keep the joy from her face. The same lanes, the same brick houses, the same river glinting through the palms. But everything was different now.

The child grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. She named him Moses, and said, Because I drew him out of the water.

His name, Moses, sounds like a Hebrew word meaning to draw out. It is also close to an Egyptian word meaning son, which appears in the names of some of the pharaohs. So his very name holds both of his worlds together. A Hebrew child, drawn out of the river, raised as the son of an Egyptian princess.

We do not know much about his years in the palace. Much later, in the New Testament, it is said that Moses was educated in all the learning of the Egyptians. We may imagine him in the great halls and gardens, learning to read and write, studying the stars and the law and the old wisdom of Egypt, wearing fine linen, eating at a royal table. And yet, somewhere deep inside, he never forgot whose son he really was.

Let us leave him there for tonight, a small boy asleep in a palace bedroom, with the curtains moving softly in the warm night air, and far below, the great river flowing on in the dark, past the reeds where his little basket once floated, quietly, toward the sea.
