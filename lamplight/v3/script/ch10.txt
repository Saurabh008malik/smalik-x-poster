## Chapter 10: Water from the Rock, and a Wise Father-in-Law

Let us walk on with the people, deeper into the wilderness, toward the mountain of God.

They broke camp and travelled on, stage by stage, following the cloud. And they came to a place called Rephidim. It was a dry and stony place, and once again, there was no water.

By now the people were weary of the desert. They gathered around Moses, angry and frightened. Give us water to drink, they said. Why did you bring us up out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our animals with thirst? It grew so heated that Moses cried out to God, What shall I do with these people? They are almost ready to stone me.

And God told him to walk on ahead of the people, with some of the elders, and to take in his hand the same staff with which he had struck the river Nile. There, at the rock in Horeb, God said, I will stand before you. Strike the rock, and water will come out of it.

So Moses lifted the old shepherd's staff, and struck the rock, in the sight of the elders. And out of the hard, dry stone, water came flowing, cool and clear, running down over the rocks and across the sand, enough for all the people and all their flocks.

Let us imagine the sound of it, after the long silence of the desert. The splash of water over stone. People hurrying with their jars and skins. Children cupping their hands and drinking. Sheep crowding forward. And Moses, standing quietly beside the rock, an old man leaning on his staff, watching them drink.

He named the place Massah and Meribah, which mean testing and quarrelling, because there the people had asked, Is the Lord among us, or not?

Not long after, at Rephidim, a desert people called the Amalekites came out and attacked Israel. Moses sent a young man named Joshua, who would one day lead the people himself, to gather fighters. And Moses climbed a hill nearby, with Aaron and a man named Hur, and stood there holding up the staff of God.

As long as Moses held up his hands, Israel prevailed. But when his arms grew tired and he let them fall, Amalek prevailed. And Moses was an old man, and his arms grew heavy.

But Moses' hands were heavy; so they took a stone, and put it under him, and he sat on it. Aaron and Hur held up his hands, the one on the one side, and the other on the other side. His hands were steady until sunset.

It is a gentle, quiet picture inside a hard day. An old man sitting on a stone at the top of a hill, too tired to keep his arms up on his own, and his brother and his friend standing on either side of him, holding his arms up for him, all through the long afternoon, until the sun went down. None of us can carry everything alone. Sometimes we need others to hold up our arms.

When the long day was over, Moses built an altar of stones on that spot, and gave it a name.

Moses built an altar, and called its name, The Lord our Banner.

A banner was a flag or a standard, lifted high on a pole, so that people in a crowd or a battle could see it and gather around it. Moses had held up his staff on the hill all day like a banner. But the altar said that the true banner, the one the people gathered under, was not a staff, and not a man. It was the Lord.

Let us imagine that small altar of rough stones, standing alone on the hilltop in the evening, the wind moving over it, the sun going down behind the hills of the desert, and below, the camp settling for the night.

Then, one day, visitors came to the camp. It was Jethro, the old priest of Midian, Moses' father-in-law, who had heard about everything that God had done. And with him came Zipporah, Moses' wife, and his two sons, who had been staying with Jethro.

Moses went out to meet his father-in-law, and bowed and kissed him. They asked each other of their welfare, and they came into the tent.

Let us picture that evening in the tent. The two old men sitting together on the rugs, with cups of water and a little bread between them, and the lamp burning. Moses telling the whole story, from the beginning. The burning bush, Pharaoh, the plagues, the night of the Passover, the sea, the hardships of the road, and how God had saved them. And Jethro listening, and rejoicing, and blessing God for all the good he had done.

The next day, Jethro watched Moses at work. From morning until evening, Moses sat to judge the people. Anyone with a quarrel, or a question, or a problem, came and stood in line to bring it to him. And the line went on all day.

And Jethro, wise and kind, said something that many a tired leader has needed to hear.

The thing that you do is not good. You will surely wear away, both you, and this people that is with you; for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to perform it yourself alone.

He gave Moses some practical advice. Choose capable people, he said, people who are honest and fair, who fear God and hate bribes. Put them in charge of groups of thousands, and hundreds, and fifties, and tens. Let them decide the small matters, and bring only the hardest ones to you. So shall it be easier for you, and they shall share the load with you.

So Moses listened to the voice of his father-in-law, and did all that he had said.

There is something lovely about this little scene. The great prophet, who had spoken with God at the burning bush and stretched out his hand over the sea, was still willing to listen to the plain good sense of an old man from the desert. And it made his burden lighter.

Then Jethro said goodbye, and went home to his own country.

Let us leave the camp there, on a quiet evening at Rephidim. The water from the rock is still running softly over the stones. The tents are pitched in long rows. In one of them, an old man sleeps more easily than he has in many months, because he no longer carries everything alone.
