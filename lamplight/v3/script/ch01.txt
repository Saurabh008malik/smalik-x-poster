## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a People in Egypt

Good evening, and welcome. Settle in. Let your head sink into the pillow, and let your shoulders go soft. The day is finished now, and whatever it held, you can set it down for a while.

This is Lamplight Scripture. Tonight we are going to walk, slowly, through one of the greatest stories in the whole Bible. It is the story of the Exodus. A baby in a basket among the reeds. A shepherd and a bush that burned but was not consumed. A people who walked out of slavery, through the middle of the sea, and into a wide and quiet wilderness, where they learned, day by day, to trust. It fills the book of Exodus, the second book of the Bible, and it has been told around fires and tables for thousands of years.

I will tell it in plain words, gently, the way you might tell it to someone drifting off beside you. And now and then I will read the old words themselves, from a public domain translation called the World English Bible, so you can hear the scripture in its own voice. Where the old text uses the holy name of God, I will say the Lord, as many readers do. You do not have to follow every step. If you fall asleep somewhere in the desert, the story will keep walking, and it will be here whenever you come back.

Let us begin where the last great story ended, in the land of Egypt.

You may remember Joseph, the dreamer with the coat of many colors, who was sold by his brothers and rose to stand beside Pharaoh. In a time of famine, Joseph brought his old father, Jacob, and all his brothers and their families down into Egypt, and they settled in a green and fertile region in the north, near the mouths of the Nile, called the land of Goshen. There were seventy of them when they came. They were shepherds, and they were given good pasture, and for a long time they lived there in peace.

Here are the old words.

Joseph died, as did all his brothers, and all that generation. The children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and grew exceedingly mighty; and the land was filled with them.

Let us picture Goshen for a moment. Flat green fields beside slow channels of water. Flocks of sheep and goats moving across the pasture in the evening. Low houses of sun-dried brick. Palm trees, and the wide pale sky of Egypt, and the great river nearby, bringing its floods every year and leaving behind dark, rich soil. Generation after generation, the families of Israel grew there. Children were born, and grew up, and had children of their own. And the memory of Joseph grew faint.

Then the story turns.

Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who didn't know Joseph. He said to his people, Behold, the people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we. Come, let's deal wisely with them, lest they multiply, and it happen that when any war breaks out, they also join themselves to our enemies and fight against us, and escape out of the land.

So the new Pharaoh grew afraid. He looked at these foreign shepherds living in his land, so many of them now, and he did not see neighbours. He saw a danger. And fear, as it so often does, turned hard and cruel.

Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens. They built storage cities for Pharaoh: Pithom and Raamses. But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and the more they spread out.

The people of Israel became slaves. They were made to dig clay from the riverbanks, and mix it with water and straw, and shape it into bricks, and dry the bricks in the sun, and carry them, and build. Day after day, under the hot sun, with overseers standing over them. They built whole cities of storehouses for Pharaoh, to hold his grain. The scripture says the Egyptians made their lives bitter, with hard service in mortar and in brick, and in all kinds of service in the field.

And still, the more they were pressed down, the more they grew. It is one of the quiet themes of the whole book. Something in this people would not be crushed.

So Pharaoh tried something darker. He called for two women, two Hebrew midwives, the women who helped mothers bring their babies into the world. The Bible remembers their names, and that alone is worth noticing, because it does not remember the name of the Pharaoh. The midwives were called Shiphrah and Puah.

Pharaoh gave them a terrible order. If a baby boy was born to a Hebrew mother, they were to let him die. Only the baby girls were to live.

Here is what they did.

But the midwives feared God, and didn't do what the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the baby boys alive.

When Pharaoh called them back, angry, and asked why they had disobeyed him, they gave him a clever answer. The Hebrew women, they said, are not like the Egyptian women. They are strong, and their babies come before the midwife even arrives. It was a quiet, brave act of resistance by two ordinary women, and the scripture says that God dealt well with them, and gave them families of their own.

Let us rest with them for a moment. Two women, walking home in the evening, through the narrow lanes between the brick houses, carrying their baskets of cloths and oils, having saved another child that day. They had no army and no power. They had only courage, and kindness, and they used them.

But Pharaoh did not give up. At last he gave an order to all his people. Every baby boy born to the Hebrews was to be thrown into the river Nile.

It was a dark time to be born. And yet, in that dark time, in a small house in Goshen, a mother was about to have a child, and the whole story of a people was about to change.

Let us leave it there for now, in the warm Egyptian night, with the river flowing quietly in the darkness, the frogs singing in the reeds, and in one small house, a lamp burning, and a mother waiting.
