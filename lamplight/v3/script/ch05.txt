## Chapter 5: Let My People Go

Let us return now to Egypt, where Moses and Aaron have come home after so many years.

First, they went to their own people. They gathered the elders of Israel, the old men who led the families, and Aaron told them everything that God had said to Moses at the burning bush, and Moses showed them the signs. The old words say that the people believed. And when they heard that the Lord had seen their suffering, they bowed their heads and worshipped.

Let us imagine that gathering. A crowded room in Goshen in the evening, lit by small clay lamps. Tired faces, lined with years of hard work in the sun. And then a new feeling moving through the room, something many of them had almost forgotten. Hope.

Then the two brothers went to the palace.

The scripture tells us that Moses was eighty years old, and Aaron eighty-three, when they stood before Pharaoh. Two old men from the desert, in plain clothes, standing in the great hall of the most powerful king in the world, among painted columns and golden thrones and rows of courtiers and guards.

Afterward Moses and Aaron came, and said to Pharaoh, This is what the Lord, the God of Israel, says, Let my people go, that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness. Pharaoh said, Who is the Lord, that I should listen to his voice to let Israel go? I don't know the Lord, and moreover I will not let Israel go.

Let my people go. Four simple words, which have echoed down the centuries, sung by enslaved people and by all who have longed for freedom. And Pharaoh's answer was just as simple. Who is the Lord? I don't know him. And I will not.

And then Pharaoh made things worse. He was angry that the Hebrews were dreaming of freedom, and he decided to crush the dream with work. Until now, the slaves had been given straw to mix into their bricks. Now, Pharaoh said, they would have to go out and gather the straw themselves, and still make just as many bricks as before.

So the people scattered across the fields, gathering stubble left after the harvest, working longer and longer hours, and still they could not make the full number of bricks. Their own Hebrew foremen were beaten for it. And when those foremen came out from Pharaoh's court and met Moses and Aaron in the road, they were bitter. You have made things worse, they said. You have put a sword in their hand to kill us.

It was a very low moment. Moses went back to God, and he spoke honestly, the way the Bible's great figures often do. Lord, why have you brought trouble on this people? Why is it that you have sent me? You have not rescued your people at all!

And God answered with a promise. Now you shall see what I will do. And he spoke to Moses words full of tenderness and strength.

I am the Lord, and I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, and I will rid you out of their bondage, and I will redeem you with an outstretched arm, and with great judgments. I will take you to myself for a people. I will be your God.

But when Moses told this to the people, the scripture says they could not listen, because their spirits were crushed, and their work was so cruel. Sometimes people are simply too tired to hope. And that, too, is part of the story.

So the long contest began, between Moses and Pharaoh.

Moses and Aaron went in again before the king. Aaron threw down his staff, and it became a serpent. Pharaoh called his wise men and his magicians, and they did the same with their own rods, but Aaron's staff swallowed up theirs. Still Pharaoh would not listen.

Then came the wonders that we call the plagues of Egypt. Ten of them, one after another, over many weeks or months.

The first touched the great river itself. In the sight of Pharaoh, Aaron stretched out his staff over the Nile, and the water turned to blood. The fish died, the river grew foul, and the Egyptians had to dig along the riverbanks for water to drink. The river was the heart of Egypt, its life and its pride, and some of the Egyptians even worshipped it. Now it had become a place of death.

Seven days passed. Then came the frogs. Out of the river they came, in their thousands upon thousands, hopping into the houses, into the bedrooms, onto the beds, into the ovens and the bread troughs. Pharaoh begged Moses to take them away, and promised to let the people go. Moses prayed, and the frogs died, and the people gathered them up into great heaps. But when Pharaoh saw that there was relief, he hardened his heart again.

Then Aaron struck the dust of the ground, and it became tiny biting insects, gnats or lice, all over the land, on people and animals alike. This time the magicians could not copy the sign. They went to Pharaoh, and they said, This is God's finger. But still Pharaoh would not listen.

The old story keeps returning to one phrase. Pharaoh's heart was hardened. Sometimes it says that Pharaoh hardened his own heart. Sometimes it says that God hardened it. Readers and teachers have puzzled over that for thousands of years. But one thing is clear. Each time Pharaoh said no, it became a little easier for him to say no again, and a little harder to turn back.

Let us leave the story there for now, on a quiet night in Goshen, where the people of Israel lie on their mats, listening. Outside, the night is still. Somewhere far off, beyond the fields, a great palace stands dark against the stars, and inside it a proud king lies awake, and will not bend.
