## Chapter 7: A Long Road East, and the Palace at Susa

Now we must make a long journey, through both distance and time.

Ruth's great-grandson David became king, and built Jerusalem into his royal city. His son Solomon built the temple there. Then came many generations of kings, some good and some not, and the kingdom divided in two, and great empires rose in the east. The Assyrians came, and then the Babylonians. And at last, as we heard in an earlier story on this channel, Jerusalem was taken, and many of its people were carried away to Babylon, by the rivers, where they hung up their harps on the willow trees.

Then Babylon itself fell, to the Persians. And the Persian king Cyrus allowed the exiles to go home, if they wished, and rebuild their temple. Some went home. But many did not. They had been born in the east by then. They had houses, and families, and work. And so, in the cities of the Persian Empire, there were Jewish families living among many other peoples, keeping their own customs as well as they could, far from the hills of Judah.

Our second story takes place among them, perhaps six hundred years or more after the days of Ruth.

The Persian Empire was the largest the world had ever seen. It stretched from the borders of India in the east to the lands of Ethiopia in the south and west, and from the Aegean Sea to the mountains of central Asia. The king had several royal cities, and he moved between them with the seasons. One of the greatest was Susa, in the lowlands of what is now southwestern Iran. The old Bible calls it Shushan. On a high mound above the city stood the citadel, with its great audience hall, a forest of tall stone columns topped with carved bulls, and its palace courts, and its gardens.

The king in our story is called Ahasuerus. Many historians think this is the Hebrew form of the name of the king the Greeks called Xerxes, who ruled Persia about four hundred and eighty years before Christ. The book itself does not stop to tell us, and we need not be too certain. We will simply call him the king, or Ahasuerus, as the old text does.

It is also worth knowing, before we begin, that the book of Esther is unusual in one gentle way. In its Hebrew form, it never once mentions the name of God. There are no prophets in it, no visions, no voices from heaven. And yet, as many readers have noticed over the centuries, it is full of quiet coincidences and turnings, of things that happen at just the right moment. Like the book of Ruth, it seems to show God working out of sight, behind the curtain, through ordinary people and their choices.

Let us begin with the opening of the book, and a great royal feast.

{{EST 1:1-9}}

What a scene. For a hundred and eighty days, half a year, the king displays the riches of his kingdom to his princes and governors and the commanders of his armies. And then, at the end, a feast of seven days for all the people in the citadel, in the court of the palace garden. Picture the hangings of white and blue linen, fastened with cords of fine linen and purple to silver rings and marble pillars. Couches of gold and silver on a pavement of colored stone, red and white and shining. Wine served in golden cups, no two the same, as much as anyone wished.

And at the same time, in another part of the palace, Queen Vashti gives a feast for the women.

{{EST 1:10-12}}

On the seventh day of the feast, when the king's heart is merry with wine, he sends his chamberlains to bring Queen Vashti before him, wearing her royal crown, to show her beauty to the people and the princes. And Vashti refuses to come.

The book does not tell us why. Perhaps she would not be put on display before a hall full of men who had been drinking for a week. Many readers over the years have admired her quiet dignity. But the king is very angry, and his anger burns in him.

{{EST 1:13-22}}

The king turns to his wise men, the seven princes of Persia and Media who sit closest to him, and asks what should be done according to the law. And one of them, named Memucan, gives a long, anxious answer. If the queen gets away with this, he says, every wife in the kingdom will hear of it, and they will all refuse their husbands. So let a royal command go out, that Vashti will never again come before the king, and let her royal position be given to someone else. And let letters be sent to every province, in its own script, and to every people in its own language, that every man should be master in his own house.

There is a gentle humor in the way the old storyteller tells this. The greatest king in the world, ruling a hundred and twenty-seven provinces, cannot persuade his own wife to come to dinner. And his whole council of wise men responds by sending out letters, in every language of the empire, by the royal couriers.

But behind the humor, the story has quietly set something in motion. There is no queen in the palace at Susa now. And somewhere in the city below the citadel, a young woman is living quietly in the house of her cousin, with no idea of what is coming.
