## Chapter 12: The Queen Speaks, a Day of Rest, and Goodnight

{{EST 7:1-6}}

So the king and Haman came to the second banquet with Queen Esther. And again, as they drank wine, the king asked her, What is your petition, Queen Esther? It shall be granted you. What is your request? Even to half of the kingdom it shall be done.

And now, at last, Esther speaks.

If I have found favor in your sight, O king, she says, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request. For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed.

Notice how she says it. She does not say, the Jews. She says, I and my people. For the first time, the queen tells the king who she is. The secret she kept for so many years, she now gives up, because her people need her.

And the king, astonished, asks, Who is he, and where is he, who dared presume in his heart to do so?

And Esther says, An adversary and an enemy, even this wicked Haman.

{{EST 7:7-10}}

The king rises in anger from the banquet and goes out into the palace garden. And when he returns, the matter is quickly settled. One of the chamberlains, Harbonah, mentions the tall gallows that Haman had made for Mordecai, standing beside Haman's own house. And the king commands that Haman be hanged on it. And then, the old story says, the king's wrath was pacified.

It is a stern ending for Haman, and the book tells it briefly and without delight. The trap he set was the one he fell into. And we can let that part of the story go by quietly, like a dark cloud passing over the moon.

But the danger is not yet over. The king's decree has gone out to every province, sealed with the king's ring, and the old law of the Persians and Medes says that such a decree cannot be revoked.

{{EST 8:3-8}}

So Esther goes in to the king once more, uninvited, and falls at his feet, and weeps, and begs him to put away the evil plan that Haman had devised against the Jews. And again the king holds out the golden scepter, and she rises and stands before him. How can I bear to see the evil that would come to my people, she says. How can I bear to see the destruction of my relatives?

The king cannot cancel his first decree. But he can send a second one. He gives Haman's house to Esther, and his own signet ring to Mordecai. Write as you please, he tells them, in the king's name, and seal it with the king's ring.

So a new decree goes out, in every script and every language, by couriers on swift royal horses. It gives the Jews in every city the right to gather together and defend their lives on the day that had been chosen against them. And when it is proclaimed, the book says, the Jews had light, and gladness, and joy, and honor.

{{EST 8:15-17}}

Mordecai goes out from the presence of the king in royal clothing of blue and white, with a great crown of gold, and a robe of fine linen and purple. And the city of Susa shouted and was glad.

Do you remember how the city was perplexed, on the day the first decree went out? Now the same city is shouting for joy. Everything has been turned around.

When the thirteenth day of Adar came, the day the lot had chosen, the book records that the Jews gathered in their cities and defended themselves, and that those who had planned to destroy them were overcome. It is a hard chapter, and an old one, from a harsh and distant world, and we will not linger over it tonight. What the book itself chooses to remember most is what came after. Rest.

{{EST 9:20-22}}

On the fourteenth and fifteenth days of the month, the Jews rested, and made it a day of feasting and gladness. And Mordecai wrote letters to all the Jews in all the provinces, near and far, to keep those days every year, as the days when they had rest from their enemies, and the month that was turned for them from sorrow to gladness, and from mourning into a good day. Days of feasting and joy, of sending food to one another, and gifts to the poor.

Because of the pur, the lot that Haman had cast, the days were called Purim. And to this day, every year, in late winter or early spring, Jewish families around the world celebrate Purim. They read the whole book of Esther aloud, and they share food with friends, and give gifts to those in need, just as Mordecai's letter asked, many centuries ago.

{{EST 10:1-3}}

And the book ends with a quiet picture of Mordecai, second only to the king, great among his people and well loved, seeking the good of his people, and speaking peace to all his descendants.

And so our two stories come to an end.

Two women. One was a poor foreigner gleaning at the edge of a barley field. The other was a queen in a palace of marble and gold. One walked into Bethlehem at the beginning of the harvest, beside a grieving widow. The other walked into a throne room, not knowing whether she would walk out again. Neither of them ever heard a voice from heaven, or saw a vision. They simply chose, at the moment it mattered, to be loyal, and brave, and kind. Where you go, I will go. If I perish, I perish.

And in both stories, the old storytellers suggest, there was a hand moving quietly behind the curtain. A young widow who happened to come to the right field. A king who happened not to sleep, on the one night when it mattered most.

Perhaps that is something to take with you into the dark tonight. That small, faithful choices matter. That the story is not finished yet. And that even on the nights when you cannot sleep, you are not forgotten.

Now let the stories go. Let the barley fields of Bethlehem fade into the evening haze. Let the lamps of Susa go out, one by one, across the citadel. Let the great columns stand silent under the stars. Let your breathing slow, and your shoulders soften, and your hands lie still.

You have done enough today. There is nothing more to carry. You can rest now.

And let me leave you with the blessing that Boaz spoke over Ruth, on that first morning in the barley field.

{{RUT 2:12}}

Goodnight. Sleep well.
