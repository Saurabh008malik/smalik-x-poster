## Chapter 3: Gleaning in the Barley Field

Barley was the first grain to ripen in the hills of Judah. It came in the spring, a few weeks before the wheat. When it was ready, the whole town went out into the fields. The reapers moved along the rows with curved sickles, gathering the stalks in one hand and cutting them with the other. Behind them came others, binding the cut stalks into sheaves, and the sheaves were carried off to the threshing floor. It was hard work, under a warm sun, from early morning until evening.

And behind the reapers, at the edges of the fields, came the gleaners.

The law of Israel had a gentle rule about harvest. When you reap your field, it said, do not reap all the way to the very edges, and do not go back to pick up what you dropped. Leave it, the law said, for the poor, and for the foreigner, and for the orphan, and for the widow. So the poorest people of the town could follow the reapers, and gather the stalks that were left behind, and in that way they would not go hungry.

Ruth was a foreigner, and a widow, and poor. And so, on one of those first mornings of harvest, she went to Naomi and asked if she might go out and glean.

{{RUT 2:1-7}}

Listen to that phrase. She happened to come to the portion of the field belonging to Boaz. The old story says it almost with a smile. It was just chance, it seems to say, just where her feet happened to take her. And yet the reader already knows that Boaz is a relative of Elimelech, a man of standing in Bethlehem. Nothing in this story is announced with trumpets. Everything simply happens, quietly, the way the morning happens.

And then Boaz himself arrives from town, and he greets his workers in the field. The Lord be with you, he says. And they answer, The Lord bless you. That is how this man greets the people who work for him. It tells us a good deal about him before he has done anything else.

He notices the young woman at the edge of the field, and asks who she is. And the servant in charge of the reapers tells him. She is the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi. She asked to glean, and she has been working steadily since early morning, with only a little rest in the shelter.

Then Boaz goes over and speaks to her.

{{RUT 2:8-13}}

Boaz has heard the whole story already. In a small town, everyone has. He has heard how Ruth left her father and her mother and the land where she was born, and came to a people she did not know. And he blesses her for it, with some lovely words. May the Lord repay your work, he says, and may a full reward be given to you from the Lord, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge.

Under whose wings. It is an image of a bird, sheltering its young beneath its outstretched feathers. Ruth has come to take shelter under the wings of the God of Israel. Keep that picture in your mind, because it will come back later in the story, in a very quiet way.

{{RUT 2:14-17}}

At the mealtime, Boaz calls her over to eat with the reapers. He gives her bread to dip in the vinegar, which was a kind of sour wine, refreshing in the heat. He hands her roasted grain, more than she can eat, so that some is left over. And when she gets up to go back to gleaning, he quietly tells his young men to let her glean even among the sheaves, and to pull out a few stalks on purpose and drop them for her, and not to embarrass her.

That is a lovely kind of kindness. It does not make a show of itself. It does not make her feel like a beggar. It simply makes sure there is more barley lying on the ground wherever she happens to walk.

So Ruth gleaned in the field until evening. Then she sat down and beat out what she had gathered, knocking the grain loose from the stalks with a stick. And when she had finished, she had about an ephah of barley. That was a great deal for one day of gleaning, enough to feed two women for many days.

Imagine her walking home in the evening light, carrying that heavy bundle of grain. The sun is low over the hills. The fields are full of long shadows. The reapers are going home in twos and threes, tired and talking softly. Swallows are circling over the stubble. And Ruth climbs the path back up to the town, toward the little house where Naomi is waiting.
