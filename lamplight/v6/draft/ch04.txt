## Chapter 4: An Evening Conversation, and a Long Harvest

When Ruth came in through the door that evening, Naomi saw the great bundle of barley, and she must have been astonished. Where did you glean today, she asked. Where did you work? Blessed be the man who took notice of you.

{{RUT 2:18-23}}

Boaz, Naomi says. The man is a close relative of ours. He is one of our near kinsmen.

That word, kinsman, carries something important in the old Hebrew. It is the word goel, which is often translated redeemer. In ancient Israel, families looked after one another in a particular way. If a man fell into poverty and had to sell his land, his nearest relative had the right, and the duty, to buy it back, so that the land would stay in the family. If a man was sold into service because of debt, his kinsman could buy his freedom. The redeemer was the one who stepped in to set things right for his own family, when they could not set things right for themselves.

And so, when Naomi hears the name of Boaz, something lifts in her heart. For the first time since she came home, she speaks a blessing instead of a lament. May he be blessed by the Lord, she says, who has not abandoned his kindness to the living and to the dead.

The word for kindness there is the Hebrew word hesed. It is one of the great words of the Bible, and it is hard to put into English. It means kindness, but more than kindness. It means loyal love, faithful love, the kind of love that keeps its promises and goes beyond what is required. Ruth has shown hesed to Naomi, by staying with her. Boaz has shown hesed to Ruth, by sheltering her in his field. And Naomi, who said that the Lord had dealt bitterly with her, now begins to see his hesed again, shining through the kindness of ordinary people.

So Ruth kept close to the young women of Boaz's household, and she gleaned in his fields until the end of the barley harvest. And after that came the wheat harvest, and she gleaned through that too. And all that time, she lived with her mother-in-law.

That was a long season, perhaps two months or more. Let us rest in it for a while, the way the story itself seems to rest.

Every morning, very early, before the sun was high, Ruth would walk out of the town and down the terraced path to the fields. The dew would still be on the grass. The air would be cool and smell of earth and growing things. The reapers would be gathering, and the overseer would call out the day's work. And Ruth would take her place behind the young women, and stoop, and gather.

The hours would go by. The sun would climb. The sound of the sickles would go on and on, a soft swishing and cutting along the rows. Sometimes someone would sing a harvest song, and others would join in. At midday they would rest in the shade of the shelter, and eat bread and parched grain, and drink water from the clay jars. Then back into the field.

And in the evening, the long walk home, with the grain on her shoulder, and the sky turning gold and then rose and then deep blue over the hills of Judah.

The barley was gathered, and then the wheat. The fields turned from gold to stubble. Out on the threshing floors, the sheaves were piled high, and the oxen walked round and round over them, treading out the grain. And then came the winnowing.

The threshing floor was a wide, flat place of beaten earth or bare rock, often on a hill outside the town, where the wind could reach it. In the evening, when the breeze rose, the workers would take up the trampled grain and straw with wooden forks, and toss it into the air. The light chaff would blow away on the wind like a pale cloud. The heavy grain would fall back down onto the floor, in golden heaps. They would do it again and again, as the sun went down and the stars came out, until the grain lay clean.

It was the happiest time of the farming year. The work was nearly done. The harvest was safely in. There would be food for the winter. And at night, the owners of the grain would often sleep out on the threshing floor beside their heaps, to guard them, under the open sky.

And it was there, on a night of winnowing, that the next part of our story took place.
