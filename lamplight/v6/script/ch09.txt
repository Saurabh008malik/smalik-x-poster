## Chapter 9: Haman, and the Casting of the Lot

Every story like this one needs a shadow, and now the shadow enters.

After these things King Ahasuerus promoted Haman the son of Hammedatha the Agagite, and advanced him, and set his seat above all the princes who were with him. All the king's servants who were in the king's gate bowed down and paid homage to Haman, for the king had so commanded concerning him. But Mordecai didn't bow down or pay him homage. Then the king's servants who were in the king's gate said to Mordecai, Why do you disobey the king's commandment? Now it came to pass, when they spoke daily to him, and he didn't listen to them, that they told Haman, to see whether Mordecai's reason would stand; for he had told them that he was a Jew. When Haman saw that Mordecai didn't bow down nor pay him homage, Haman was full of wrath. But he scorned the thought of laying hands on Mordecai alone, for they had made known to him Mordecai's people. Therefore Haman sought to destroy all the Jews who were throughout the whole kingdom of Ahasuerus, even Mordecai's people.

The king promotes a man named Haman, the son of Hammedatha the Agagite, and sets his seat above all the princes who are with him. And all the king's servants who sit in the king's gate bow down and pay homage to Haman, because the king has commanded it.

But Mordecai does not bow.

Day after day, the other servants ask him why. Why do you disobey the king's command? And day after day, he does not listen to them. At last they tell Haman, to see whether Mordecai's reason will stand. For Mordecai had told them that he was a Jew.

The book does not tell us exactly why Mordecai refused. Some readers think it was because he would give that kind of honor to God alone. Others notice that Haman is called an Agagite, a name that recalls an old enemy of Israel from the days of King Saul, who was himself from the tribe of Benjamin, Mordecai's own tribe. The old text leaves it unexplained, and so shall we. It is enough to know that Mordecai would not bow, and that Haman noticed.

And when Haman saw that Mordecai would not bow, he was full of fury. But it seemed too small a thing to him to punish Mordecai alone. He had learned who Mordecai's people were. And so, in his pride and his anger, he set his heart on something far greater and far darker, to destroy all the Jews throughout the whole kingdom.

In the first month, which is the month Nisan, in the twelfth year of King Ahasuerus, they cast Pur, that is, the lot, before Haman from day to day, and from month to month, and chose the twelfth month, which is the month Adar. Haman said to King Ahasuerus, There is a certain people scattered abroad and dispersed among the peoples in all the provinces of your kingdom, and their laws are different from other people's. They don't keep the king's laws. Therefore it is not for the king's profit to allow them to remain. If it pleases the king, let it be written that they be destroyed; and I will pay ten thousand talents of silver into the hands of those who are in charge of the king's business, to bring it into the king's treasuries. The king took his ring from his hand, and gave it to Haman the son of Hammedatha the Agagite, the Jews' enemy. The king said to Haman, The silver is given to you, the people also, to do with them as it seems good to you.

In the first month, the month of Nisan, in the twelfth year of the king, they cast pur before Haman. Pur is an old word for a lot, a small stone or die that was thrown to choose a day. They cast it from day to day, and from month to month, to find the right time. And the lot fell on the twelfth month, the month of Adar, almost a whole year away.

Then Haman goes to the king, and speaks very carefully. He does not name the people. He only says, there is a certain people scattered and dispersed among the peoples in all the provinces of your kingdom. Their laws are different from those of other people, and they do not keep the king's laws. It is not for the king's profit to let them be. And he offers to pay ten thousand talents of silver into the king's treasury, an enormous sum.

And the king, without even asking who these people are, takes his signet ring from his hand and gives it to Haman. The silver is yours, he says, and the people too, to do with them as seems good to you.

It is a chilling moment in the story, and the book tells it plainly, without making a show of it. A great king, careless and easily persuaded. A proud man with a grudge. A whole people, scattered across the provinces, who do not yet know anything about it.

Then the king's scribes were called in on the first month, on the thirteenth day of the month; and all that Haman commanded was written to the king's local governors, and to the governors who were over every province, and to the princes of every people, to every province according to its writing, and to every people in their language. It was written in the name of King Ahasuerus, and it was sealed with the king's ring. Letters were sent by couriers into all the king's provinces, to destroy, to kill, and to cause to perish, all Jews, both young and old, little children and women, in one day, even on the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, which is the month Adar, and to plunder their possessions. A copy of the letter, that the decree should be given out in every province, was published to all the peoples, that they should be ready against that day. The couriers went out in haste by the king's commandment, and the decree was given out in the citadel of Susa. The king and Haman sat down to drink; but the city of Susa was perplexed.

The king's scribes are called, and the letters are written, in every script and every language, sealed with the king's ring. And the couriers ride out from Susa along the great royal roads of Persia, carrying the decree to every province. On the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, it says, all the Jews were to be destroyed.

And then the chapter ends with a strange and quiet picture. The king and Haman sat down to drink. But the city of Susa was perplexed.

Even the ordinary people of the city, who were not Jewish, were troubled and confused. They could not understand what was happening, or why. Their neighbors, the families they had lived beside for years, were suddenly under a sentence of death. And in the palace, the two men who had decided it were sitting together with their cups of wine.

The old storyteller does not add any more. He simply lets that picture rest there, in the evening light over Susa, and moves on. We will let it rest too, for a moment, and breathe slowly, before we go on.

The couriers are still riding, far out on the long roads. The dust rises behind their horses and settles again. The sun goes down over the plains, and the lamps are lit in the towns, one by one.
