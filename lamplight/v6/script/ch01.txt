## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Famine in Bethlehem

Good evening, and welcome. Let your body settle into the place where you are lying. Let the pillow take the weight of your head. Let your hands grow loose and warm. Whatever the day asked of you, it has finished asking now. There is nothing more you need to do tonight except listen, or not listen, and rest.

This is Lamplight Scripture. Tonight we are going to hear two stories, each one named after a woman. The first is the story of Ruth, a young widow from the land of Moab, who walked away from everything she knew because she would not leave her mother-in-law alone on the road. It is a story of barley fields and harvest evenings, of a kind landowner, and of a quiet night on a threshing floor. The second is the story of Esther, a young woman who became queen in the palace of the Persian king, far to the east, and who had to decide, one morning, whether to risk her life by walking into a room where she had not been called.

The two stories are separated by many centuries, and by a very long road. One is set in the small hill town of Bethlehem, among farmers and shepherds. The other is set in the great citadel of Susa, among princes and scribes and courtiers. But they belong together in a gentle way. Both are short books. Both are about loyalty, and about courage that does not shout. And in both of them, the hand of God moves quietly, almost out of sight, through ordinary choices made by ordinary people.

I will tell the stories in plain words, slowly. And because both books are short, we will read a great deal of the old text itself, almost all of Ruth, and much of Esther, from a public domain translation called the World English Bible. Where the old text uses the holy name of God, I will say the Lord, as many readers do.

You do not need to follow every name. If you drift away in the barley field, or somewhere in the king's garden, that is perfectly all right. The story will keep walking along without you, and it will be here when you come back.

Let us begin with the first lines of the book of Ruth.

In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land. A certain man of Bethlehem Judah went to live in the country of Moab with his wife and his two sons. The name of the man was Elimelech, and the name of his wife Naomi. The names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem Judah. They came into the country of Moab and lived there. Elimelech, Naomi's husband, died; and she was left with her two sons. They took for themselves wives of the women of Moab. The name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years. Mahlon and Chilion both died, and the woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband.

Those five verses hold a great deal of sorrow, told very simply. Let us go back through them slowly, the way you might walk back along a path to look again at something you passed too quickly.

The book begins in the days when the judges ruled. That was a long, unsettled time in the story of Israel, after the people had come into the land, and before there were any kings. There was no capital city, no palace, no royal court. The tribes lived in their own hill country and valleys, each family on its own small fields. When trouble came, a leader would rise up for a while, a judge, and then the land would go back to its ordinary life. The book of Judges, which comes just before Ruth in our Bibles, tells many stories from those years, and many of them are stormy and troubled. The book of Ruth is different. It is a small, quiet story from the same years, as if, in the middle of a noisy house, someone had opened a door onto a peaceful room.

The town at the center of the story is Bethlehem. In Hebrew the name means house of bread. It sat on a ridge in the hill country of Judah, a few miles south of Jerusalem, which in those days was not yet the royal city of Israel. Around it were terraced slopes, and narrow valleys, and fields of barley and wheat. In the spring the fields turned green, and then gold, and the whole town went out to bring in the harvest. It was a farming town, where everyone knew everyone else, and news traveled from the well to the gate before you could finish your bread.

But in the days of our story, the house of bread had no bread. There was a famine in the land. Perhaps the rains had failed. Perhaps the harvest had failed more than once. The old story does not say why. It only says that a man of Bethlehem, named Elimelech, took his wife and his two sons and went to live for a while in the country of Moab.

Moab lay to the east, across the deep valley of the Jordan, and beyond the Dead Sea. From the hills near Bethlehem, on a clear day, you could see its high, flat tableland on the far side of the water, blue and hazy in the distance. It was not very far, perhaps a few days' walk. But it was another country, with another people and other gods, and between Israel and Moab there was an old, uneasy history. To go and live there was not a small thing. It was the kind of choice a family makes only when there seems to be no other way.

Elimelech's name means my God is king. His wife was Naomi, and her name means pleasant, or sweet. Their two sons were named Mahlon and Chilion. They were Ephrathites, which is an old name for the people of Bethlehem. They went down into Moab, and they stayed.

And then the sorrows came, one after another. Elimelech died, and Naomi was left a widow in a foreign land, with her two sons. The sons grew up and married women of Moab. One was named Orpah, and the other was named Ruth. The family lived there for about ten years. And then both sons died as well. And Naomi was left alone, without her husband, and without her children, in a country that was not her own.

That is where the story really begins. Not with a hero, and not with a victory, but with an older woman sitting in an empty house, far from home, with two young widows beside her.

Let us stay with her for a moment, in that quiet house in Moab. The evening is coming. Somewhere outside, someone is drawing water. The light is fading over the tableland, and to the west, across the deep valley, the hills of home are turning dark against the sky.
