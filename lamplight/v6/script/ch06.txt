## Chapter 6: At the Town Gate, and a Child Named Obed

In the old towns of Israel, the gate was the heart of public life. It was not just a door in the wall. It was a deep passage, with stone benches along the sides, and an open space just inside, where the elders of the town would sit. Farmers passed through it on their way to the fields in the morning, and on their way home in the evening. Merchants came and went. And when there was a matter to settle, a sale of land, a dispute, a marriage, it was settled there, in the gate, before witnesses, where everyone could see.

That morning, Boaz went up to the gate and sat down. And just as he had hoped, the nearer kinsman came walking by.

Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there. Behold, the near kinsman of whom Boaz spoke came by. Boaz said to him, Come over here, friend, and sit down! He came over, and sat down. Boaz took ten men of the elders of the city, and said, Sit down here, and they sat down. He said to the near kinsman, Naomi, who has come back out of the country of Moab, is selling the parcel of land, which was our brother Elimelech's. I thought I should tell you, saying, Buy it before those who sit here, and before the elders of my people. If you will redeem it, redeem it; but if you will not redeem it, then tell me, that I may know. For there is no one to redeem it besides you; and I am after you. He said, I will redeem it. Then Boaz said, On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must buy it also from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance. The near kinsman said, I can't redeem it for myself, lest I endanger my own inheritance. Take my right of redemption for yourself; for I can't redeem it.

The old text does not give us this man's name. Boaz calls him by a Hebrew phrase that means something like so-and-so, or my friend. Perhaps the storyteller left out his name on purpose, because he did not take his place in the story.

Boaz gathers ten of the elders as witnesses. Then he lays out the matter. Naomi is selling the piece of land that belonged to Elimelech. You have the first right to buy it back, and keep it in the family. If you will redeem it, redeem it. If not, tell me, because I am next after you.

And the man says, I will redeem it. A field is a good thing to have.

Then Boaz adds the rest. On the day you buy the field from Naomi, you also take on Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of the dead man, to raise up his name on his inheritance. Any son she has would carry the name of Elimelech's family, and the land would go to him.

And now the man hesitates. That would be a different matter. It would cost him the field, in a sense, and it might complicate his own family's inheritance. I can't redeem it, he says, or I would endanger my own inheritance. You take my right of redemption. I cannot do it.

He is not a villain. He simply makes the careful choice, the safe choice, the way Orpah made the sensible choice on the road from Moab. And the book quietly lets him walk away, still nameless.

Now this was the custom in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning exchanging, to confirm all things: a man took off his sandal, and gave it to his neighbor; and this was the way of formalizing transactions in Israel. So the near kinsman said to Boaz, Buy it for yourself, then he took off his sandal. Boaz said to the elders and to all the people, You are witnesses today, that I have bought all that was Elimelech's, and all that was Chilion's and Mahlon's, from the hand of Naomi. Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of Mahlon, I have purchased to be my wife, to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brothers and from the gate of his place. You are witnesses today. All the people who were in the gate, and the elders, said, We are witnesses. May the Lord make the woman who has come into your house like Rachel and like Leah, which both built the house of Israel; and treat you worthily in Ephrathah, and be famous in Bethlehem. Let your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, of the offspring which the Lord will give you by this young woman.

In those days, the story explains, the custom for confirming such an agreement was for one man to pull off his sandal and give it to the other. It was a little like signing your name, or shaking hands. So the nearer kinsman pulls off his sandal and hands it to Boaz.

And Boaz turns to the elders and to all the people standing in the gate, and declares that he has bought everything that belonged to Elimelech and his sons, and that he has taken Ruth the Moabitess to be his wife, so that the name of the dead would not be cut off from among his brothers, or from the gate of his town.

And all the people in the gate, and the elders, say, We are witnesses. And they bless the marriage. May the Lord make this woman like Rachel and Leah, they say, who together built up the house of Israel. May you do worthily in Ephrathah, and be famous in Bethlehem.

So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and he went in to her, and the Lord enabled her to conceive, and she bore a son. The women said to Naomi, Blessed be the Lord, who has not left you today without a near kinsman. Let his name be famous in Israel. He shall be to you a restorer of life and sustain you in your old age; for your daughter-in-law, who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has given birth to him. Naomi took the child, laid him in her bosom, and became nurse to him. The women, her neighbors, gave him a name, saying, A son is born to Naomi. They named him Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.

So Boaz took Ruth, and she became his wife, and the Lord gave her a son.

And now the women of Bethlehem come back into the story. Do you remember them? They were the ones who came out when Naomi first came home, and asked, Is this Naomi? Now they gather around her again, and this time they bless the Lord. He has not left you, they say, without a redeemer. This child will restore your life and sustain you in your old age. For your daughter-in-law, who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has given birth to him.

Better than seven sons. In that world, seven sons was the very picture of perfect blessing. And the women of Bethlehem look at Ruth, the foreigner from Moab, and say she is better than all of that.

Naomi takes the child, and lays him in her lap, and becomes his nurse. The woman who said call me bitter, who said she had come home empty, now sits in the sunlight with a baby in her arms. And the neighbor women give the child his name. They call him Obed, which means servant, or one who serves.

And then the book adds a quiet line that opens onto the whole history of Israel. Obed, it says, was the father of Jesse, and Jesse was the father of David.

Now this is the history of the generations of Perez: Perez became the father of Hezron, and Hezron became the father of Ram, and Ram became the father of Amminadab, and Amminadab became the father of Nahshon, and Nahshon became the father of Salmon, and Salmon became the father of Boaz, and Boaz became the father of Obed, and Obed became the father of Jesse, and Jesse became the father of David.

David. The shepherd boy of Bethlehem, who would one day become the great king of Israel, the sweet singer of the psalms. Ruth the Moabitess, the foreigner who would not leave her mother-in-law on the road, was his great-grandmother. And centuries later, the Gospel of Matthew, at the very beginning of the New Testament, would name her again, in the long family line that leads down to Jesus.

That is the end of the book of Ruth. It began with a famine and three funerals, and it ends with a harvest, a wedding, and a baby asleep in his grandmother's lap. No one in it did anything very dramatic. No one fought a battle or worked a miracle. They were simply kind to one another, and loyal, and honest, and generous, a little more than they had to be. And through all of it, without a single voice from heaven, the story suggests that God was quietly at work.

Let the barley fields of Bethlehem grow dim now in the evening light. The threshing floor is quiet. The gate is closed for the night. Somewhere in a small house, a lamp is burning, and an old woman is singing softly to a child.
