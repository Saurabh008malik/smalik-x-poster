## Chapter 10: For Such a Time as This

When Mordecai learned all that had been done, he tore his clothes, and put on sackcloth and ashes, and went out into the middle of the city, and cried with a loud and bitter cry. He came as far as the king's gate, but no further, because no one was allowed to enter the king's gate dressed in sackcloth.

And in every province, wherever the king's command and his decree came, there was great mourning among the Jews, and fasting, and weeping, and wailing. And many lay in sackcloth and ashes.

Esther's maidens and her eunuchs came and told her this, and the queen was exceedingly grieved. She sent clothing to Mordecai, to replace his sackcloth, but he didn't receive it. Then Esther called for Hathach, one of the king's eunuchs, whom he had appointed to attend her, and commanded him to go to Mordecai, to find out what this was, and why it was. So Hathach went out to Mordecai, to the city square which was before the king's gate. Mordecai told him of all that had happened to him, and the exact sum of the money that Haman had promised to pay to the king's treasuries for the destruction of the Jews. He also gave him the copy of the writing of the decree that was given out in Susa to destroy them, to show it to Esther, and to declare it to her, and to urge her to go in to the king to make supplication to him, and to make request before him for her people. Hathach came and told Esther the words of Mordecai.

Esther, inside the palace, did not yet know about the decree. Her maids and her chamberlains came and told her only that Mordecai was outside the gate in sackcloth. And she was deeply distressed. She sent him clothes to wear instead, but he would not accept them.

So she sent one of the king's chamberlains, a man named Hathach, who had been appointed to attend her, to go out to Mordecai and find out what was wrong. And Hathach went out to the open square of the city, in front of the king's gate. Mordecai told him everything that had happened, and the exact sum of money Haman had promised to pay. And he gave him a copy of the decree that had been posted in Susa, to show to Esther. And he asked that she go in to the king, and plead with him for her people.

Then Esther spoke to Hathach, and gave him a message to Mordecai: All the king's servants and the people of the king's provinces know that whoever, whether man or woman, comes to the king into the inner court without being called, there is one law for him, that he be put to death, except those to whom the king might hold out the golden scepter, that he may live. I have not been called to come in to the king these thirty days. They told Esther's words to Mordecai.

Esther sends back her answer. Everyone knows the law, she says. Anyone, man or woman, who goes into the inner court to the king without being called, is put to death, unless the king holds out his golden scepter to them. And I have not been called to come in to the king these thirty days.

It is a real fear, and an honest one. Esther is queen, but she is not safe. The king's moods are changeable, as Vashti had learned. And she has not even been called to see him in a month. To walk in uninvited would be to put her life in his hands.

Then Mordecai sends back the words for which this book is best remembered.

Then Mordecai asked them to return this answer to Esther: Don't think to yourself that you will escape in the king's house any more than all the Jews. For if you remain silent now, then relief and deliverance will come to the Jews from another place, but you and your father's house will perish. Who knows if you haven't come to the kingdom for such a time as this?

Don't think to yourself, he says, that you will escape in the king's house any more than all the Jews. For if you remain silent now, relief and deliverance will come to the Jews from another place, but you and your father's house will perish. Who knows if you haven't come to the kingdom for such a time as this?

Who knows. Mordecai does not claim to know the future. He does not say, God has told me. He only wonders aloud, gently and seriously. He seems sure that deliverance will come somehow, from somewhere, because he does not believe his people will simply be forgotten. But he also wonders if perhaps this is why the orphan girl from his house was brought into the palace at all. Perhaps all those quiet turnings, the queen who would not come, the long months of preparation, the crown on her head, were leading to this one moment.

For such a time as this. Many people, over many centuries, have taken those words to heart in their own hard moments. Perhaps there is a reason I am here, in this place, at this time. Perhaps there is something only I can do.

Then Esther asked them to answer Mordecai, Go, gather together all the Jews who are present in Susa, and fast for me, and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day. I and my maidens will also fast the same way. Then I will go in to the king, which is against the law; and if I perish, I perish. So Mordecai went his way, and did according to all that Esther had commanded him.

And Esther makes her choice.

Go, she says, gather together all the Jews who are present in Susa, and fast for me. Don't eat or drink for three days, night or day. I and my maidens will also fast the same way. And so I will go in to the king, which is against the law. And if I perish, I perish.

If I perish, I perish. There is no boasting in it, and no false certainty. It is the voice of someone who is afraid, and who goes anyway. Like Ruth on the road from Moab, saying where you go, I will go, Esther binds herself to her people, whatever it costs.

And Mordecai went his way, and did everything that Esther had commanded him.

Let us stay for a little while in those three days. In the city of Susa, in houses all over the town, families are fasting and praying quietly. In the palace, in the queen's rooms, Esther and her maids are fasting too. The days are long and the nights are longer. The lamps burn low. Outside, the gardens of the citadel are still, and the great columns of the audience hall stand silent in the moonlight.

And on the third morning, the queen rises, and begins to dress in her royal robes.
