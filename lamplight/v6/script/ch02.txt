## Chapter 2: The Road Back, and Where You Go, I Will Go

News travels, even across deep valleys. One day, word came to Naomi in Moab that the famine in Judah was over. The Lord had visited his people, the old story says, and given them bread. The house of bread had bread again.

And so Naomi decided to go home.

It is worth imagining what that decision must have felt like. She had left Bethlehem with a husband and two sons. She would return with no one. She would walk back into the same streets, past the same doorways, and everyone would see. And yet home was home. Her people were there, and her own small piece of family land, and the graves of her ancestors. In Moab there was nothing left for her but memories.

So she set out, and her two daughters-in-law set out with her. Let us hear what happened on the road.

Then she arose with her daughters-in-law, that she might return from the country of Moab; for she had heard in the country of Moab how the Lord had visited his people in giving them bread. She went out of the place where she was, and her two daughters-in-law with her. They went on the way to return to the land of Judah. Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, Go, return each of you to her mother's house. May the Lord deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me. May the Lord grant you that you may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband. Then she kissed them, and they lifted up their voices, and wept. They said to her, No, but we will return with you to your people. Naomi said, Go back, my daughters. Why do you want to go with me? Do I still have sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands? Go back, my daughters, go your way; for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say, I have hope, if I should even have a husband tonight, and should also bear sons, would you then wait until they were grown? Would you then refrain from having husbands? No, my daughters, for it grieves me seriously for your sakes, for the Lord's hand has gone out against me. They lifted up their voices and wept again; then Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth stayed with her.

There is so much tenderness in that conversation. Naomi is not thinking of herself. She is thinking of these two young women, who had been good to her sons and good to her. She knows what lies ahead for them if they come with her. They will be foreigners in Bethlehem, widows with no husbands and no land, and she has nothing to give them. So she blesses them, and she kisses them, and she tells them to go home to their mothers' houses, and to find rest, each in the house of a new husband.

At first they both refuse. Then Naomi speaks more plainly, almost with a sad kind of humor. Am I going to have more sons, she asks, for you to marry? Even if I married tonight, and had sons, would you wait for them to grow up? No, my daughters. And the three of them weep together on the road.

Then Orpah kisses her mother-in-law goodbye. And it is important to say, gently, that the story does not blame her for it. She does exactly what Naomi asks. She is a loving daughter-in-law, and she makes a sensible choice, and she goes home to her people. We can wish her well as she turns back along the road toward the towns of Moab.

But Ruth clings to Naomi. And here come some of the most beautiful words in the whole Bible.

She said, Behold, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her god. Follow your sister-in-law. Ruth said, Don't urge me to leave you, and to return from following you, for where you go, I will go; and where you stay, I will stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May the Lord do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me. When Naomi saw that she was determined to go with her, she stopped urging her.

Where you go, I will go. Where you stay, I will stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried.

Ruth is choosing a whole new life, and she is choosing it for love. Not for a husband, because there is none to be had. Not for wealth, because Naomi has none. She is choosing to stay beside an older woman who has lost everything, so that she will not walk the road alone. And she binds herself to it with a vow, calling on the Lord, the God of Israel, whom she now takes as her own.

When Naomi saw that Ruth was determined, she stopped urging her. And the two of them walked on together.

Let us walk with them for a while. The road from Moab to Bethlehem goes down from the high tableland, along steep paths, into the deep valley of the Jordan. The air grows warm and heavy as you descend. Far to the south, the Dead Sea lies flat and silver in its basin, the lowest place on the face of the earth. Then the road crosses the river at one of the fords, and begins to climb again, up and up, through the dry wilderness of Judah, until at last the land turns green and the terraces begin, and there, on its ridge, is Bethlehem.

It is spring when they arrive. The barley is ripe in the fields.

So they both went until they came to Bethlehem. When they had come to Bethlehem, all the city was excited about them, and they asked, Is this Naomi? She said to them, Don't call me Naomi. Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and the Lord has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since the Lord has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me? So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned out of the country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest.

The whole town is stirred. The women come out and look, and say to each other, Is this Naomi? Can this be her? And Naomi answers with great sadness. Don't call me Naomi, she says, don't call me pleasant. Call me Mara, which means bitter. I went out full, she says, and the Lord has brought me home empty.

It is an honest thing to say, and the Bible lets her say it. The story does not hurry her out of her grief, or tell her she should feel otherwise. It simply lets her speak, and then it tells us one quiet thing that she herself does not yet see. She did not come home entirely empty. Ruth the Moabitess was standing beside her.

And the chapter ends with a small detail, almost like a door left open. They came to Bethlehem, it says, at the beginning of barley harvest.
