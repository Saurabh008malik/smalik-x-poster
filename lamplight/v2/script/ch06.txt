## Chapter 6: Seven Years of Plenty, Seven Years of Hunger

Joseph went out from the presence of Pharaoh, and went throughout all the land of Egypt. In the seven plenteous years the earth produced abundantly. And he gathered up all the food of the seven years, and laid it up in the cities. The food of the fields around each city he stored in that city. He heaped up grain, the story says, as the sand of the sea, very much, until he stopped counting, for it was without number.

Picture it. Seven harvests, one after another, each one heavier than the last. The threshing floors crowded. The granaries, great mud-brick storehouses, filled to the roof and then more built beside them. And a man in a fine linen robe riding from city to city with a scroll, making sure that one fifth of everything went into the stores, year after year, while everyone around him said that the good years would surely never end.

It is not hard to be careful in a hungry year. The hard thing is to be careful in a fat one. Joseph was careful for seven fat years in a row.

In those years, two sons were born to him. He named the first Manasseh, which sounds like the word for forgetting, because, he said, God has made me forget all my toil and all my father's house. He named the second Ephraim, which sounds like the word for fruitful, because God has made me fruitful in the land of my affliction.

I want to be gentle with those names, because they tell you something true about grief. Made me forget all my father's house. He had not forgotten. You will see in a moment how far he had not forgotten. But he had reached a place where the past did not bite every day. He had a wife and two little boys and a life, in the land of his affliction, and he called that fruitfulness, and it was.

Then the seven years of plenty ended. And the seven years of famine began, as Joseph had said. There was famine in all lands, but in all the land of Egypt there was bread.

When all the land of Egypt was hungry, the people cried to Pharaoh for bread, and Pharaoh said to all the Egyptians, go to Joseph. What he says to you, do. The famine was over all the surface of the earth. And Joseph opened all the storehouses, and sold to the Egyptians. And all countries came into Egypt to Joseph to buy grain, because the famine was severe in all the earth.

All countries. The famine did not stop at the border of Egypt. It reached north, into the hill country of Canaan, into a valley near Hebron, into the tents of an old man with eleven sons.

Now Jacob saw that there was grain in Egypt, and Jacob said to his sons, why do you look at one another? Behold, I have heard that there is grain in Egypt. Go down there, and buy for us from there, so that we may live, and not die.

Why do you look at one another. You can hear the old man's impatience, and under it his fear. Ten grown men standing around while the children went hungry, each waiting for someone else to say the word Egypt. Perhaps they had their reasons not to want to go south. But hunger does not care about reasons.

So Joseph's ten brothers went down to buy grain from Egypt. But Jacob did not send Benjamin, Joseph's brother, with his brothers, for he said, lest perhaps harm happen to him. Benjamin was Rachel's other son. Jacob had lost one of Rachel's boys already. He would not risk the second.

And the sons of Israel came to buy among those who came, for the famine was in the land of Canaan.

Here the story draws a breath, and so should we. Think about what is about to happen. Joseph was the governor over the land. It was he who sold to all the people of the land. And Joseph's brothers came, and bowed themselves down to him with their faces to the earth.

Bowed themselves down. Sheaves in a field. Twenty-two years before, a boy had told a dream about sheaves bowing, and his brothers had thrown him in a pit for it. Now ten men were on their faces in the dust before an Egyptian lord, and did not know that the dream had just come true, with themselves inside it.

Joseph saw his brothers, and he recognized them. But he acted like a stranger to them, and spoke roughly with them. Where did you come from? From the land of Canaan, they said, to buy food. Joseph recognized his brothers, but they did not recognize him. Of course they did not. They had sold a boy of seventeen with a shepherd's beard. They were looking at a clean-shaven man of nearly forty, in Egyptian robes, speaking through an interpreter, with the power of life and death in his hand.

And Joseph remembered the dreams which he dreamed about them.

That is the exact sentence. He remembered the dreams. Not the pit, not the twenty pieces of silver, not the coat. The dreams. Standing there, looking down at ten bowed heads, what came back to him first was not the wrong that had been done to him but the promise that had been made.

What he does next is going to take several chapters, and it is going to look harsh, and I will ask you to be patient with him. He accuses them of being spies. He puts them all in custody for three days. He is testing them. He needs to know who these men are now. Are they the same brothers who sat down to eat bread beside a pit? Or has something changed in twenty-two years? He has one brother, Benjamin, who is not with them, and he has to know whether these men have done to Rachel's second son what they did to her first.

That is where we will pause. The brothers in an Egyptian cell for three days, bewildered, hungry, afraid, and arguing among themselves in a language they are certain the governor cannot understand.

Rest a while. Something is about to be said in that cell, in Hebrew, that the man standing by the door has waited twenty-two years to hear.
