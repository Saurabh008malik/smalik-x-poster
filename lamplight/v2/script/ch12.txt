## Chapter 12: You Meant Evil, God Meant Good, and Goodnight

Jacob called all his sons to his bedside, all twelve of them, and spoke a word over each one, a long poem of blessings and warnings that is one of the oldest passages in the Bible. Reuben, unstable as water. Simeon and Levi, brothers in violence. Judah, a lion's cub, whose brothers would praise him, to whom the scepter would belong. Joseph, a fruitful vine by a spring, whose branches run over the wall, whose bow stayed strong when archers shot at him. Benjamin, a wolf in the morning. Each son his own word, the good and the hard together.

And when Jacob finished charging his sons, he gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded up the spirit, and was gathered to his people.

And Joseph fell on his father's face, and wept on him, and kissed him. Joseph closed his eyes, as God had promised at Beersheba he would.

Then Joseph did a remarkable thing. He had his father embalmed, in the Egyptian way, which took forty days, and the Egyptians mourned for Jacob seventy days, which was nearly the mourning of a king. And then Joseph asked Pharaoh's leave, and went up to bury his father in Canaan, and with him went all the servants of Pharaoh, the elders of his house, and all the elders of the land of Egypt, with chariots and horsemen, a very great company. A shepherd from the hills of Hebron was carried home to his cave by the nobility of Egypt. They buried him in the cave of the field of Machpelah, beside Leah, near Abraham and Sarah and Isaac and Rebekah, and then they came back down to Egypt.

And then, when the father was gone, the old fear came back.

Here are the old words.

When Joseph's brothers saw that their father was dead, they said, "It may be that Joseph will hate us, and will fully pay us back for all of the evil which we did to him." They sent a message to Joseph, saying, "Your father commanded before he died, saying, 'You shall tell Joseph, "Now please forgive the disobedience of your brothers, and their sin, because they did evil to you."' Now, please forgive the disobedience of the servants of the God of your father." Joseph wept when they spoke to him.

Seventeen years in Goshen. Seventeen years of being fed by his hand, of their children growing up near his, of his kindness every day. And still, when the father who might have held him back was gone, they thought, now it comes. They sent a message, not even themselves at first, a message, with a story in it about what their father had supposedly said. The text leaves it open whether Jacob ever said any such thing. It does not matter. It was the fear talking.

And Joseph wept. Not from anger. From the sheer weariness and sorrow of being feared by people he had already forgiven. Have I not shown you, after all these years? And still you think I am waiting to strike.

His brothers also went and fell down before his face, and said, behold, we are your servants. The sheaves, one last time.

Here are the old words.

Joseph said to them, "Don't be afraid, for am I in the place of God? As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save many people alive. Now therefore don't be afraid. I will nourish you and your little ones." He comforted them, and spoke kindly to them.

You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good. If you remember nothing else from tonight, you can keep that. It does not say the evil was not evil. It says that it was not the whole story, and it was not the end. It says that the thing done to you, whatever it was, has been handed to someone who can make something out of it that the ones who did it never intended. And then, do not be afraid. I will nourish you and your little ones. He comforted them, and spoke kindly to them.

He comforted them. The word comforted has been following this family through the whole book. Jacob refused to be comforted. Isaac was comforted after his mother's death. And now Joseph comforts the men who threw him in the pit. It is the last turn of the story, and it is the gentlest.

Joseph lived in Egypt, he and his father's house, and he lived a hundred and ten years. He saw Ephraim's children to the third generation. The grandchildren of his grandchildren were brought up on his knees. The boy who was sold for twenty pieces of silver died an old man, surrounded by great-grandchildren, in the land of his affliction which had become the land of his fruitfulness.

And his last words were not about himself.

Here are the old words.

Joseph said to his brothers, "I am dying, but God will surely visit you, and bring you up out of this land to the land which he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." Joseph took an oath of the children of Israel, saying, "God will surely visit you, and you shall carry up my bones from here." So Joseph died, being one hundred ten years old, and they embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt.

That is the last sentence of the book of Genesis. A coffin in Egypt. It sounds like an ending, and it is not one. Joseph's last words were a promise about a future he would not see. God will surely visit you. You will go home. Take me with you when you go. And they did. Hundreds of years later, when the children of Israel finally left Egypt, the book of Exodus records that Moses took the bones of Joseph with him, because of this oath. The coffin was carried through the sea and the wilderness and was buried, at last, in the land of promise, at Shechem, the very place where a boy in a coat of many colors had once gone looking for his brothers.

So the story that began with a dream about sheaves ends with a box of bones waiting patiently in a foreign land, trusting a promise. And the promise was kept.

Let me gather the night together before you go. A boy was loved too much and told his dreams too freely. His brothers threw him in a pit and sold him, and lied to their father, and carried the lie for twenty-two years. He was a slave, and faithful. He was accused, and faithful. He was forgotten in a prison, and faithful. And then one morning he was lifted up, and he fed a nation, and he fed the world, and when his brothers came and bowed before him without knowing him, he tested them, and wept, and tested them, and wept, until he saw that they had changed. And then he told them who he was, and said, come near, and do not be afraid, and it was not you, it was God, and you meant evil, but God meant good.

The Lord was with Joseph. In the house, in the prison, in the palace, in the end. That was the whole secret. Not that nothing went wrong. Nearly everything went wrong. Only that he was never, in any of it, alone.

And neither are you, tonight, in the dark, with the day behind you and the morning promised. Whatever was meant against you, something greater is at work in it, slowly, the way grain grows in a storehouse through seven long years.

Thank you for listening. Rest now. Do not be afraid.

Goodnight.
