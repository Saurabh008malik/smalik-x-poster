## Chapter 7: We Are Certainly Guilty

On the third day, Joseph had the brothers brought out, and he spoke to them differently than before. Do this, he said, and live, for I fear God. If you are honest men, let one of your brothers be bound in your prison house, but the rest of you go, carry grain for the famine of your houses. And bring your youngest brother to me. So will your words be verified, and you will not die.

He is sending them home with food. He is keeping one as surety. And he is asking for the one thing he needs to see with his own eyes. Benjamin. Alive, and well, and still with them.

And then the brothers, standing there in front of the Egyptian governor, began to talk among themselves. They did not know he could understand them. There was an interpreter between them, and they assumed, reasonably, that a lord of Egypt spoke no Hebrew.

Here are the old words.

They said one to another, "We are certainly guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the distress of his soul, when he begged us, and we wouldn't listen. Therefore this distress has come upon us." Reuben answered them, saying, "Didn't I tell you, saying, 'Don't sin against the child,' and you wouldn't listen? Therefore also, behold, his blood is required."

We saw the distress of his soul, when he begged us. This is the first time in the whole story that we hear what happened at the pit from the brothers' side. The text did not tell us, back in chapter thirty-seven, that Joseph begged. It only said they sat down to eat bread. Now, twenty-two years later, in a foreign prison, the brothers tell us themselves. He begged, and we would not listen. They had carried that sound with them the whole time. They had never said it out loud, and now it came pouring out, in front of the one man on earth who had been in the pit.

And Joseph turned himself away from them, and wept.

It is the first time he weeps in the story. It will not be the last. He weeps seven times before the end, if you count them, and each time it is harder for him to hide. But this first time he manages. He turned away, wept, came back, and spoke to them again, and took Simeon from among them and bound him before their eyes. Simeon, the second son. Perhaps because Reuben, the eldest, had just been heard trying to save him all those years ago.

Then Joseph gave orders to fill their sacks with grain, and to restore every man's money into his sack, and to give them provisions for the way. They loaded their donkeys and left. And at the lodging place that night, one of them opened his sack to feed his donkey, and saw his money, in the mouth of the sack. And their hearts failed them, and they turned trembling to one another, saying, what is this that God has done to us?

Think about it from their side. They had come to buy grain honestly. They had been accused of spying, imprisoned, and relieved of a brother. Now they find their silver returned, which to them could only look like a trap. Someone wanted them accused of theft. They could not see kindness in it because they were not expecting kindness. Guilt makes every gift look like a snare.

They came home to Jacob and told him everything. The man, the lord of the land, spoke roughly with us, and took us for spies. He has kept Simeon. He says we must bring Benjamin. And as they emptied their sacks, every man's bundle of money was in his sack, and when they and their father saw the bundles of money, they were afraid.

And Jacob their father said to them, you have bereaved me of my children. Joseph is no more, Simeon is no more, and you want to take Benjamin away. All these things are against me.

All these things are against me. If you have ever been an old person who has lost too much, you know the sound of that sentence. Reuben, trying, said something almost absurd. Kill my two sons if I do not bring him back to you. Put him in my hands and I will bring him to you again. And Jacob said, my son shall not go down with you. His brother is dead, and he only is left. If harm happens to him on the way, you will bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to the grave.

He only is left. In a tent full of sons, the old man says that one boy is all he has left. The other ten heard him say it. They had been hearing it, in one form or another, for twenty-two years.

So they did not go back. Simeon stayed in the Egyptian prison. And the famine went on.

The famine was severe in the land. And when they had eaten up the grain they had brought out of Egypt, their father said to them, go again, buy us a little food. And Judah spoke to him.

Remember Judah. Judah who said, let us sell him. Judah who said, he is our brother, our flesh, and sold him anyway. Listen to Judah now.

The man solemnly warned us, he said, saying, you shall not see my face unless your brother is with you. If you will send our brother with us, we will go down and buy you food. But if you will not send him, we will not go down. And Israel said, why did you treat me so badly, telling the man you had another brother? And they said, the man asked us about ourselves and our relatives. He asked, is your father still alive? Have you another brother? We just answered him. How could we know he would say, bring your brother down?

And Judah said to Israel his father, send the boy with me, and we will arise and go, that we may live and not die, both we and you and also our little ones. I will be collateral for him. From my hand you will require him. If I do not bring him to you and set him before you, then let me bear the blame forever.

I will be collateral for him. Judah, who sold one of Rachel's sons for twenty pieces of silver, is now offering himself as the guarantee for the other. Something has happened to Judah in twenty-two years. The story does not explain it. It simply shows you a man who has changed.

And the old father gave in. If it must be so, he said, then do this. Take some of the best fruits of the land in your bags and carry down a present for the man. A little balm, a little honey, spices and myrrh, nuts and almonds. And take double money in your hand, and carry back the money that was returned in your sacks, perhaps it was an oversight. Take your brother also. And may God Almighty give you mercy before the man, that he may release to you your other brother and Benjamin. And if I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved.

If I am bereaved, I am bereaved. It is the sound of a man letting go of the last thing he was holding.

So the men took the present, and double money, and Benjamin, and rose up and went down to Egypt, and stood before Joseph.

And that is where we rest tonight. Eleven brothers at last, on the road south, with honey and almonds in their bags and a frightened old man's blessing on their heads. Joseph does not know they are coming. He only knows he has been waiting.

Breathe out. The hardest part of the waiting is over.
