## Chapter 11: The Road to Goshen

Israel traveled with all that he had, and came to Beersheba, at the southern edge of the land, the last well before the desert. And there he offered sacrifices to the God of his father Isaac. He was leaving the land that had been promised to his grandfather, his father, and himself. He was an old man, going down into Egypt, and he did not know if he would ever come back. So he stopped at the edge, and prayed.

And God spoke to Israel in the visions of the night.

Here are the old words.

He said, "I am God, the God of your father. Don't be afraid to go down into Egypt, for there I will make of you a great nation. I will go down with you into Egypt. I will also surely bring you up again. Joseph will close your eyes."

Three promises, for an old man afraid of a journey. I will go down with you. I will bring you up again. And the last one, the tenderest. Joseph will close your eyes. In that time and place, closing the eyes of the dead was the duty of the nearest and dearest. God was telling Jacob, not only will you see your son, he will be there at the very end, and his hand will be the last thing you feel. Do not be afraid.

So Jacob rose up from Beersheba, and the sons of Israel carried their father, and their little ones, and their wives, in the wagons which Pharaoh had sent. They took their livestock and their goods, and came into Egypt, Jacob and all his offspring with him. The story pauses here to give the names, all of them, the sons and grandsons, a daughter and a granddaughter, seventy souls in all who came into Egypt. I will not read all the names tonight. But it is a long list, and it is a list of a family, and a family is what had been saved.

Jacob sent Judah ahead of him to Joseph, to show the way to Goshen. Judah again. The one who went first, now, every time.

And they came into the land of Goshen, which is the rich delta country in the north of Egypt, green and wide, good for flocks.

Here are the old words.

Joseph made ready his chariot, and went up to meet Israel, his father, in Goshen. He presented himself to him, and fell on his neck, and wept on his neck a good while. Israel said to Joseph, "Now let me die, since I have seen your face, that you are still alive."

A good while. The text does not say how long. Only that it was a good while. The governor of Egypt, in his chariot, with his escort, riding out into the pasture country, and seeing from far off a crowd of shepherds and wagons and an old man being helped down, and getting out of the chariot, and walking, and then not walking, and falling on his father's neck, and weeping for a good while. Twenty-two years. The boy who went to check on his brothers at Shechem and never came home.

And Jacob's first words. Now let me die, since I have seen your face. Not a complaint. A completion. The thing he had been refusing to be comforted about had been undone, and he could rest.

Joseph settled his family in Goshen. He brought his father before Pharaoh, and Jacob blessed Pharaoh, and Pharaoh asked him, how old are you? And Jacob said, the days of the years of my pilgrimage are one hundred thirty years. Few and evil have been the days of the years of my life, and they have not attained to the days of the years of the life of my fathers. Few and evil, he says, at a hundred and thirty. He was an honest old man. His life had been hard, and he said so, to a king, and then he blessed the king and went out.

And Joseph provided for his father and his brothers and all his father's household with bread, according to their families. Through the rest of the famine, five more years, while Egypt itself grew poor and sold its land and its labor to Pharaoh for grain, the family of Israel sat in Goshen and was fed. And they grew and multiplied exceedingly.

Jacob lived in the land of Egypt seventeen years. The same number of years Joseph had been with his father before the pit. Seventeen years of a boy, and then, at the other end of a life, seventeen years of an old man, with the same son beside him. The story does not point this out. It just lets the number be there, for anyone who notices.

When the time drew near that Israel must die, he called his son Joseph, and made him swear not to bury him in Egypt but to carry him back to the cave of Machpelah, where Abraham and Sarah lay, and Isaac and Rebekah, and Leah. And Joseph swore it.

And then Jacob did one more thing, and it is a scene worth falling asleep on. He was ill, and nearly blind, and Joseph brought his two sons to him, Manasseh and Ephraim, the boys born in Egypt, to be blessed. The old man gathered his strength and sat up in bed. Who are these, he asked. They are my sons, Joseph said, whom God has given me here. And Israel said, bring them to me, and I will bless them. And he kissed them and embraced them. I had not thought to see your face, he said to Joseph, and behold, God has let me see your offspring also.

Here are the old words of the blessing.

He blessed Joseph, and said, "The God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk, the God who has fed me all my life long to this day, the angel who has redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads, and let my name be named on them, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac. Let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth."

The God who has fed me all my life long to this day. That is a shepherd's way of saying it. Fed me, like a flock. And a moment ago he had told Pharaoh his days were few and evil, and now, with his grandsons under his hands, he says, all my life long, fed, and redeemed from all evil. Both things were true. He had been honest about the hardness and he was honest about the mercy. Old people who have lived long enough often hold both at once.

He crossed his hands as he blessed them, putting his right hand on the younger boy's head, and Joseph tried to correct him, and the old man said, I know, my son, I know. The younger would be greater. It had been that way in his own life, and his father's, and it would go on being that way. The story keeps choosing the one nobody expected.

That is where we rest tonight. An old man propped up in bed in Goshen, nearly blind, with two little boys under his crossed hands and his lost son standing by, saying the names of Abraham and Isaac over children who would never see Canaan. The lamp is low. The blessing has been given. Only the goodbyes are left.
