## Chapter 3: The Lord Was With Joseph

Egypt. Picture it, if you can, as it would have looked to a shepherd's son from the hills. The widest river he had ever seen, brown and slow and enormous, with green fields on either side of it and desert beyond. Cities of mud brick and stone. Temples with pillars thicker than trees. Men with shaved heads and painted eyes. The smell of the river. And nowhere, in any direction, a single familiar face.

Joseph was bought by Potiphar, an Egyptian, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard. And then the story says something it is going to say four times in two chapters, like a hand resting on a shoulder.

Here are the old words.

The Lord was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man. He was in the house of his master the Egyptian. His master saw that the Lord was with him, and that the Lord made all that he did prosper in his hand. Joseph found favor in his sight. He ministered to him, and he made him overseer over his house, and all that he had he put into his hand.

The Lord was with Joseph. Not the Lord rescued Joseph, or the Lord took Joseph home. He was still a slave. He still belonged to another man. But he was not alone in it. And slowly, the way a plant turns toward a window, things began to go right for him. He was good at whatever he was given to do. His master noticed. And Potiphar put him in charge of the whole household, and the story says that from that time the Lord blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake, and the blessing was on all that he had, in the house and in the field. Potiphar left everything in Joseph's hand, and did not concern himself with anything except the food he ate.

That is a strange and lovely detail. A great officer of Pharaoh, who no longer knows or cares what is in his own storerooms, because a Hebrew slave is running them so well. The old teachers liked to point out that God's blessing on Joseph spilled over onto a pagan household. It was never meant to be kept in one place.

The story also pauses to tell us that Joseph was well built and handsome. And that is where the trouble begins.

Potiphar's wife looked at him, and kept looking. And after a while she said to him, lie with me. And Joseph refused. Listen to how he refused, because it tells you who he had become in those years. Behold, he said, my master does not know what is with me in the house, and he has put all that he has into my hand. He is not greater in this house than I am, and he has kept back nothing from me but you, because you are his wife. How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?

He is loyal to his master, and he is loyal to God, and he names both. And she did not stop. Day after day she spoke to him, the text says, and he did not listen to her, to lie by her, or to be with her. He kept out of her way. But one day he went into the house to do his work, and there was none of the men of the house inside, and she caught him by his garment, and said, lie with me. And he left his garment in her hand, and fled, and ran outside.

A garment again. The coat of many colors was taken from him and used to tell a lie to his father. Now his cloak is taken from him and used to tell a lie to his master. Twice in his young life a piece of his clothing has been held up by someone else as evidence of something that never happened.

She called the men of her house and told them the Hebrew had come in to mock her, and that she had cried out, and that he had fled and left his garment. She kept the garment beside her until Potiphar came home, and told him the same story. And Potiphar's anger burned. He took Joseph and put him in the prison, the place where the king's prisoners were bound.

Some readers have noticed that Potiphar did not have Joseph killed, which a man in his position could easily have done to a slave accused of such a thing. They wonder whether he half believed Joseph. The text does not say. It only says, prison.

So Joseph, who had climbed from the pit to the top of a great house, was thrown down again. He was perhaps in his late twenties now. He had been faithful, and the reward for his faithfulness was a cell. If the story stopped here it would be a bitter one. But listen to what comes next, because it is the same hand on the shoulder.

Here are the old words.

Joseph's master took him, and put him into the prison, the place where the king's prisoners were bound, and he was there in custody. But the Lord was with Joseph, and showed kindness to him, and gave him favor in the sight of the keeper of the prison. The keeper of the prison committed to Joseph's hand all the prisoners who were in the prison. Whatever they did there, he was responsible for it. The keeper of the prison didn't look after anything that was under his hand, because the Lord was with him; and that which he did, the Lord made it prosper.

But the Lord was with Joseph. The same words as before. In a rich man's house, the Lord was with him. In a prison cell, the Lord was with him. The place changed completely. The presence did not change at all.

And the same thing happened again. The keeper of the prison noticed him, and trusted him, and put the whole prison in his hands, and stopped checking. Joseph did in a dungeon what he had done in a palace. He ran it well, and the people in charge relaxed.

There is a kind of person who, wherever you put them, ends up quietly holding things together. Joseph was that person. The story does not say he was happy about it. It does not say he never lay awake at night wondering why he had been thrown into pits and prisons for doing the right thing. It only says that he kept doing the right thing, and that he was not alone.

That is where we will leave him tonight. In a prison in Egypt, with the keys, oddly enough, on his own belt. Far from home, falsely accused, and trusted by everyone around him. The dreams have gone very quiet. But two new dreamers are about to arrive in his cell, and their dreams are going to open a door.

Rest a while first. Even in prison, the nights were long and quiet, and the river moved past outside in the dark.
