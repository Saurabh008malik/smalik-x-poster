## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Coat of Many Colors

Good evening, and welcome back. Settle in. Let the pillow take the weight of your head and let your hands go loose. The day is over, and you have done enough.

This is Lamplight Scripture. Tonight we are going to spend the whole night with one man, and one family, in one of the oldest and best-loved stories ever told. It is the story of Joseph, the dreamer, the boy with the coat of many colors, who was thrown into a pit by his own brothers, sold into Egypt, forgotten in a prison, and raised up to stand beside a king. It fills the last fourteen chapters of the book of Genesis, and it has everything a story can have. Jealousy and dreams, famine and forgiveness, a father's grief and a long-delayed embrace.

I will tell it slowly, in plain words, the way you might tell it to someone falling asleep beside you. And now and then I will read the old words themselves, from a public domain translation called the World English Bible, so you hear the scripture in its own voice. You do not need to follow every turn. If you drift off in the middle of a dream or a famine, the story will carry on without you, and it will be waiting whenever you come back.

Let us begin in the hill country of Canaan, in the tents of an old man named Jacob.

Jacob, who was also called Israel, had twelve sons. That is a great many sons, and they came from four different mothers, which is part of why this story is so tangled. But of all of them, there was one he loved best. His name was Joseph, and he was the first son of Rachel, the wife Jacob had loved from the moment he saw her at a well, the wife he had worked fourteen years to marry, the wife who had died on the road giving birth to Joseph's younger brother, Benjamin.

So when Jacob looked at Joseph, he saw Rachel. And he could not hide it.

Here are the old words.

Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age, and he made him a coat of many colors. His brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, and they hated him, and couldn't speak peaceably to him.

The coat of many colors. Nobody knows exactly what the Hebrew phrase means. It may have been a coat with long sleeves, or a coat reaching to the ankles, or a coat of many pieces, richly worked. What everyone agrees on is what it meant. It was the coat of someone who did not work in the fields. It was the coat of a favorite. And every time the other brothers came in dusty from the flocks and saw Joseph wearing it, something in them curdled.

Joseph was seventeen years old. The story says he was shepherding the flock with his brothers, and that he brought back a bad report of them to their father. In plain language, he told on them. Whether what he said was true or not, the text does not say. But you can imagine how it landed.

And then there were the dreams.

Here are the old words.

Joseph dreamed a dream, and he told it to his brothers, and they hated him all the more. He said to them, "Please hear this dream which I have dreamed: for behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and behold, my sheaf arose and also stood upright; and behold, your sheaves came around, and bowed down to my sheaf." His brothers said to him, "Will you indeed reign over us? Or will you indeed have dominion over us?" They hated him all the more for his dreams and for his words.

Sheaves of grain, bowing. And then a second dream, which he also told. The sun, the moon, and eleven stars, bowing down to him. This time even his father rebuked him. What is this dream that you have dreamed? Shall I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow ourselves down to you to the earth? His brothers envied him. But his father, the story says, kept the saying in mind.

I want to pause on Joseph for a moment, because it would be easy to dislike him here, and I think the story does not want you to. He is seventeen. He is his father's favorite and he knows it. He has had two strange, vivid dreams and he cannot keep them to himself. He is not wicked. He is young, and loved, and tactless, and he has no idea what is coming. Most of us have been that person once. Some of us have been the brothers.

And notice this. The dreams were true. That is the quiet shock of the whole story. The sheaves really would bow. The stars really would. Joseph was not boasting. He was, without knowing it, telling the truth about a future that would take more than twenty years and a great deal of suffering to arrive. The old teachers say that this is often how it goes. A promise comes early, in a form that makes everyone angry, and then it goes underground for a long time, and when it finally comes true nobody recognizes it at first.

So here is the family, at the beginning of the night. An old father who loves one son too openly. Ten older brothers, hard men who work the flocks, with a grievance that has been growing for years. A little brother, Benjamin, too young to matter yet. And a boy in a beautiful coat, who has dreams he should not have told.

The flocks are out at Shechem, a long way from home. In a moment, the father is going to send the boy to go and check on his brothers. He is going to put on his coat and set out alone across the hills.

But let him stay home tonight a little longer. Let him be seventeen, and safe, and asleep in his father's tent, with the coat folded beside him and the dreams still only dreams.

Breathe slowly. The road to Shechem can wait.
