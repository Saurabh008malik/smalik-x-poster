## Chapter 2: The Pit

Jacob said to Joseph, your brothers are feeding the flock in Shechem. Come, and I will send you to them. Go now, see whether it is well with your brothers, and well with the flock, and bring me word again.

So Joseph went. He went out of the valley of Hebron, wearing his coat, and walked north toward Shechem, which was a journey of several days. And when he got there, the brothers were gone. A man found him wandering in the field and asked him what he was looking for. I am looking for my brothers, Joseph said. Please tell me where they are feeding the flock. And the man said, they have left here, for I heard them say, let us go to Dothan.

The story includes this small detail, the stranger in the field, and it has always struck readers as strange and sad. If the man had not been there, Joseph would have gone home. He would have told his father the brothers had moved on, and the whole story would have been different. Instead, a nameless man pointed him down the road to Dothan, and everything that followed, followed from that.

Joseph went after his brothers, and found them at Dothan. And they saw him from far off. They would have known the coat before they knew the face.

Here are the old words.

They said one to another, "Behold, this dreamer comes. Come now therefore, and let's kill him, and cast him into one of the pits, and we will say, 'An evil animal has devoured him.' We will see what will become of his dreams."

We will see what will become of his dreams. It is almost the cruelest line in the chapter. They are not only going to kill the boy. They are going to kill the future he talked about.

But not all of them agreed. Reuben, the eldest, heard them, and spoke up. Let us not take his life, he said. Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit that is in the wilderness, but lay no hand on him. The story tells you Reuben's plan plainly. He meant to come back later, alone, and pull Joseph out, and bring him home to their father. Reuben had his own reasons to want to be back in his father's good graces. But whatever the reasons, he tried.

So when Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped him of his coat, the coat of many colors that was on him, and took him and threw him into the pit. And the pit was empty. There was no water in it. Those are the exact words. The story wants you to know that the boy did not drown. He was simply down there, in the dark, in the dry.

And then comes a line that is easy to read past. They sat down to eat bread.

They put their brother in a hole in the ground and sat down to lunch. You can hear, if you listen for it, a voice coming up out of the pit. The text does not record it here. But years later the brothers will remember it. We saw the distress of his soul, they will say, when he begged us, and we would not listen. So he was begging. And they ate.

While they ate, they lifted up their eyes and looked, and a caravan of Ishmaelites was coming from Gilead, with their camels bearing spices and balm and myrrh, going down to Egypt. Traders on the long road south. And Judah, the fourth son, had an idea.

Here are the old words.

Judah said to his brothers, "What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood? Come, and let's sell him to the Ishmaelites, and not let our hand be on him; for he is our brother, our flesh." His brothers listened to him. Midianites who were merchants passed by, and they drew and lifted up Joseph out of the pit, and sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver. They brought Joseph into Egypt.

Twenty pieces of silver. That was the price of a young slave. He is our brother, our flesh, Judah said, and then sold him anyway. Judah is going to matter a great deal later in this story. Keep him in mind. The man who said sell him is going to become, before the end, the man who says take me instead.

Reuben had not been there. When he came back to the pit, alone, to carry out his rescue, the pit was empty. And he tore his clothes, which is what a person did in that time and place when something was unbearable, and he went back to his brothers and said, the child is no more, and I, where will I go?

The brothers took Joseph's coat, and killed a male goat, and dipped the coat in the blood. And they brought it to their father, and said, we have found this. Examine it now, and see whether it is your son's coat or not.

Notice how they say it. Your son. Not our brother. They cannot even say his name.

Here are the old words.

He recognized it, and said, "It is my son's coat. An evil animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn in pieces." Jacob tore his clothes, and put sackcloth on his waist, and mourned for his son many days. All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted. He said, "For I will go down to Sheol to my son mourning." His father wept for him.

All his sons rose up to comfort him. The same sons who had done it. Picture that house. The old man weeping over a bloodied coat, and ten grown men standing around him, patting his shoulder, carrying the secret. They would carry it for more than twenty years.

And one more detail, which the chapter ends on. The Midianites sold Joseph into Egypt, to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh's, the captain of the guard. So while his father wept in Hebron, Joseph was being walked through the gates of a great foreign city, with his hands tied, seventeen years old, speaking the wrong language, owned by a stranger.

The pit had no water in it. The story made sure to tell you. Hold on to that. He was in the dark, but he was alive. And the dreams, which the brothers had thrown into the hole with him, had climbed out with him too, and gone down to Egypt, and were waiting.

Rest now. The next part of the story takes place in a rich man's house, and for a while, things are going to go surprisingly well.
