## Chapter 10: I Am Joseph

Here are the old words, and I will read them slowly, because this is the moment the whole night has been walking toward.

Then Joseph couldn't control himself before all those who stood before him, and he cried, "Cause every man to go out from me!" No one else stood with him, while Joseph made himself known to his brothers. He wept aloud. The Egyptians heard, and the house of Pharaoh heard. Joseph said to his brothers, "I am Joseph! Does my father still live?" His brothers couldn't answer him; for they were terrified at his presence. Joseph said to his brothers, "Come near to me, please." They came near. He said, "I am Joseph, your brother, whom you sold into Egypt. Now don't be grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that you sold me here, for God sent me before you to preserve life."

Picture it. The governor shouting for the room to be cleared. The Egyptian servants and guards and scribes filing out, baffled. And then, alone with eleven Hebrew shepherds, the second most powerful man in the world breaking down and weeping so loudly that the servants in the courtyard heard it, and word of it reached Pharaoh's own house.

I am Joseph. Two words in Hebrew. And then, before anything else, before any accusation, the question he had asked already through an interpreter and now asks in his own voice, in his own language. Does my father still live?

And the brothers could not answer. The text says they were terrified at his presence. Of course they were. The boy from the pit was standing in front of them in a gold chain, with an army at his call. Every one of them must have thought the same thing. Now it comes.

And what came was, come near to me, please. Please. He asked them to come closer. And they came.

I am Joseph, your brother, whom you sold into Egypt. He does not pretend it did not happen. He names it, plainly. You sold me. And then, in the same breath, now do not be grieved, nor angry with yourselves. He had watched them for weeks. He had heard them say we are guilty. He had seen all eleven turn around on the road. He had heard Judah offer his life. He knew they were already carrying as much grief as men can carry. And he set it down for them.

Here are the old words that follow.

God sent me before you to preserve for you a remnant in the earth, and to save you alive by a great deliverance. So now it wasn't you who sent me here, but God, and he has made me a father to Pharaoh, lord of all his house, and ruler over all the land of Egypt.

It was not you who sent me here, but God. I want to be careful with that line, because it is easy to misuse. Joseph is not saying the brothers did nothing wrong. He has just said, you sold me. He is saying something harder and more generous than that. He is saying that the evil they did has been folded, by a hand larger than theirs, into a story that saved thousands of lives, including their own. The pit was real. The prison was real. And the famine was real too, and there was grain in Egypt because a boy had been thrown into a pit. He is not excusing them. He is telling them that their worst act did not get the last word.

Then he gave them instructions, quickly, like a man who has been planning this for a long time. Hurry, go up to my father, and tell him, your son Joseph says this. God has made me lord of all Egypt. Come down to me, do not wait. You shall live in the land of Goshen, and you will be near me, you and your children and your children's children, your flocks and herds and all that you have. There I will provide for you, for there are still five years of famine. And behold, your eyes see, and the eyes of my brother Benjamin, that it is my mouth that speaks to you. You shall tell my father of all my glory in Egypt, and of all that you have seen. You shall hurry and bring my father down here.

And he fell on his brother Benjamin's neck and wept, and Benjamin wept on his neck. And he kissed all his brothers, and wept on them. And after that, his brothers talked with him.

After that, his brothers talked with him. Twenty-two years ago the story said they could not speak peaceably to him. Now, after the weeping, they talked. It is the quietest line in the chapter and it may be the most important.

The news reached Pharaoh's house, Joseph's brothers have come, and it pleased Pharaoh well. Pharaoh said to Joseph, tell your brothers, load your animals and go to the land of Canaan. Take your father and your households and come to me, and I will give you the good of the land of Egypt. Take wagons out of Egypt for your little ones and your wives, and bring your father. Do not worry about your belongings, for the good of all the land of Egypt is yours.

So Joseph gave them wagons, and provisions for the way, and to each of them he gave changes of clothing. But to Benjamin he gave three hundred pieces of silver and five changes of clothing. And to his father he sent ten donkeys loaded with the good things of Egypt, and ten female donkeys loaded with grain and bread and provision for the way. And he sent his brothers away, and as they left he said one more thing to them, and it is the kind of thing only a brother would say.

See that you do not quarrel on the way.

He knew them. He knew that somewhere on the road north, one of them would say, this was your idea, and another would say, you held the rope. So he told them, like a father telling children in the back of a cart, do not fight on the way home.

And they went up out of Egypt, and came into the land of Canaan, to Jacob their father. And they told him, Joseph is still alive, and he is ruler over all the land of Egypt. And his heart fainted, for he did not believe them.

He did not believe them. Why would he? These were the men who had brought him a bloody coat. But they told him all the words of Joseph, and when he saw the wagons which Joseph had sent to carry him, the spirit of Jacob their father revived. And Israel said, it is enough. Joseph my son is still alive. I will go and see him before I die.

It is enough. An old man who had refused to be comforted for twenty-two years, who had said all these things are against me, looking at a line of Egyptian wagons in front of his tent and saying, it is enough.

That is where we rest tonight. With the wagons standing in the dust outside Hebron, and an old man's spirit revived, and a long road south ahead of him, at the end of which there is a face he was told he would never see again.

Let it be enough for you too, tonight. Whatever you have been refusing to be comforted about. The wagons are at the door.
