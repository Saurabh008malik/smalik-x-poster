## Chapter 4: Two Dreams in a Prison

Some time after Joseph was put in prison, two new prisoners arrived, and they were not ordinary men. One was the chief cupbearer of the king of Egypt, the man who tasted Pharaoh's wine and handed him the cup. The other was the chief baker, who was in charge of Pharaoh's bread. Both of them had offended their lord in some way the story does not explain, and Pharaoh was angry with them, and had them put in custody in the house of the captain of the guard, in the same prison where Joseph was bound.

The captain of the guard assigned Joseph to them, and he served them. The former overseer of a great house, now waiting on two disgraced courtiers in a cell. And they were there, the text says, for a while.

Then one night they both dreamed. Each man his own dream, in the same night, and each dream with its own meaning. And in the morning Joseph came in to them and saw that they were troubled. He asked them, why do you look so sad today?

That question tells you something about Joseph. He had every reason to be wrapped up in his own misery. Instead he noticed two other men's faces and asked what was wrong. It is a small thing. The whole rest of his life turns on it.

Here are the old words.

They said to him, "We have dreamed a dream, and there is no one who can interpret it." Joseph said to them, "Don't interpretations belong to God? Please tell it to me."

Do not interpretations belong to God. Years ago, Joseph had told his own dreams as though they belonged to him, and his brothers had hated him for it. Now, older and lower, he says something different. The meaning is not mine. It is God's. But tell it to me anyway. He has not lost his gift. He has lost his pride about it, which is a different thing entirely.

The cupbearer went first. In my dream, he said, a vine was in front of me, and in the vine were three branches. It was as though it budded, its blossoms came out, and its clusters brought forth ripe grapes. Pharaoh's cup was in my hand. And I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh's cup, and gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand.

Joseph said, this is its interpretation. The three branches are three days. Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your office. You will give Pharaoh's cup into his hand, the way you used to when you were his cupbearer.

And then Joseph asked for something. The only time in the whole story he asks anyone for anything for himself. Remember me, he said, when it is well with you, and please show kindness to me, and make mention of me to Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house. For indeed I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews, and here also I have done nothing that they should put me into the dungeon.

Stolen away. He does not say sold by my brothers. He does not tell the story against them, even here, even to a stranger, even when it would cost nothing. He only says, I was taken, and I have done nothing wrong, and please remember me.

The chief baker had been listening, and he liked how the first dream had gone, so he told his. I also was in my dream, he said, and behold, three baskets of white bread were on my head. In the top basket there were all kinds of baked food for Pharaoh, and the birds ate them out of the basket on my head.

And Joseph had to tell him the truth. This is its interpretation. The three baskets are three days. Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you, and will hang you on a tree, and the birds will eat your flesh from off you.

The same phrase, lift up your head, used two ways. For one man it meant restoration. For the other it meant something very different. Joseph did not soften it. A gift for seeing the truth is not much of a gift if you only use it to say pleasant things.

Three days later, it was Pharaoh's birthday, and he made a feast for all his servants. And he lifted up the head of the chief cupbearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants. He restored the chief cupbearer to his position, and he gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand. And he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them.

And then the chapter ends with one sentence, and it is a sentence that anyone who has ever waited for a phone call will recognize.

Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph, but forgot him.

Not did not remember. The text says it twice, to make sure you feel it. Did not remember, but forgot. The man went back to the palace, back to the wine and the feasts and the sunlight, and the Hebrew in the prison who had told him his future simply slipped out of his mind. It is such an ordinary betrayal. He did not mean any harm. He was just busy, and glad to be free, and Joseph was a sad story from a bad time, and he let it go.

And Joseph waited. The next chapter will tell you how long. Two full years. Two more years in the prison, running it well, serving whoever came through the door, with no word from the palace and no sign that his one request had gone anywhere at all.

I want to sit in those two years for a moment, because the story does, and because they may be the most important part. Nothing happens in them. That is the point. Joseph had done everything right. He had been faithful in the house. He had been faithful in the prison. He had used his gift honestly and asked, humbly, for help. And the answer, for two years, was silence.

If you are in a silent stretch tonight, waiting on something that has not come, you are in the same cell Joseph was in. The story does not pretend the waiting is easy or short. It only promises that the silence is not the end. Somewhere in a palace upriver, a man is going to wake from a dream, and it will be the kind of dream that no one in Egypt can explain, and a cupbearer is going to clear his throat and say, I remember my faults today.

But not yet. For now, the river moves past in the dark, and the prison is quiet, and a man who has been forgotten is sleeping, and does not know that the forgetting is almost over.
