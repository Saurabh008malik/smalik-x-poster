## Chapter 10: A Rumour from Syria

In the spring of the year one hundred and seventy-five, a rumour began to spread through the eastern provinces of the empire. It said that Marcus Aurelius was dead.

It was not true. Marcus was alive, though often unwell, and still at work on the northern frontier. But news travelled slowly in the ancient world, carried by riders and ships over hundreds of miles, and a false report could run a long way before anyone could stop it. And in Syria, the rumour reached Avidius Cassius.

We met Cassius earlier in our story. He was the tough, gifted general who had led the Roman armies deep into Parthia, years before. Since then he had been given great authority over the eastern provinces. He was respected, feared, and very ambitious. When he heard that the emperor was dead, he had himself proclaimed emperor in his place. Much of the east, including Egypt, the great granary of Rome, accepted him.

Why did he do it? Did he truly believe that Marcus had died, and act quickly to keep order? Or did he use the rumour as an excuse for something he had long wanted? Ancient writers were not sure, and neither are we. There was even a rumour, repeated by later writers, that Faustina herself had encouraged him, fearing that her husband was dying and that her young son Commodus would be left unprotected. Many historians doubt this story, and we cannot know the truth of it. It is one of those dark whispers that clings to powerful families, and we will let it lie in the shadows, where it belongs.

What we do know is how Marcus responded when the news reached him on the Danube. He did not rage. According to the historian Cassius Dio, he gathered his soldiers and spoke to them calmly. He said, more or less, that he was not angry so much as grieved. He said that the one reward he wished for from the whole affair was the chance to forgive Cassius, to show that even a man who had broken faith could be treated with mercy, and to remain his friend.

He began to prepare for the long journey east. He made peace on the Danube as best he could, and set out. But before he could arrive, the whole rebellion collapsed. Cassius had reigned for about three months. Then one of his own officers, a centurion, killed him, and sent his head to Marcus.

Dio says that Marcus would not look at it, and ordered it to be buried. And he says something else. When the letters and papers of Cassius were brought to the emperor, papers that might have shown which senators and officials had secretly supported the rebellion, Marcus had them burned without reading them. He did not want to know. He did not want to be forced to punish anyone, or to live the rest of his life suspecting his friends.

It is one of the most remarkable things that any ruler of the ancient world is reported to have done. In an age when a failed rebellion was usually followed by a long and bloody hunt for every accomplice, Marcus chose not to look.

In his notebook, there are many lines about anger, and about forgiveness. He reminds himself, again and again, that those who do wrong do so because they do not understand what is good. He tells himself that the best way to avenge yourself is not to become like the one who wronged you. He reminds himself to be gentle with people, and to teach them if he can, and to bear with them if he cannot. Reading those lines, and then reading of the burned letters, we can see a man trying, at least, to live by what he wrote.

Even though the rebellion was over, Marcus went on with his journey to the east, to restore order and to show himself to the people of those provinces. Faustina travelled with him, and so did their son Commodus, who was now about fourteen.

They passed through the mountains of Asia Minor, the land we call Turkey today, travelling slowly through the high plateau of Cappadocia, a strange and beautiful country of bare hills and wide skies. And there, in the winter of one hundred and seventy-five, in a small village called Halala at the foot of the Taurus Mountains, Faustina fell ill and died.

She was about forty-five years old. She had been married to Marcus for thirty years. She had borne him many children, and buried many of them. She had followed him to the army camps on the cold northern frontier, where the soldiers had called her Mother of the Camp.

Marcus grieved for her deeply. He asked the Senate to declare her a goddess, and they did. He raised the little village where she died to the rank of a city, and named it after her: Faustinopolis, the city of Faustina. In Rome, he founded a new charity in her name, to support young girls from poor families, as had once been done in memory of her mother. And there are coins and inscriptions honouring her, from all over the empire.

He also, it seems, wrote to the Senate asking that no one connected with the rebellion of Cassius should be put to death. One ancient account says that he wished that no senator should be executed in his reign at all. We may picture him, on the long road through the mountains, with an empty place beside him in the carriage, writing letters asking for mercy for others.

There is a line in his notebook, in the list of his thanks to the gods, that we heard earlier tonight. He thanked them for a wife who was so affectionate, and so simple. It is impossible to know exactly when he wrote those words. Perhaps it was before she died, while she was still with him in the camps by the Danube. Perhaps it was after. Either way, they are the words he chose to keep.

Let us rest here, at the edge of the Taurus Mountains, on a winter evening long ago. The sky is wide and pale, and the first stars are appearing over the bare hills. In a small village, lamps are burning, and a man sits quietly beside the one he has lost. Tomorrow the journey will go on. But for tonight, there is only stillness, and the gentle hush of a winter evening.
