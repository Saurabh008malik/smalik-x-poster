## Chapter 2: A Rough Cloak and a Plank Bed

The education of a well-born Roman boy in those days was long and thorough. It began with reading and writing, in both Latin and Greek, because every educated Roman was expected to move easily between the two languages. Latin was the language of the law courts, the Senate and the army. Greek was the language of philosophy, of science, of poetry and of the great old books. A cultured man was supposed to be at home in both, like someone who lives in a house with two gardens and walks freely from one to the other.

So the young Marcus learned his letters, and then his grammar, and then he began to read the poets. He had teachers for Greek and teachers for Latin, a teacher of music and a teacher of geometry. And he had a teacher of painting, a man named Diognetus, who seems to have mattered to him far more than an ordinary drawing master.

Diognetus taught him more than how to mix colours. In his notebook, Marcus remembered him with real warmth. From Diognetus, he wrote, he learned not to busy himself with trifling things, and not to believe the miracle workers and conjurors who claimed they could drive away spirits with spells. He learned not to keep quails for fighting, which was a popular pastime among boys and idle men. He learned to put up with plain speaking. And through Diognetus he first came close to philosophy. He even began, as a boy, to write little dialogues of his own, in the manner of the old Greek philosophers.

And then he added a lovely detail. From Diognetus, he wrote, he came to desire a plank bed and a skin, and whatever else of that kind belongs to the Greek discipline.

What does that mean? In the ancient world, a philosopher was not only someone who thought deep thoughts. He was someone who lived in a particular way. The serious philosophers, especially the followers of the Stoic school, tried to live simply and hardily. They wore a rough cloak. They ate plain food. They slept on hard beds. They did this not to punish themselves, but to practise needing very little, so that nothing the world took away from them could shake them.

There is a story about this, told in a collection of imperial lives written long after Marcus died, a book that historians treat with some caution. According to that account, when Marcus was about twelve years old, he took up the dress of a philosopher, and began to study and to sleep on the ground. His mother, the story says, could hardly bear it, and only with great difficulty persuaded him to sleep on a little couch spread with skins. We cannot be sure the story is true in every detail. But it fits so well with what Marcus himself wrote about the plank bed and the skin, that it is easy to picture. A serious boy of twelve, in a rough cloak, lying down on the floor of a rich man's house on the Caelian Hill, while his mother stands in the doorway with a lamp, sighing, and begging him to at least take a cushion.

During these same years, Marcus caught the eye of the emperor himself.

Hadrian was a frequent visitor in the great houses of Rome, when he was not away on his long journeys through the provinces. And somehow he came to know this solemn, truthful boy. The same later collection of lives says that Hadrian, playing on the family name Verus, which means true, used to call him Verissimus, the most true. Whether or not Hadrian really said it, the nickname seems to have followed Marcus, and later writers used it of him too.

Hadrian showed the boy favour in other ways. At the age of six, Marcus was enrolled among the knights, the second great order of Roman society. At the age of eight, he was made a member of the Salian priests, an ancient college of priests of the war god Mars. The Salii were famous for a strange old ceremony. Each year, in March, they processed through the city in old-fashioned armour, carrying sacred shields, leaping and dancing and chanting a hymn so ancient that by Marcus's time hardly anyone understood its words. One can imagine a small boy in bronze armour, solemnly hopping through the streets of Rome in the spring sunshine, singing words no one could translate. It was a great honour, and Marcus took it very seriously. The later account says that he learned all the chants by heart, and performed every duty of the priesthood himself.

As he grew into his teens, his education moved on to its higher stages. In Rome, the crown of a young man's learning was rhetoric, the art of speaking well in public. A Roman leader was expected to stand up in the Senate or the law courts and persuade other men with words. So Marcus would, in time, be given the very best teachers of speech in the empire, men we will meet properly a little later.

But even now, as a teenager, his heart was turning in a different direction. He was drawn not so much to fine speaking as to the quieter questions underneath it. How should a person live? What is truly good, and what only seems good? What do we owe to other people? What can we control, and what must we simply accept?

These were the great questions of Stoic philosophy, and they would stay with him all his life. The Stoics taught that the world is ordered by a kind of divine reason, and that each of us is a small part of one great whole. They taught that the only truly good thing is to have a good character, to be just and brave and wise and moderate. Wealth, fame, comfort and even health, they said, are not truly good or bad in themselves. What matters is how we use them, and how we respond when they come and go. And they taught that all human beings are, in a sense, citizens of one great city, the whole world, and so we owe kindness even to strangers.

For a boy who would one day hold more wealth and fame than almost anyone alive, these were strange and powerful lessons. They were lessons about holding everything lightly.

Let us picture him, then, at fifteen, in the house on the Caelian Hill. It is evening, and the household is quiet. He has finished his lessons for the day. Somewhere a slave is trimming the lamps, and the smell of olive oil hangs in the air. Marcus is sitting by a window with a scroll open on his knees, reading, slowly and carefully, a book of philosophy. He does not know, and nobody yet knows, that within a year or two his whole life will be turned upside down. Let us leave him reading there, in the soft lamplight, a little longer, before the great change comes.
