## Chapter 8: A Sickness from the East

The sickness seems to have broken out among the Roman armies in the east around the year one hundred and sixty-five, during the last campaigns of the Parthian war. As the legions marched home, it travelled with them, along the roads and across the sea, from camp to town and town to city. By the year one hundred and sixty-six, it had reached Rome.

We call it today the Antonine Plague, after the family name of the emperors. We do not know exactly what disease it was. Many modern scholars think, from the descriptions that survive, that it may have been smallpox, though no one can be completely certain. What we do know is that it was terrible. It brought fever and sores and great suffering, and it killed a very large number of people. Estimates vary widely, and we should be cautious with them. But it may have taken the lives of millions across the empire over the following years, and it would return again and again for more than a decade.

One of the people who left an account of it was a doctor named Galen. Galen was a Greek from the city of Pergamum, and he was the most famous physician of his age, a brilliant, prickly, tireless man who wrote enormous numbers of books on medicine. He described the fever, the dark rash, the course of the disease, in careful, clinical language. Later, Galen would become a personal physician to the imperial family, and he wrote warmly, in his own way, about Marcus as a patient.

We should not try to picture the plague too closely tonight. It is enough to know that it came, and that it brought great sorrow to many homes, rich and poor alike. In Rome, there were so many dead that the ordinary ways of burial broke down. The army lost many soldiers. Farms lost their workers. Towns lost their councillors and their craftsmen. It was a grief that touched almost every family in the empire.

How did Marcus respond? He did what a Roman emperor was expected to do. He carried out religious rites to ask the gods for mercy. He arranged for the dead to be buried, and the later collection of imperial lives says that he paid for the funerals of poorer people out of public funds. He tried to keep the government working while so many of its people were sick or dying.

And in his notebook, we can perhaps feel the shadow of these years. He writes, many times, about death. Not with fear, but with a kind of steady, gentle attention. He reminds himself how short life is, how quickly everything passes, how many famous people have come and gone, and how even the great cities of the past have vanished. How small a part of boundless time, he writes, is given to each person, for it is very soon swallowed up in the eternal. These thoughts may sound gloomy at first. But for Marcus, they were not meant to bring despair. They were meant to bring calm. If everything passes, then there is no need to cling. There is only the present moment, and the chance, in this moment, to be kind and to do one's work well.

Meanwhile, to the north, another danger was gathering.

All along the great river Danube, which formed much of the empire's northern border, there lived peoples whom the Romans called Germans and Sarmatians. There were the Marcomanni and the Quadi, in the lands that are today the Czech Republic and Slovakia. There were the Iazyges, horsemen of the great plain of Hungary. And beyond them, further north and east, other peoples were moving, pushing the tribes on the frontier southward. For years there had been small raids and quarrels. Now, with the Roman armies weakened by the plague and by the war in the east, the pressure on the Danube grew stronger and stronger.

In the year one hundred and sixty-eight, Marcus and Lucius set out together for the north. It was the first time in his life that Marcus, now forty-seven years old, had left Italy. He was not a soldier by nature. He was a scholar and a judge, a man of books and quiet rooms. But he believed it was his duty to go, and so he went.

The two emperors made their headquarters at Aquileia, a great city at the head of the Adriatic Sea, near the passes through the eastern Alps. From there they worked to strengthen the frontier, to raise new troops and to restore order. But the plague was in the camps too, and it was killing soldiers in great numbers. Marcus summoned Galen to come and care for the army. And as the winter came on, the emperors decided to return to Rome.

They set out together in the winter of one hundred and sixty-eight going into one hundred and sixty-nine. And on the road, near the town of Altinum, not far from where Venice stands today, Lucius was suddenly taken ill as they travelled in the same carriage. Ancient writers say it was a kind of stroke. He lost the power of speech, and a few days later he died. He was only thirty-eight years old.

Marcus brought his brother's body back to Rome, and gave him a splendid funeral, and the Senate, at his request, declared Lucius a god. Ancient gossips, who loved nothing better than a dark rumour, later whispered that Marcus might somehow have been responsible for Lucius's death. Historians today dismiss these stories. There is no good evidence for them at all, and they fit nothing we know of Marcus's character. More likely, Lucius was simply another man struck down in a hard season, on a cold road, far from home.

Then, in that same year, there came a deeper grief. One of Marcus's own sons, a little boy of seven named Marcus Annius Verus, became ill with a growth beneath his ear. The doctors operated, and the child died. The later account says that Marcus mourned him for only five days, and then returned to his public duties, because the business of the state could not wait. It is a story that may make us sad, rather than admiring. But perhaps that is how he bore it. Work, for Marcus, was a way of carrying on when there was nothing else to do.

His notebook says nothing directly about this boy. But there are lines in it about the loss of children that are hard to read without thinking of him. He writes, in one place, that a person might pray, not, may I not lose my little child, but rather, may I not be afraid to lose him. It is a hard teaching. Perhaps he found that he could not quite live up to it. Perhaps that is why he kept writing it down.

So now Marcus was alone at the head of the empire. His father was gone, his brother was gone, and one more small boy was gone. The plague was still in the cities, and across the Danube, the tribes were growing bolder. And in the spring, he would have to go north once more, this time for many years.

Let us rest here, in the quiet of a house in mourning, where the lamps burn late and the doors are hung with dark cloth. In the next part of our story, we will go north with him, to the cold camps beside the great river, where he began to write.
