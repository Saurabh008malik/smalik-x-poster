## Chapter 9: Winters by the Danube

The wars that filled the next years of Marcus's life are known today as the Marcomannic Wars, after one of the peoples who fought in them. They went on, with pauses, for most of the rest of his reign. And they were among the hardest wars Rome had faced in a very long time.

Around the year one hundred and seventy, though the exact date is still debated, a great force of Marcomanni and Quadi broke through the Roman defences on the upper Danube. They crossed the Alps and came down into northern Italy itself. They besieged the city of Aquileia and destroyed the town of Opitergium. It had been centuries since an enemy army had stood on Italian soil. The shock in Rome must have been enormous.

The empire was short of everything. The plague had emptied the ranks of the army, and the treasury had been drained by the war in the east. Marcus raised new troops wherever he could find them. And when it came to money, he did something that later writers remembered with admiration. Rather than impose heavy new taxes on the provinces, the later accounts say, he held a great public sale in the Forum of Trajan in Rome. For two months, the treasures of the imperial palace were auctioned off: gold and crystal cups, fine statues and paintings, silk garments embroidered with gold, even the jewels of the empress. Afterwards, the story goes, he offered to let anyone who wished return what they had bought and have their money back, but he forced no one to do so. Whether every detail is exact, we cannot say. But the story tells us what people believed about him. He would rather sell his own possessions than squeeze his people.

Then he went north, to the frontier. And for most of the next several years he lived not in his palace in Rome, but in army camps and frontier towns along the Danube.

One of the most important of these places was Carnuntum. It stood on the southern bank of the Danube, about halfway between modern Vienna and Bratislava. It was a large legionary fortress, with a busy town growing up beside it, with an amphitheatre, baths and streets of houses. Marcus made his headquarters there for about three years. Later, he also spent long periods at Sirmium, further south and east, in what is today Serbia.

And it was here, in these camps, that he wrote much of his notebook.

We know this, at least in part, from two small headings that have survived in the old manuscripts. At the end of the first book, there is a single line. Among the Quadi at the Granua. The Granua is a river, today called the Hron, which flows down through Slovakia into the Danube. And at the end of the second book, there is another short line. This in Carnuntum.

That is all. Two little notes, telling us where he was. But they are enough to let us picture it.

Imagine a winter night in a Roman camp on the northern frontier. The cold is bitter. The great river, half frozen, moves slowly in the dark. The wooden watchtowers stand black against a sky full of stars. Inside the camp, rows of tents and wooden barracks, the smell of smoke from a thousand small fires, the stamping of horses, the low voices of sentries calling the watch. And in the commander's quarters, in a plain room warmed by a brazier, a tired man of about fifty sits at a small table, with a lamp beside him. He has spent the day in councils of war, in hearing reports, in judging disputes, in writing letters to Rome. Now, at last, the day is over. And he takes up his pen, and he writes, in Greek, a few lines to himself.

What did he write? Not a history of the war. Not a record of battles. Almost nothing about the fighting at all. Instead, he wrote reminders. How to begin the day. How to deal with difficult people. How to keep calm. How to remember that everything passes. How to do his duty without complaint.

In the morning, he wrote to himself, in a famous passage, when you rise unwillingly, let this thought be present. In the old translation it goes like this. I am rising to the work of a human being. Why then am I dissatisfied, if I am going to do the things for which I exist, and for which I was brought into the world? Or have I been made for this, to lie in the bedclothes and keep myself warm?

It is a lovely, gentle, almost funny passage. A Roman emperor, in a freezing camp on the Danube, arguing with himself about getting out of bed. Most of us have had that argument on a cold morning. It is comforting to know that he had it too. And tonight, of course, you are excused from it entirely. Tonight you are allowed to lie in the bedclothes and keep yourself warm, for as long as you like.

Another passage begins in a similar way. Begin the morning, he tells himself, by saying, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious and the unsocial. It sounds rather gloomy. But he goes on to say that such people act as they do because they do not know what is truly good. And that he himself is akin to them, part of the same human family, and so he cannot be harmed by them, nor be angry with them, nor hate them. We are made, he says, for cooperation, like the feet, like the hands, like the eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth.

There is a famous story from these years, too, though it is hard to know exactly what happened. At some point in the war, perhaps around the year one hundred and seventy-two, a Roman army found itself surrounded and suffering terribly from thirst in the summer heat. And then, quite suddenly, a great storm broke over them, with heavy rain that saved the soldiers, while lightning and hail fell on their enemies. The Romans called it a miracle. The scene was carved, a few years later, on the great column of Marcus Aurelius that still stands in Rome, showing a strange, solemn rain god with outstretched arms, and water pouring down from his wings and beard. Different people in later times gave different explanations, giving the credit to different gods, or to the prayers of different soldiers. We do not need to decide tonight. Only to picture the rain, falling at last on a thirsty army, and the soldiers holding up their helmets and shields to catch it.

The war went on, year after year. There were victories and setbacks, treaties made and broken. Little by little, the Romans pushed the invaders back across the Danube, and made peace with one people after another. It was slow, grinding, exhausting work. And through it all, Marcus kept writing his notes, a few lines at a time, by lamplight, to himself.

Somewhere around these years, too, his old teacher Fronto died. We do not know exactly when, or where Marcus was when he heard the news. But we can imagine a letter arriving, and a man reading it alone, and remembering a long-ago autumn day of grape picking, and a letter written at bedtime to a beloved friend.

Let us leave him there, by the river, in the long northern night. The fire in the brazier is burning low. The pen is resting on the table. And outside, the snow is falling softly on the tents of the sleeping camp.
