## Chapter 4: Letters to Fronto

As the heir to the empire, Marcus was given the finest teachers that Rome and Greece could offer. For Greek speech, he studied with Herodes Atticus, an Athenian of enormous wealth and fame, who built theatres and fountains across Greece and was one of the most celebrated speakers of the age. For Latin speech, his teacher was Marcus Cornelius Fronto, a man from the city of Cirta in North Africa, who had risen to become the most admired Latin orator in Rome.

Fronto is a particularly lovely figure in our story, and the reason is a lucky accident of history.

For more than a thousand years, everyone assumed that the private letters between Fronto and Marcus were lost forever. Then, in the early eighteen hundreds, an Italian scholar named Angelo Mai was studying old manuscripts in a library in Milan, and later in the Vatican. Some of these manuscripts were palimpsests. A palimpsest is a page of parchment that has been used twice. In the Middle Ages, parchment was precious, so monks would sometimes scrape away an old text and write a new one over the top. But the old writing often left faint traces underneath. And underneath the later writing on some of these pages, Angelo Mai found the letters of Fronto and Marcus Aurelius.

The letters are damaged and incomplete, with many gaps. But what survives is precious, because it lets us hear the voice of the young Marcus, not as a stern emperor, but as a warm, affectionate, sometimes silly young man writing to a teacher he loved.

And he did love him. The letters are full of endearments. Marcus calls Fronto his dearest master, and tells him how much he misses him. He worries about Fronto's health, for Fronto suffered terribly from pains in his joints and was often ill. Fronto, in turn, writes back with fondness and with fussing, giving advice on words and phrases, scolding gently, praising generously. They talk about their colds and their sore throats, about the weather, about books they are reading, and about the little events of family life.

In one of the most charming letters, Marcus describes a single day in the country, during the grape harvest, at a family estate outside Rome. He tells his teacher, more or less, how the day went. He had a slight cold, so he slept a little late, and then read part of an old book about farming and wrote for a while. He soothed his throat with honey and water, and went to stand beside his father at a morning sacrifice. At lunch, while the others ate beans and onions and little fish, he had only a bit of bread. Then they went out among the vines, and worked and sweated and called out cheerfully as the grapes were gathered. He came home, tried to study, and did not get very far. Then he had a long talk with his mother as she sat on her couch, and the two of them teased each other about which of them loved their absent friend Fronto more. He bathed, and dined in the room where the grapes were pressed, listening to the country people joking with one another. And then he went home, wrote the whole day down for his teacher, and turned over on his side to sleep. It is an ordinary day. Nothing at all happens. But that is what makes it so beautiful to read. Across almost two thousand years, a young man tells his friend about a quiet autumn day, the sound of laughter in the vineyard, and a long talk with his mother at dusk.

Fronto wanted Marcus to become a great speaker. He believed that a ruler needed words, beautifully chosen and carefully arranged, to move the hearts of men. He drilled his pupil in finding just the right word, in rare old expressions, in the elegant turning of a sentence. And Marcus worked hard at it, for years.

But slowly, gently, Marcus began to drift away from rhetoric and towards philosophy. It was not that he stopped loving Fronto. It was that the questions of philosophy came to seem more important to him than the questions of style. Fine words could make a speech admired. But could they make a person good?

The teacher who did most to turn him in this new direction was a man named Quintus Junius Rusticus. Rusticus was a Stoic philosopher, and also a serious public man, who would later hold high offices in the state. He seems to have been quite different from the warm and fussy Fronto. He was plain, direct and demanding.

Marcus's notebook remembers Rusticus at length, and the list is revealing. From Rusticus, he wrote, he received the impression that his character needed improvement and discipline. He learned not to be led astray into showing off, not to write about speculative matters, not to give little sermons, and not to make a display of how disciplined or how generous he was. He learned to write his letters simply. He learned to forgive those who had offended him, as soon as they showed they wanted to make peace. He learned to read carefully, and not to be satisfied with a surface understanding of a book.

And then comes one of the most important sentences in the whole notebook. Marcus wrote that he was indebted to Rusticus for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus, which Rusticus lent him out of his own collection.

Epictetus had been a Greek philosopher who was born a slave, and who had lived and taught in the generation before Marcus. He had been lame, and poor, and he owned almost nothing. But his teaching was clear and strong and practical, and his pupil Arrian had written down his lessons. The heart of what Epictetus taught was very simple. Some things are within our power, and some are not. Our own judgements, our choices, our wishes, are ours. Our bodies, our possessions, our reputations, the actions of other people, are not. And so peace comes from caring for what is ours, and accepting calmly what is not.

Imagine the scene, if you like. A heavy, well-thumbed copy of these lessons, passed from the hands of an older philosopher into the hands of a young prince. Perhaps Rusticus said something gruff, like, read this properly, not quickly. And the young man took it home to the Palatine, and opened it by lamplight, and read the words of a former slave. Those words would shape the inner life of a Roman emperor for the rest of his days.

Marcus had other philosophy teachers too, and he remembered each of them with gratitude. There was Apollonius, from whom he learned steadiness of purpose, and to look to nothing but reason, and to be the same in sharp pains, in long illness, and even in the loss of a child. There was Sextus, a relative of the famous Greek writer Plutarch, from whom he learned kindness, and the example of a household governed with a father's love, and the idea of living in agreement with nature. And there was Claudius Maximus, from whom he learned self-control, and cheerfulness in every circumstance, including illness, and to do what was set before him without complaining.

It is worth pausing on that phrase, without complaining. Marcus did not write that Maximus taught him to enjoy everything. He did not pretend that life would always be pleasant. He simply noticed a man who did his work, whatever it was, and did not grumble. And he wanted to be like that.

So the years of his early manhood passed, in study and in duty, in letters to Fronto full of affection and complaints about colds, and in long evenings with the plain, strong words of Epictetus. Let us leave him there for a while, reading. Because in the next part of our story, at last, he will marry, and the house on the Palatine will begin to fill with the voices of children.
