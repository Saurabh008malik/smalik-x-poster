## Chapter 6: The Quiet Years of Antoninus

The reign of Antoninus Pius lasted for twenty-three years, from the summer of one hundred and thirty-eight until the spring of one hundred and sixty-one. And by the standards of Roman history, those years were remarkably quiet.

There were troubles, of course, on the distant edges of the empire. There was fighting in northern Britain, where a new wall of turf was built across the narrow neck of Scotland, north of the great stone wall of Hadrian. There were small disturbances in Africa and elsewhere. But there were no great civil wars, no terrible invasions, no dramatic conspiracies. Antoninus never left Italy as emperor. He governed the whole vast empire from Rome and from his country houses nearby, through letters and messengers, through governors he chose with care, and through patient, steady attention to detail.

Later generations looked back on these years as something like a golden afternoon. The roads were safe. Trade flowed along the rivers and across the sea. Cities in the provinces built theatres and baths and libraries. It was not paradise, and we should never forget the many people, the poor and the enslaved, for whom life was always hard. But for the empire as a whole, it was a time of unusual calm.

Through all these years, Marcus was at his adoptive father's side. And there is no doubt at all about what he thought of him. In the first book of his notebook, after he has thanked his grandfather and his mother and all his teachers, Marcus writes a long and loving portrait of Antoninus. It is the longest single passage in the whole book, and it is one of the most beautiful descriptions of a good ruler ever written.

He begins with these words, in the old translation. In my father I observed mildness of temper, and unchangeable resolution in the things which he had determined after due deliberation, and no vainglory in those things which men call honours.

And then the list goes on, and on, and on, one gentle quality after another, as if Marcus could not stop once he had started. Antoninus loved hard work and kept at it. He was ready to listen to anyone who had something useful to say. He gave every person what they deserved, firmly and fairly. He considered himself no more than any other citizen. He did not expect his friends to dine with him or to follow him about. He looked into every question carefully and did not stop at first appearances. He kept his friends, and did not tire of them. He was cheerful and content. He looked far ahead, and prepared for small things without fuss. He stopped flattery at once.

He was careful with public money, and patiently put up with the criticism this brought him. He did not try to win the crowd with gifts or flattery. He enjoyed the good things of life when he had them, without making a show of it, and when he did not have them, he did not miss them. He took sensible care of his health, so that he rarely needed a doctor. He did not take his bath at odd hours. He was not fond of building grand houses, nor fussy about his food, nor about the colour and texture of his clothes. His clothes, Marcus tells us, came from Lorium, his villa near the coast, and from Lanuvium, the little town in the hills where he had been born.

And then comes a line that is very touching in its simplicity. Marcus writes that there was nothing harsh about Antoninus, nothing violent, nothing carried, as he put it, to the sweating point. He examined everything, one thing at a time, as if he had plenty of time, without confusion, in an orderly way, steadily and consistently.

As if he had plenty of time. Imagine being the ruler of an empire of many millions of people, and still going about your work as if you had plenty of time. That was what Marcus admired. Not brilliance. Not glory. Calm.

Elsewhere in the notebook, Marcus gives himself an instruction. Do everything, he tells himself, as a disciple of Antoninus. Remember his steadiness, his evenness in all things, the serenity of his face, his gentleness. Remember how he would never let a matter go until he had looked into it fully. How he put up with people who blamed him unfairly. How he was never in a hurry. How little he needed, in lodging and bed and clothes and food, and how he could keep at his work all day long until the evening.

So we can picture these years in a soft and steady light. Antoninus at his country house at Lorium, a few miles outside Rome, with its gardens and its fields. The emperor walking through the olive groves in the morning, reading letters at midday, receiving visitors in the afternoon. Marcus beside him, learning by watching, more than by being told. Fronto visiting when his aching joints allowed. Faustina and the children in the next courtyard. Harvests coming in, festivals passing, one year folding quietly into the next.

But no afternoon lasts forever, however golden it may be.

In the early spring of the year one hundred and sixty-one, Antoninus fell ill at Lorium. He was seventy-four years old. According to the later collection of imperial lives, he had eaten some rich cheese at dinner one evening, and during the night he was sick, and the next day he had a fever. Over the next days he grew weaker. When he understood that he was dying, he summoned his friends and his officials, and in their presence he formally commended the state, and his daughter, to Marcus. He ordered that the golden statue of the goddess Fortune, which always stood in the bedroom of the emperor, should be carried to the bedroom of Marcus.

Then, the account says, the officer of the guard came to ask for the watchword for the night, as he did every evening. And Antoninus gave him one word. Aequanimitas. Equanimity. Calmness of mind. And then he turned over, as if to sleep, and he died.

We cannot be sure that this story is true in every detail. It comes from a later and sometimes careless book. But if it is true, it is hard to imagine a more fitting last word for that steady, patient old man. And if it is only a story, it is a story that people told because it felt true to who he was.

Antoninus Pius died on the seventh of March, in the year one hundred and sixty-one. He was mourned sincerely, and the Senate declared him a god, as he had once asked them to do for Hadrian.

Marcus was now nearly forty years old. For twenty-three years he had been the heir. Now, at last, the whole weight of the empire had come to rest upon his shoulders.

Let us pause here, in the hush after a long life has ended. The house at Lorium is quiet. The lamps are burning low. Somewhere a golden statue is being carried carefully down a corridor, from one bedroom to another. And a middle-aged man sits alone for a while in the dark, thinking of the father who taught him to be calm, before he rises and goes out to meet the world.
