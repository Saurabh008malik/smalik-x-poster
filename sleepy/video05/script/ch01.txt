## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Boy on the Caelian Hill

Welcome. Let us settle in together. If you can, dim the lights a little, and let your shoulders loosen. There is nothing you need to do tonight. You do not have to remember any names, and you do not have to keep track of any dates. You can close your eyes whenever you like, and the story will carry on quietly beside you, like a lamp left burning in the next room. We will go slowly, and we will not hurry anywhere.

Tonight we will keep company with Marcus Aurelius. He was an emperor of Rome, and for almost twenty years he ruled one of the largest states the ancient world had ever known. But he is remembered today for something much smaller than an empire. He is remembered for a little private notebook. In the evenings, often far from home, in army camps beside cold northern rivers, he wrote short notes to himself in Greek. He wrote about patience, about kindness, about getting out of bed in the morning, and about how to stay calm when the world is loud. He never meant anyone else to read them. And yet, nearly two thousand years later, people still keep that notebook by their beds.

So this is the story of a man who had almost every kind of power, and who spent his quiet hours trying to become a little gentler and a little wiser. It is a long story, and parts of it are sad, because his years on the throne were years of war and sickness. But it is also a story of teachers and friends, of letters and books, of a father he admired, and of a mind that kept returning, again and again, to stillness. We will try to tell it honestly. Where a tale comes only from a later and less reliable writer, we will say so.

Let us begin in the spring of the year one hundred and twenty-one, in the city of Rome.

Rome in those days was enormous. Perhaps a million people lived there, crowded into narrow streets and tall apartment blocks, with markets and temples and bathhouses, and great aqueducts striding in from the hills to carry fresh water into the city. The emperor at that time was Hadrian, a restless, clever, complicated man who loved Greek culture and who spent years travelling around the provinces. The empire stretched from the misty north of Britain to the deserts of Egypt and Syria, and for the most part, in those years, it was at peace.

On the twenty-sixth of April, in the year one hundred and twenty-one, a boy was born into a wealthy and well-connected family. His name at birth was Marcus Annius Verus. The name Marcus Aurelius, by which we know him, would come much later, after a great change in his life. For now, he was simply a child of the Annii, a family that had come to Rome from the province of Spain, and had risen high.

His father, also called Marcus Annius Verus, held public office in the city. But his father died young, when the little boy was only about three years old. Marcus could hardly have remembered him. Many years later, in his private notebook, he wrote that from the reputation and remembrance of his father he had learned modesty and a manly character. It is a touching line, if you think about it. He did not say from his father. He said from the memory of him, from what other people told him his father had been like.

His mother was Domitia Lucilla. She was a woman of great wealth, who had inherited, among other things, a large brick-making business near Rome. Bricks may not sound very romantic, but in a city that was always building, they were worth a great deal. Lucilla was also, by all accounts, a thoughtful and educated woman, and her son loved her deeply. In his notebook he wrote that from his mother he learned piety and generosity, and to avoid not only doing wrong, but even thinking of doing wrong, and to live simply, far removed from the habits of the rich. Those are unusual words to write about a woman who owned half the brickyards near Rome. But they tell us something of the household he grew up in.

Marcus also had a younger sister, Annia Cornificia. The two children were raised in Rome, and after their father's death, the boy was brought up largely in the house of his grandfather, another Marcus Annius Verus. This grandfather was one of the most distinguished men in the city. He had been consul three times, which was a very great honour, and he had served as prefect of Rome, the official who kept order in the capital.

The grandfather's house stood on the Caelian Hill, one of the seven hills of Rome. The Caelian was a pleasant, leafy place, a little away from the noise and smoke of the crowded valleys below. Great families had their houses there, with courtyards and gardens, cool rooms with painted walls, and fountains that splashed quietly in the afternoon. We can imagine, though we cannot know for certain, a small boy running through those gardens in the warm Roman evenings, past clipped bushes and marble benches, with the sound of the city drifting up from below.

From his grandfather, Marcus wrote, he learned good morals and how to govern his temper. It is the very first line of his notebook. Of all the lessons of his whole life, that is the one he chose to put first. Be good, and keep your temper. It is simple advice. Most of us spend our lives trying to follow it, and most of us, like Marcus, find it harder than it sounds.

Now, in those days, a boy from a family like his would usually be sent to a public school, or to the lecture rooms of teachers in the city. But Marcus was not. Instead, teachers came to him, at home. He remembered this too. From his great-grandfather, he wrote, he learned not to frequent the public schools, but to have good teachers at home, and to know that on such things a man should spend generously. So the house on the Caelian Hill became a little school of its own, with tutors arriving in the morning, with wax tablets and pens, with scrolls of Greek and Latin laid out on the tables.

There was also a man who looked after the boy day to day, a kind of guardian or governor. Marcus never tells us his name. But he tells us what he learned from him. Not to take sides in the chariot races, where the whole city cheered for the Greens or for the Blues. Not to become a fan of one kind of gladiator over another. To put up with hard work, to want little, to do things with his own hands, to mind his own business, and not to listen to gossip. It is a list that sounds almost like the advice of a kindly old servant to a rich child who might easily be spoiled. And perhaps that is exactly what it was.

So let us leave him there for now, a quiet, serious little boy in a great house on a green hill, with a mother who taught him to live simply, a grandfather who taught him to keep his temper, and a father he knew only through other people's memories. Outside the walls, Rome hummed and clattered, as it always did. Inside, the lamps were lit, and the lessons went on. Let us drift gently forward a few years, into his schooling, and see who came to teach him.
