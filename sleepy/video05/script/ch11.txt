## Chapter 11: Athens, and a Gift to Philosophy

After Faustina's death, Marcus continued his journey through the eastern provinces. He travelled through Syria and Egypt, showing himself to the cities that had briefly supported Cassius, and treating them, for the most part, with mildness. Then, in the year one hundred and seventy-six, on his way home to Rome, he came to Athens.

For a man like Marcus, Athens must have felt like a kind of homecoming, even though he had never been there before. All his life he had read the books of the Athenian philosophers. Socrates had walked these streets, asking his endless gentle questions. Plato had taught in the grove of the Academy, just outside the walls. Aristotle had walked and talked with his students in the Lyceum. And Zeno, the founder of the Stoic school, had taught in the Painted Porch beside the marketplace, the Stoa from which the Stoics took their name.

By Marcus's time, Athens was no longer a great power. It was a smaller, quieter city, living on its memories, a kind of university town of the ancient world, where young men from all over the empire came to study. But its temples still crowned the Acropolis, white and gold in the Greek sunlight, and its philosophers still argued in the porches and the gardens, as they had done for six hundred years.

Marcus did two things in Athens that people remembered.

First, he was initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries. These were ancient and secret rites, held each year at Eleusis, a short distance from Athens, in honour of the goddess Demeter and her daughter Persephone. They told, in some way we still do not fully understand, the story of the seasons, of loss and return, of the daughter carried down into the underworld and the mother's grief, and of the spring that comes back each year. Those who were initiated were said to lose their fear of death. What exactly Marcus saw and heard in the great hall at Eleusis, we will never know. The initiates kept their secret well.

The second thing was a gift. Marcus established, at Athens, salaried teaching posts in philosophy, one for each of the four great schools: the followers of Plato, the followers of Aristotle, the Stoics and the Epicureans. It is striking that he did not only support his own school. He supported all four, including the Epicureans, whose ideas were quite different from his own. It was a gift to philosophy itself, to the long conversation of people trying to understand how to live, rather than to any one answer.

Picture him, if you like, on an evening in Athens, standing on one of the hills of the city as the sun goes down. Below him are the old streets, the marketplace, the porch where Zeno taught. Above him is the Acropolis, glowing rose and amber in the last light. The air smells of thyme and dust and the sea. And for a little while, the emperor of Rome is simply a student in the city of philosophers, looking at the places he had read about all his life.

Then he went home to Rome. He had been away for many years. On the twenty-third of December, in the year one hundred and seventy-six, he and his son Commodus celebrated a triumph together for their victories in the north. The procession wound through the streets, the crowds cheered, and the boy rode beside his father in the triumphal chariot.

In the year that followed, one hundred and seventy-seven, Marcus raised Commodus, who was then only fifteen, to the rank of Augustus, making him co-emperor. And here we must be honest, because this is one of the great questions people ask about Marcus Aurelius.

For almost a century, the best emperors of Rome had not been succeeded by their sons. They had chosen their heirs by adoption, picking capable grown men, as Hadrian had chosen Antoninus, and Antoninus had raised Marcus. Some historians, long ago, liked to imagine that this was a deliberate policy, a wise system of choosing the best man. In truth, it was mostly because those emperors had no surviving sons. Marcus did have a son. And in the Roman world, it would have been almost unthinkable for an emperor to pass over his own living son in favour of someone else. If he had tried, Commodus would almost certainly have become a rival, and perhaps the cause of a civil war.

So Marcus did what fathers do. He hoped for the best. He gave his son the finest teachers, as he himself had been given. He kept him close and took him on campaign. Whether he saw clearly what kind of man Commodus would become, we cannot know. History would judge Commodus very harshly indeed. But that lay in the future, and Marcus would not live to see it.

There are other shadows in these years, too, and we should not pass over them. During Marcus's reign, in several parts of the empire, Christians were put on trial and executed for refusing to honour the Roman gods. The best known case happened in the year one hundred and seventy-seven, in the city of Lugdunum in Gaul, the city we call Lyon today, where a group of Christians were cruelly put to death. Historians still debate how much Marcus himself had to do with such events. Most of them were driven by local hostility and local officials, under laws that were older than his reign. But they happened under his rule, and he did not stop them. A man can be wise and gentle in many ways, and still share the blind spots of his age. It is right that we remember that too.

In Rome, meanwhile, the people were making monuments to him. A great column began to rise in the Campus Martius, carved from bottom to top with a spiralling band of scenes from the northern wars: soldiers marching, rivers crossed, camps built, and the strange rain god pouring water from his wings. That column still stands today, in a square in the middle of Rome.

And somewhere in these years, a great bronze statue was made of Marcus on horseback, his right hand raised in a calm gesture, as if greeting his people or bringing peace. That statue survived, almost alone among the great bronze statues of the Roman emperors. For centuries, many people believed it showed Constantine, the first Christian emperor, and perhaps for that reason it was not melted down. Today the original stands indoors, in the Capitoline Museums in Rome, and a copy stands in the square on the Capitoline Hill, gazing out quietly over the city.

Let us rest here, beneath that bronze horseman, in the evening light on the Capitoline Hill. The square is quiet. The pigeons are settling on the rooftops. And the old emperor, with his raised hand and his calm bearded face, looks out over Rome, as he has done for almost two thousand years.
