## Chapter 12: The Setting Sun, and Goodnight

Marcus did not stay long in Rome. The peace on the Danube had never been fully secure, and soon the Marcomanni and the Quadi were troubling the frontier once again. So on the third of August, in the year one hundred and seventy-eight, he set out for the north one last time, with his son Commodus beside him.

He was fifty-seven years old now, and he was not a strong man. For many years he had suffered from pains in his chest and stomach, and he slept poorly. Galen and the other doctors had prescribed remedies for him, and he took them faithfully. But his body was tired. He had spent most of the last decade in army camps, through hard winters and long campaigns, and it had worn him down.

Still, he went on with his work. The war on the Danube went well for Rome in these last years. The Roman armies pushed deep into the lands of the Marcomanni and the Quadi, and stationed troops in their territory. Some historians believe that Marcus may have been planning to bring these lands permanently into the empire, as new provinces. We cannot be sure. He did not live long enough to finish whatever he had planned.

In the early spring of the year one hundred and eighty, Marcus fell seriously ill. It happened in his camp on the Danube. Most ancient sources say he was at Vindobona, the Roman fort that would one day grow into the city of Vienna. Another early writer places him at Sirmium. Either way, he was far from Rome, beside the great northern river, where he had spent so many of his last years.

The accounts of his final days come from later writers, so we should hold them gently. According to these accounts, when he understood that he was dying, he stopped eating and drinking, and waited calmly for the end. He called his friends and spoke with them about his son, and asked them to guide and support Commodus. He spoke about death, and about the shortness of human life, without fear. And on the last day, it is said, when an officer came to him to ask for the watchword for the night, as had once happened at the bedside of Antoninus, Marcus said to him, in effect: go to the rising sun, for I am already setting.

He meant, go to Commodus, the young emperor. My day is over. His is beginning.

Marcus Aurelius died on the seventeenth of March, in the year one hundred and eighty. He was fifty-eight years old. His body was burned, as was the Roman custom, and his ashes were carried back to Rome and placed in the great round tomb that Hadrian had built beside the Tiber. That tomb still stands, though it has changed greatly over the centuries. Today it is called the Castel Sant'Angelo, the Castle of the Holy Angel.

The historian Cassius Dio, who lived through these years as a young man, wrote that with the death of Marcus, the history of Rome fell from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust. Dio had seen what came next. The reign of Commodus was troubled and cruel, and it ended in murder, and after it came civil wars. Looking back from those dark years, the age of Marcus seemed golden, even though, as we have seen, it had been full of war and sickness and grief. Perhaps it seemed golden because of the man himself, rather than the times.

And what of the little notebook?

Marcus wrote it for himself alone. He never gave it a title. In the old manuscripts, it is called something like To Himself, in Greek. He did not send it to a publisher, or read it aloud to friends, or polish it for posterity. It was a private record, written by lamplight, in camps beside cold rivers, of a man trying to remind himself how to live.

And yet, somehow, it survived. Someone, perhaps a family member, perhaps a secretary or a friend, kept it after he died. It was copied by hand, again and again, through the long centuries, in a few precious manuscripts. Scholars in the Byzantine world read it and treasured it. Then, in the year fifteen hundred and fifty-nine, it was printed for the first time, in the Swiss city of Zurich. And after that, it travelled everywhere. It has been translated into almost every language on earth. Kings and presidents, soldiers and nurses, students and sleepless people, have kept it beside their beds. Today we call it the Meditations.

What would Marcus think of that? He would, I suspect, be a little embarrassed. He might remind himself, as he so often did, that fame is empty, and that soon all those who remember him will themselves be forgotten. But perhaps he would also be quietly glad, if he knew that his reminders to himself had helped other people, on hard mornings and long nights, to be a little calmer and a little kinder. Because that was what he wanted most of all. To do good to others, and to be at peace.

So let us gather up the threads of our story, slowly, as the lamps burn low.

We began on the Caelian Hill, with a quiet little boy, a grandfather who taught him to keep his temper, and a mother who taught him to live simply. We watched him take up the rough cloak of a philosopher at twelve, and sleep on the floor, until his mother persuaded him to take a couch with skins. We saw him chosen, suddenly, as the heir to an empire, and carried up to the Palatine Hill. We listened to his letters to Fronto, and to an ordinary autumn day of grape picking and talk with his mother. We watched him marry Faustina, and hold his first daughter, and lose so many of his children. We spent the long, golden afternoon of Antoninus beside him, and heard the old emperor's last word: equanimity. We followed him through floods and war, through the shadow of the plague, through his brother's death on a winter road. We sat with him by the Danube on freezing nights, as he wrote to himself about getting out of bed. We watched him burn his enemy's letters unread, and grieve for his wife in the mountains of Cappadocia, and stand as a student in the city of philosophers. And we heard him, at the very end, send his officer to the rising sun.

Near the very end of his notebook, Marcus wrote about death, and about the shortness of life, using the picture of an actor in a play. The actor complains, I have not finished the five acts, but only three of them. And Marcus answers him, gently. In life, he says, the three acts are the whole drama. And then he writes the final lines of the whole book. In the old translation, they read like this. Depart then satisfied, for he also who releases thee is satisfied.

It is a calm and generous way to end. Not with fear, and not with complaint, but with a kind of peace.

And so, as our own story comes to a close tonight, perhaps you might let go of the day in the same way. Whatever you did today is done. Whatever you did not finish can wait until the morning. There is no need to finish all five acts tonight. For now, there is only this moment, and this quiet room, and the slow, soft rhythm of your breathing.

Somewhere, far away and long ago, a lamp is burning in a tent beside a great river. A tired man lays down his pen, and blows out the light, and lies back on his plain bed under a rough blanket. Outside, the snow is falling softly on the sleeping camp. The river moves slowly in the dark. And everything is still.

Thank you for spending this evening with The Sleepy Biographer. Rest well. Sleep gently. And goodnight.
