## Chapter 7: Two Emperors, and a War in the East

When Antoninus died, the Senate was ready to give Marcus all the powers of an emperor at once. But Marcus did something that no Roman ruler had ever done before. He insisted that his adoptive brother, Lucius, should share the throne with him, as an equal.

Lucius was the boy who had been adopted alongside Marcus, all those years ago, on Hadrian's orders. He was now about thirty, nine years younger than Marcus. He was tall and good-looking, friendly and easy-going, fond of the races, of good dinners and of pleasant company. He was not a scholar, and he was not a philosopher. But he was loyal to his elder brother, and Marcus was fond of him. In his notebook, Marcus thanked the gods for giving him such a brother, one whose character roused him to watch over himself, and who pleased him with his respect and affection.

So, in March of the year one hundred and sixty-one, for the first time in its history, Rome had two emperors at once. They were known as Marcus Aurelius Antoninus and Lucius Aurelius Verus. Marcus remained the senior partner, and everyone understood that. But the honours and titles were shared between them. To bind the family even closer, Marcus promised his daughter Lucilla in marriage to Lucius.

That summer brought happy news. On the thirty-first of August, at Lanuvium, the little hill town where Antoninus had been born, Faustina gave birth to twin boys. One was named Titus Aurelius Fulvus Antoninus. The other was named Lucius Aurelius Commodus. Coins were struck to celebrate them, showing two little babies side by side. The elder twin, Antoninus, would die at the age of four. The younger, Commodus, would one day be emperor. But that is a story for the very end of our evening.

The new reign did not have long to enjoy its peace. Almost at once, troubles came from every side.

First, there was water. The river Tiber, which flows through the middle of Rome, flooded badly, perhaps in the year one hundred and sixty-two. Floods in Rome could be terrible. The river rose into the low-lying parts of the city, washing through streets and warehouses, drowning animals, ruining stores of grain. A food shortage followed. The two emperors had to turn their attention to the practical work of relief, to bringing in grain and helping the stricken parts of the city.

And then there was war.

Far away to the east, beyond the Euphrates river, lay the kingdom of Parthia, Rome's great rival in that part of the world. Its king, Vologases the Fourth, saw an opportunity in the change of emperors. His forces moved into Armenia, a mountain kingdom that both empires claimed to influence, and placed their own candidate on its throne. A Roman governor marched against them, and his army was destroyed. Soon the Parthians were threatening the Roman province of Syria itself.

It was decided that one of the emperors should go east to take charge. Marcus stayed in Rome, to govern the empire and to manage its affairs. Lucius went, in the year one hundred and sixty-two, travelling slowly by land and sea, and set up his headquarters at Antioch, the great, rich and pleasure-loving city of Syria.

Ancient writers liked to say that Lucius enjoyed Antioch rather too much, with its theatres, its gardens and its suburbs, while his generals did the real fighting. There may be some truth in that. But the generals he chose did their work well. A commander named Statius Priscus recovered Armenia. Another, Avidius Cassius, a tough and very able officer from Syria, led the Roman armies down the Euphrates and deep into Parthian territory. In the year one hundred and sixty-five, Roman troops captured the great twin cities of Seleucia and Ctesiphon, near the place where, many centuries later, the city of Baghdad would grow.

During these years, in the year one hundred and sixty-four, Lucilla travelled east to marry Lucius. Marcus went with her part of the way, then turned back to Rome. The wedding took place at Ephesus, the splendid city on the coast of Asia, with its great temple of Artemis and its marble streets running down to the harbour.

What was Marcus doing in Rome while all this went on? He was working, and working very hard. An emperor in those days was, among many other things, the highest judge in the empire. Petitions and appeals came to him from every corner of the Roman world. People wrote about their land, their inheritances, their slaves, their quarrels with their neighbours, their troubles with the tax collectors. And Marcus, by every account, took this work extremely seriously. The historian Cassius Dio, who wrote some decades later, tells us that Marcus would sometimes spend many days on a single case, and would even sit in judgement late into the night, because he believed that justice deserved time.

Many of the laws and rulings from his reign survive in later Roman law books. Again and again they show a careful, humane mind at work. There are rulings to protect orphans and their property. There are rulings to make it easier for enslaved people to be freed, and to protect the freedom of those who had been promised it. These were small steps in a world that took slavery for granted. Marcus did not try to end slavery. No Roman emperor did. But within the world as it was, he tried to make the law a little fairer and a little kinder.

We can picture him in these years, then, not on a battlefield, but at a desk. Morning after morning, the lamps are lit before dawn. The clerks bring in the day's letters and petitions. The emperor reads each one carefully, makes notes, asks questions, sends for more information. Outside, Rome is waking up. Carts rumble in the streets below. The smell of bread drifts from the bakeries. And the emperor of half the known world sits patiently, reading the troubles of a widow in Egypt, or a farmer in Spain, one at a time, as if he had plenty of time.

In the year one hundred and sixty-six, Lucius came home from the east. The two emperors celebrated a joint triumph through the streets of Rome, with the soldiers, the spoils and the cheering crowds. They took new victory titles. Coins were struck. It seemed as if the troubles of the new reign were over.

But the soldiers returning from the east had brought something else back with them, something invisible and terrible, that no triumph could hold back. Let us rest here for a little while, in the bright noise of the triumph, before we come to it.
