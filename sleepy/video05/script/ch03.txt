## Chapter 3: A Sudden Heir

By the year one hundred and thirty-six, the emperor Hadrian was sixty years old, and he was not well. He had no children of his own. And so the great question that hung over every Roman emperor began to press upon him. Who would come next?

Rome had no simple law of succession. In theory, the Senate and the people chose the emperor. In practice, an emperor usually chose his own successor, often by adopting him as a son. Adoption in Rome was not only for small children. A grown man could be adopted into another family, take its name, and become its heir in every legal sense. Several of the best emperors of that age had come to the throne in exactly this way.

Hadrian's first choice was a man named Lucius Ceionius Commodus. He was handsome, cultured and charming, though not, it seems, very strong in health. Hadrian adopted him, and he took the name Lucius Aelius Caesar. At around the same time, young Marcus was engaged to be married to Aelius's daughter, a girl named Ceionia Fabia. The engagement was a sign that Hadrian already had plans for Marcus, though perhaps nobody knew quite what they were.

Then, on the first day of January in the year one hundred and thirty-eight, Aelius Caesar died. He had been ill for some time, coughing blood. Hadrian's plan had fallen apart, and the emperor himself was growing weaker with every month.

So Hadrian made a second choice, and this time he arranged things very carefully. On the twenty-fifth of February, in the year one hundred and thirty-eight, he adopted a senator named Titus Aurelius Antoninus. Antoninus was a man of about fifty, wealthy, upright, calm and widely respected. He had served as consul and as governor of the province of Asia, and he had a reputation for fairness and good sense. But Hadrian added a condition. Antoninus, in his turn, had to adopt two young men as his own sons. One was Lucius, the little son of the late Aelius Caesar, who was then seven years old. The other was Marcus, who was sixteen.

In a single afternoon, it seems, the quiet, scholarly boy from the Caelian Hill had become one of the heirs to the Roman world.

From that day his name changed. He was no longer Marcus Annius Verus. He became Marcus Aelius Aurelius Verus, taking the family name of his new father. And the people of Rome would come to know him, in the end, as Marcus Aurelius.

How did he feel about it? The later collection of imperial lives tells a story that, true or not, has a ring of truth to it. When Marcus learned that he had been adopted into the imperial family, it says, he was not delighted. He was saddened. And when he was told that he must move from his mother's house to the imperial residence on the Palatine Hill, he went unwillingly. When members of his household asked him why he was so sad, he is said to have spoken about the troubles that come with power.

Whether or not he said those words, we know from his own notebook that he feared what the palace might do to him. Many years later, as emperor, he wrote a warning to himself in that notebook. In an old English translation it reads like this. Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye. He meant that power can soak into a person, like dye into cloth, and change the colour of their soul. Keep yourself simple, he went on, good, pure, serious, free from affectation, a friend of justice, kind, affectionate. It is the warning of a man who knew how easily a palace can spoil someone, and who did not want it to spoil him.

So, at sixteen, Marcus packed up his books and moved from the leafy Caelian Hill to the Palatine, the hill of the emperors. The Palatine was covered by an enormous complex of halls and courtyards, dining rooms and gardens, built up over more than a century by one emperor after another. From its terraces you could look down on the Circus Maximus on one side, and on the Forum, the old heart of Rome, on the other. It was magnificent, and it was a little overwhelming.

Meanwhile, Hadrian was dying. He had gone to Baiae, a fashionable seaside resort on the Bay of Naples, with its warm springs and its villas looking out over the blue water. There, on the tenth of July, in the year one hundred and thirty-eight, he died, at the age of sixty-two.

Hadrian had been a difficult man in many ways, and in his last years he had grown bitter and suspicious. Some in the Senate had wanted to refuse him the honours usually given to a dead emperor. But his new son, Antoninus, insisted. He pleaded with the Senate until they agreed to declare Hadrian a god, as was the custom for emperors they respected. It was an act of loyalty that won Antoninus the name by which he is still known: Antoninus Pius. Pius means dutiful, devoted, faithful to one's family and to the gods.

And so Antoninus Pius became emperor, and Marcus, at seventeen, became the emperor's son.

There were other changes too. Marcus's engagement to Ceionia Fabia was quietly set aside. Antoninus had a daughter of his own, a young girl named Annia Galeria Faustina, called Faustina the Younger to tell her apart from her mother. Antoninus wished Marcus to marry her instead, and so a new engagement was made. Faustina was still a child, and the marriage itself would have to wait several years. But from now on, Marcus was bound to his new family by ties of both adoption and affection.

Honours came quickly. In the year one hundred and thirty-nine he was given the title Caesar, the title for an imperial heir. In the year one hundred and forty, at only eighteen, he held the consulship, the highest office in the Roman state, alongside his adoptive father. It was far too young for such an honour, by the old rules. But the old rules had always bent for the family of the emperor.

He might easily have become proud. Instead, as far as we can tell, he worked harder than ever. He went on with his studies, now with even greater teachers. He sat beside Antoninus as the emperor went about his business, watching and learning how the empire was governed. He went to the Senate, and to the courts, and to the endless meetings and petitions and ceremonies of public life.

Let us picture him at the end of one of those long days, back in his rooms on the Palatine. The noise of the city below has softened. A cool breeze comes in over the terrace from the hills. On the table there are wax tablets covered with notes, and a letter half written, and a scroll he has not yet had time to finish. He is tired. But before he sleeps, he unrolls a little of the scroll, and reads a few more lines by the lamp, as he always will, all his life. Let us leave him there, and in the next part of our story we will meet the teachers who came to him in these years, and the letters that passed between them.
