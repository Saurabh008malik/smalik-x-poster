## Chapter 5: Faustina, and a House Full of Children

In the year one hundred and forty-five, Marcus married Faustina, the daughter of the emperor Antoninus Pius. He was about twenty-four years old, and she was perhaps fifteen or a little older, which in Rome was not an unusual age for a bride from a great family. That same year Marcus held his second consulship, again beside his adoptive father.

We know very little about the wedding itself. We can imagine the city decorated with garlands, the crowds in the streets, the coins struck to mark the occasion, the sacrifices and the feasting. Weddings in Rome were joyful and noisy affairs, with torches and songs and a procession at dusk to lead the bride to her new home. For the daughter of the emperor and the heir to the throne, it would have been one of the great celebrations of the year.

What we know rather better is the marriage that followed, because it lasted for thirty years, and because it was remarkably fruitful. Faustina and Marcus had a great many children together. Ancient evidence suggests at least thirteen, and perhaps more. Their first child, a daughter named Domitia Faustina, was born on the thirtieth of November in the year one hundred and forty-seven.

The very next day, Antoninus gave Marcus two great powers, the tribunician power and a share of supreme command. These were the formal marks of a co-ruler. From now on, everyone knew that Marcus was not merely an heir in waiting, but his father's partner in government. And on the same occasion, Faustina was given the title Augusta, the highest title a Roman woman could hold.

So, within the space of two days, Marcus became a father, and something very close to an emperor.

It is easy to read those lines quickly, as a list of titles. But let us slow down for a moment and picture it. A young man of twenty-six, standing in a room on the Palatine, holding a tiny newborn daughter in his arms for the first time. She is wrapped in soft wool. Her mother is resting nearby. Outside the window, the late autumn light is fading over the rooftops of Rome. Messengers are hurrying in and out, and in the Senate house the great men are preparing their speeches and their votes. But for a little while, at least, none of that matters. There is only a father, and a daughter, and a quiet room.

More children followed, year after year. There were daughters, among them Annia Lucilla, who would one day marry an emperor, and Annia Galeria Faustina, Fadilla and Cornificia. There were sons too. But childhood in the ancient world was a fragile thing. Even in the richest house in the empire, with the best doctors that money could buy, many children did not live to grow up. Fevers and illnesses that we would treat easily today took them away, often very young.

Marcus and Faustina lost many of their children. Some died as babies, some as small boys and girls. Of all the children born to them, only a handful lived to be adults, and only one of their sons survived his father.

We should be gentle here, because this was a real grief, repeated many times over. In his notebook, Marcus wrote about the death of children several times. He wrote that he had learned from his teacher Apollonius to be always the same, even at the loss of a child. He reminded himself, in the Stoic way, that everything we love is lent to us, not given forever. But we should not imagine that this made it easy. In one of the surviving letters, Fronto writes of grieving for a grandson of his own, and the two men comfort each other. Philosophy, for Marcus, was not a way of not feeling sorrow. It was a way of carrying it.

And yet there was joy in this house too. In the letters to Fronto, Marcus writes now and then about his little ones. He tells his old teacher how the little ones are, about the hot weather, about fevers that come and, thankfully, go again. The great Stoic philosopher sounds, in these letters, like any anxious young father, sitting up late, listening for a cough in the next room, and relieved when the fever finally breaks.

What was Faustina like? This is a harder question than it should be. The later Roman writers who described her were often unkind. They told scandalous stories about her, stories of lovers and intrigues. Modern historians tend to treat these tales with great suspicion. Many of them seem to come from later gossip, and from a tradition of blackening the names of powerful women. They are, as one might say, the kind of stories people tell about a queen, true or not.

What we do know is what Marcus himself thought of her. In the first book of his notebook, in the long list of things for which he thanked the gods, he gave thanks that he had a wife who was, in the old translation, so obedient, and so affectionate, and so simple. The words obedient and simple sound a little strange to modern ears. But for a Roman writer, they were words of deep praise. He meant that she was gentle, loyal and unpretentious, and that he loved her. And when, much later, she died, he grieved for her deeply and honoured her memory in many ways. We will come to that, much later on.

Faustina travelled with her husband, too, in the hard years to come. She went with him to the army camps on the northern frontier, through cold winters and long campaigns, and the soldiers gave her a special title: Mother of the Camp. That is not the title of a woman who lived only for palace pleasures.

So let us picture the household as it was in those middle years, the quiet years of Antoninus. A great house on the Palatine, but a busy and noisy one, full of nurses and tutors, small children running through the courtyards, a baby crying somewhere, a mother calling out. And in a small room set a little apart, a young father at his desk, with a pile of official papers on one side, a letter to Fronto on the other, and a book of philosophy open in the middle. He is trying to do all three things at once, and perhaps not quite managing any of them. And then, a child's voice calls him from the doorway, and he puts down his pen, and goes.

Let us leave them there, together. In the next part of our story, we will spend some time with the man who taught Marcus most of all how to rule: his adoptive father, Antoninus Pius.
