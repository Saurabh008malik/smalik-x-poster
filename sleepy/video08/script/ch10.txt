## Chapter 10: The Year Seventeen Eighty-Nine

By the winter of seventeen eighty-eight, France was in trouble. The treasury was nearly empty. The harvest of that summer had been ruined by a terrible hailstorm, and the winter that followed was one of the coldest anyone could remember. Rivers froze. The price of bread rose and rose, until in some places a labourer's whole day's wages would barely buy a loaf for his family. And everywhere, in town squares and coffee houses and village churches, people were talking about change.

The King, at last, did something that no French king had done for more than a hundred and seventy years. He called a meeting of the Estates-General, a great assembly of representatives from all over the kingdom. There would be the clergy, the nobles, and, by far the largest group, the commoners, the people of the Third Estate. They were to come to Versailles and help find a way out of the crisis.

On the fourth of May, seventeen eighty-nine, there was a solemn procession through the town of Versailles to open the assembly. The streets were hung with tapestries. The deputies walked to church in their different costumes, the nobles in silk and plumes, the clergy in their robes, and the commoners in plain black. The King was cheered. The Queen, walking in her place, was met mostly by silence, and, according to some accounts, by a few hostile shouts. It was said that she turned pale but kept her composure. The next day, at the formal opening, she sat beside the King, dignified and very still.

But her mind, that spring, was elsewhere. Her eldest son, the little Dauphin Louis Joseph, was very ill. He had been a bright, gentle, clever boy, but for some time he had been suffering from a wasting illness of the spine, probably a form of tuberculosis, and he had grown thin and bent and weak. He had been taken to the château of Meudon, outside Paris, for the fresher air. His parents visited him as often as they could. And on the fourth of June, seventeen eighty-nine, he died. He was seven years old.

There was hardly time to grieve. In the very weeks that followed, the assembly at Versailles was transforming itself. The deputies of the Third Estate declared that they were the true representatives of the nation, and called themselves the National Assembly. When they found the doors of their meeting hall locked one rainy morning in June, they gathered in a nearby indoor tennis court and swore an oath not to separate until France had a constitution. The old world was beginning to shift under everyone's feet.

On the fourteenth of July, in Paris, a great crowd stormed the Bastille, an old fortress prison that had become a symbol of royal power. Within days, the old order was falling apart. Many great nobles, fearing for their safety, began to leave France. Among those the Queen urged to go was her dear friend, the Duchesse de Polignac, whose family was now widely hated. The two women said goodbye at Versailles, and they never saw each other again. The Queen wrote to her afterwards, tender letters full of affection and sadness.

The summer passed, and then the early autumn. And on the fifth of October, seventeen eighty-nine, the storm came to Versailles itself.

It began in Paris, among the women of the markets, who were desperate about the price and scarcity of bread. Thousands of them gathered, and set out on foot in the rain, to walk the long road to Versailles to demand bread from the King. Others joined them along the way, and behind them came a column of armed citizens. There is a lovely old tradition, which we cannot be sure is true, that when the news arrived, the Queen was sitting alone in the little grotto in the gardens of the Petit Trianon, and that a page came running to find her. If it is true, it was the last time she ever sat there.

By evening, the crowds had filled the great courtyards and the streets of Versailles. The King received a group of the women, spoke kindly to them and promised them bread. Late that night, the royal family went to bed, uneasy, but believing the worst had passed.

In the early hours of the sixth of October, some of the crowd broke into the palace. They made their way towards the Queen's apartments. Her guards stood in their way, and two of them lost their lives defending her doors. The Queen was woken by the cries of her ladies, and, with only a moment to spare, she escaped through a private passage to her husband's rooms, where the King and her children soon joined her. The family was safe, but it had been a very narrow thing.

As the morning light came up over the courtyard, the crowd below began to shout for the Queen. They wanted to see her. The Marquis de Lafayette, commander of the new National Guard, advised her to go out. And so, at last, she stepped out onto the balcony, at first, it is said, holding her two children by the hand. The crowd shouted for the children to go back inside. And so she stood there alone, facing thousands of people, many of whom hated her, her hands folded, her head bowed a little, saying nothing at all.

There was a long moment of silence. Then Lafayette stepped out beside her, bowed, and kissed her hand. And something in the crowd changed. A few voices, and then many more, began to cry out, long live the Queen.

It did not change what happened next. That same day, the King and Queen were made to leave Versailles for Paris. The great procession took hours along the muddy autumn road, with the royal carriage surrounded by the crowds, by the National Guard, and by wagons of flour from the palace stores. At last, as darkness fell, they arrived at the old palace of the Tuileries, in the heart of the city, which had stood almost empty for many years. Its rooms were dusty and cold, the furniture was old, and there were not enough beds.

The royal family would never return to Versailles. The great palace, with its Hall of Mirrors and its fountains, and the little house at the bottom of the garden, with its thatched cottages and its quiet pond, was left behind for ever.

Let us leave the empty palace in the autumn rain, its windows dark, the gardens silent, and in the next chapter we will follow the family into their new life in Paris, and towards a desperate journey by night.
