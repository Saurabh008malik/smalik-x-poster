## Chapter 9: The Necklace, and the Whispering

In the early seventeen-seventies, two Paris jewellers named Boehmer and Bassenge had set out to make the most magnificent necklace in Europe. It was a cascade of diamonds, row upon row of them, hundreds of stones in festoons and tassels, so heavy and so showy that it was almost more like a sculpture than a piece of jewellery. They had hoped to sell it to Louis the Fifteenth for Madame du Barry. But the old King died, and the necklace remained unsold, an enormous fortune lying in a velvet box.

So the jewellers offered it to the new Queen. More than once, it seems, they tried to persuade her. And each time, she said no. It was far too expensive, and in any case, the story goes, she said that France had more need of warships than of necklaces. Whether she used those exact words, we cannot be sure. But we can be quite sure of the main point. Marie Antoinette did not want that necklace, and she did not buy it.

What happened next was one of the strangest frauds in history.

Among the many people who drifted around the edges of the court in those years was a woman named Jeanne de la Motte. She claimed descent from an old royal family, the Valois, and she called herself a countess, but she was poor, ambitious and very clever. She made the acquaintance of the same Prince Louis de Rohan who had greeted the young Dauphine at the doors of Strasbourg Cathedral, years before. He was now a Cardinal and the Grand Almoner of France, rich, vain and rather foolish. And he had one great sorrow. The Queen disliked him, and would not speak to him, and he longed above all else to win back her favour.

Jeanne de la Motte persuaded the Cardinal that she was a secret friend of the Queen, and that she could carry messages between them. She brought him letters, apparently from Marie Antoinette, written on fine paper edged with blue. They were forgeries. One clue might have warned him, if he had been less eager to believe. The letters were signed Marie Antoinette de France, and a queen of France would never have signed her name that way.

Then, one summer night in seventeen eighty-four, in a dark grove in the gardens of Versailles, the Cardinal was led to a meeting. A young woman in a white dress, her face shadowed by a hat, stepped out from among the trees, held out a rose, and murmured a few words. In the darkness he believed she was the Queen. She was not. She was a young Parisian woman who looked a little like Marie Antoinette, and who had been hired for the occasion. The Cardinal went home overjoyed.

A few months later, Jeanne de la Motte told him that the Queen wanted the famous diamond necklace, but could not be seen to buy it openly. Would he, as her trusted friend, arrange the purchase in secret, on credit, with payments spread over time? He agreed at once. He signed the agreement with the jewellers. The necklace was handed over, early in seventeen eighty-five, to a man who claimed to be the Queen's messenger. And it vanished. The diamonds were taken out of their settings and sold off, many of them, it seems, in London.

The fraud fell apart when the first payment came due. The jewellers, puzzled, approached the Queen directly. She had no idea what they were talking about. When the whole story came out, she was furious, and so was the King. On the fifteenth of August, seventeen eighty-five, the Feast of the Assumption, as the Cardinal was about to celebrate Mass before the court at Versailles in his scarlet robes, he was summoned before the King, questioned, and then arrested in front of the astonished courtiers.

The King decided that the case should be heard publicly, before the high court of Paris, the Parlement. He wanted the Queen's innocence to be proved for all to see. It was an understandable decision. It was also a terrible mistake. The trial became the sensation of the age. In the end, in May seventeen eighty-six, Jeanne de la Motte was found guilty and severely punished. But the Cardinal was acquitted, on the grounds that he had been deceived. And the crowds outside the court cheered him as a hero.

Why? Because, in the minds of many people, the acquittal of the Cardinal meant that the Queen must have been involved somehow. Surely, they said, he would never have believed such a thing unless she had encouraged him. Surely she had wanted the necklace, and had used him, and then let him take the blame. None of it was true. Historians who have looked closely at the evidence agree that the Queen was entirely innocent. But it did not matter. The story was too good. Jeanne de la Motte later escaped from prison, fled to London and wrote memoirs full of fresh accusations. And the necklace, which Marie Antoinette never owned and never wore, became for ever part of her legend.

The necklace affair came on top of years of whispering. Since the early days of her marriage, cheap pamphlets had been printed and passed around in secret, some in Paris, some smuggled in from abroad. They mocked her as a foreigner, the Austrian woman, and accused her of every kind of extravagance and wickedness. Many were crude and cruel, and most of their claims were simply invented. But people read them in coffee houses and passed them from hand to hand, and over time, a picture formed. The Queen was a spendthrift. The Queen did not care for France. The Queen was to blame.

There was a real crisis behind the whispers. France was deeply in debt, partly because of the cost of its wars, including the war in America. The tax system was unfair and creaking, and the government was struggling to borrow more. The Queen's spending was only a very small part of the problem. But it was visible, and easy to understand, and so people gave her a new nickname. They called her Madame Déficit.

It is in this atmosphere, too, that the old phrase about cake began to be attached to her name, though, as we heard at the beginning, it was already a story before she ever came to France. A queen who had given money to the victims of the fireworks, who had written of her love for the people of Paris, and who had once turned down that very necklace, was becoming, in the public imagination, the very symbol of a heartless court.

She did try to change. From seventeen eighty-seven, she cut back sharply on her household, dismissed many servants, sold some property and gave up a number of luxuries. She began to attend meetings with ministers and to take an interest in affairs of state. But it was late, and the whispering had grown too loud to stop.

Let us leave the dark grove at Versailles, and the rose held out among the trees, and in the next chapter we will come to the year that changed everything, the year seventeen eighty-nine.
