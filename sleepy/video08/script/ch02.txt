## Chapter 2: An Imperial Childhood in Vienna and Schönbrunn

Childhood, for the youngest daughters of the Empress, moved with the seasons between two palaces. In winter there was the Hofburg, in the middle of the city, with its old stone corridors and its chapels and its crowded courtyards. In summer the family moved out to Schönbrunn, a large, pale yellow palace on the edge of Vienna, with long formal gardens, clipped hedges, fountains and a small zoo of exotic animals. The rooms at Schönbrunn were bright and airy. The windows looked out over lawns that ran up a gentle hill towards a stone colonnade against the sky.

If you could walk into those gardens on a summer morning in the early seventeen-sixties, you might see a small fair-haired girl running along the gravel paths, with an older sister close beside her. The sister was Maria Carolina, three years older, and the two girls were inseparable. They shared a governess, they shared lessons, and, it seems, they shared a good deal of mischief. Antoine was lively, affectionate and quick to laugh. She was also, by every account we have, not very fond of books.

This was not entirely her fault. The Empress was devoted to her children's religious education and to their manners, but with so many of them, and an empire to run, she could not watch over every lesson. The governesses, wanting to please, sometimes made the work easier than it should have been. One later story says that Antoine's governess traced her letters for her in pencil so that the little girl only had to go over them in ink. Whether or not that is exactly true, we know from later letters that, by the age of thirteen, Antoine's handwriting was uncertain and her spelling was poor, in German and in French alike. She found it very hard to sit still and concentrate. Her mind, it was said, was quick, but it would not stay in one place for long.

There were things she loved, though. She loved music. She learned to play the harpsichord and, later, the harp, and she had lessons from the composer Christoph Willibald Gluck, who was then working at the Viennese court. Years afterwards, as Queen of France, she would welcome Gluck to Paris and help his operas find an audience there. She loved dancing, and she danced beautifully, with a natural lightness that people noticed all her life. And she loved the little plays and ballets that the imperial children performed for their family on special occasions. In seventeen sixty-five, when her eldest brother Joseph married for the second time, the younger children performed in a short opera and ballet at Schönbrunn. A painting of that evening still survives, showing the small dancers in their costumes on a little stage.

There is one famous story from these years that you may have heard. In the autumn of seventeen sixty-two, a six-year-old boy from Salzburg came to play the keyboard for the imperial family at Schönbrunn. His name was Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. According to the story, he slipped on the polished floor, and the little archduchess Antoine, who was about his age, helped him to his feet, and he told her very seriously that he would marry her when he grew up. It is a charming tale. But we should hold it lightly. It comes from family memory, written down long afterwards, and the details have certainly been polished in the retelling. What is certain is that the Mozart children did play for the Empress that October, and were treated kindly.

Not every memory of these years was a happy one. In the summer of seventeen sixty-five, the Emperor Francis Stephen travelled to Innsbruck for a family wedding, and there, quite suddenly, he died. He had been a gentle, easy-going father, fond of his children, more interested in science and collecting than in politics. A story was later told that, before he set out, he called his youngest daughter back to hold her one more time. Like so many stories in this life, we cannot be sure of it. But we know that Antoine was nine years old when her father died, and that her mother, grief-stricken, wore black for the rest of her own life.

Two years later, in seventeen sixty-seven, smallpox came to the palace. It was the great terror of the age, and royal families were not spared. Antoine's sister Maria Josepha, who had been promised in marriage to the young King of Naples, caught the disease and died, only days before she was to leave Vienna. The Empress, who was nothing if not practical, did not let the alliance go. Maria Carolina was sent to Naples in her sister's place. And so Antoine lost her closest companion. The two sisters wrote to each other for the rest of their lives, but they would never again run through the gardens of Schönbrunn together.

By then, Antoine's own future was being arranged. For centuries the Habsburgs of Austria and the Bourbon kings of France had been rivals and often enemies. But in seventeen fifty-six, the year after Antoine was born, the two old powers had signed an alliance, to the astonishment of Europe. Alliances in those days were sealed by marriages. The King of France, Louis the Fifteenth, had a grandson who would one day inherit the throne, a boy named Louis Auguste. He was a little more than a year older than Antoine. Through long, slow letters between diplomats, and much careful bargaining, it was agreed that the youngest archduchess would marry the future King of France.

From that moment, her childhood began to close. A French priest, the Abbé de Vermond, was sent to Vienna to teach her French history, French customs and the French language, and to prepare her for the court she would join. He found her charming and good-natured, but he reported, honestly, that she was easily distracted, and that she knew very little. A hairdresser came from Paris to arrange her hair in the French style. A dentist worked on her teeth. Her portrait was painted and sent to Versailles, so that the old King could see what his grandson was being given. She was measured, polished and taught, like a young tree being trained to grow along a wall.

Through it all, her mother watched and worried. Maria Theresa knew how difficult the French court could be, how full of factions and gossip, and how harshly it would judge a foreign girl. She also knew her daughter was very young. But she was an empress first, and the marriage was too important to delay.

So the spring of seventeen seventy came, and with it the final preparations. Let us rest here, in the quiet rooms of Schönbrunn, with the gardens turning green again beneath the windows, and in the next chapter we will travel with Antoine, slowly, across Europe towards France.
