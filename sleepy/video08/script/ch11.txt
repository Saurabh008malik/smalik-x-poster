## Chapter 11: The Tuileries, and the Road to Varennes

The Tuileries Palace stood on the bank of the river Seine, in the very centre of Paris, at the western end of the long galleries of the Louvre. In front of it, gardens of trees, terraces and gravel walks ran down towards the river and the great open square beyond. It was an old palace, much older than Versailles, and for decades no king had lived there. When the royal family arrived on that October night in seventeen eighty-nine, they found it dark and shabby, with other people living in some of its rooms. Servants hurried to make it habitable. Furniture was brought from Versailles. And slowly, a kind of life began again.

It was a strange, uneasy life. The King and Queen were not exactly prisoners, not yet. They could walk in the gardens, receive visitors and go to Mass. But they were watched at every turn by the National Guard, and they knew that they could no longer simply go where they wished. The Queen spent much of her time with her two surviving children, Madame Royale, now nearly eleven, and the little boy Louis Charles, who since his brother's death had become the new Dauphin. He was four years old, cheerful and full of energy, and he was given a small garden of his own beside the palace, where he grew flowers and could be seen playing by the passers-by. His mother taught him his prayers and watched over his lessons. For the children, at least, she tried to keep things calm.

There were moments of hope. On the fourteenth of July, seventeen ninety, the first anniversary of the fall of the Bastille, a great festival called the Festival of the Federation was held on the open ground of the Champ de Mars. Hundreds of thousands of people gathered, in pouring rain, for an enormous open-air ceremony. The King swore to uphold the new laws of the nation, and the Queen lifted up the Dauphin so the crowd could see him. Many people cheered. For a day, it seemed possible that the King and the new France might find a way to live together.

That summer, the family was allowed to stay at the château of Saint-Cloud, outside Paris, where the air was fresher and the gardens were green. There, in secret, the Queen met one of the most powerful men of the revolution, the great orator Mirabeau, who had come to believe that the monarchy could still be saved, if the King would accept a new constitution and work with it. The Queen had feared and disliked him, but she found him, to her surprise, a man she could talk to. For a while, he advised the court in secret. But in the spring of seventeen ninety-one, Mirabeau suddenly fell ill and died. With him went one of the last bridges between the King and the revolution.

By then, the King and Queen had begun to think about escape. Their friend Count Axel von Fersen, who had come back to Paris, worked for months on a plan. The family would slip out of the Tuileries at night, in disguise, and travel east towards the frontier, to the town of Montmédy, where a loyal general was waiting with troops. From there, they hoped, the King could deal with the revolution from a position of strength. Fersen arranged the false papers, the money, the horses along the route and a large, comfortable travelling coach.

On the night of the twentieth of June, seventeen ninety-one, they went. The children's governess, Madame de Tourzel, played the part of a Russian baroness travelling home. The King was dressed as her steward, in a plain coat and a round hat. The Queen played the part of the children's governess. The little Dauphin was dressed as a girl. One by one, they slipped out of the palace through quiet doors and passages and met in a dark street nearby. According to some accounts, the Queen took a wrong turning in the night and was lost for a short while among the streets around the palace, before she found her way to the carriage. Fersen himself drove them out of Paris, through the darkness, to a village on the eastern edge of the city. There he said goodbye, and rode away.

The journey went on through the long summer day of the twenty-first of June. It was slow. The coach was heavy, a strap broke, and they fell further and further behind the times that had been planned. The soldiers who were meant to meet them along the road, confused by the delays, had moved on. And at a small town called Sainte-Menehould, the local postmaster looked into the carriage and thought he recognised the King. It is often said that he knew him from the King's portrait printed on the new paper money. That may be a later embellishment. But he did recognise him, and he rode ahead through the evening to raise the alarm.

Late that night, in the little town of Varennes, the carriage was stopped. The family was taken into the house of a local grocer, a kind and frightened man, while the town bells rang and crowds gathered. There, in a small upstairs room, among the shelves and candles of an ordinary home, the King and Queen waited through the night. In the morning, messengers came from Paris with orders. They were to return.

The journey back took four days, under a burning summer sun, in a crowded carriage, through towns where crowds lined the road in silence. When they came back into Paris, on the twenty-fifth of June, the streets were full of people, and notices had been posted ordering everyone to keep their hats on and to say nothing as the King passed. It was the silence that people remembered. Madame Campan later wrote that the Queen's hair had turned grey from the strain of those days. Whether it happened so quickly, we cannot know. But she was not yet thirty-six, and she had begun to look much older.

After Varennes, nothing was quite the same. The King accepted the new constitution, but few trusted him now. In the spring of seventeen ninety-two, France went to war with Austria, the Queen's own homeland, and people whispered that she was secretly helping the enemy. In February of that year, Fersen came once more, in disguise, to see her at the Tuileries. It was the last time they would ever meet.

And on the tenth of August, seventeen ninety-two, the Tuileries was attacked. The royal family left the palace on foot that morning, crossing the gardens through the fallen leaves, to take refuge in the hall of the Assembly nearby. They would never go back. Three days later, on the thirteenth of August, they were taken to a tall, grey medieval tower in the east of Paris, called the Temple. The monarchy was abolished soon afterwards. In the violent weeks of that September, the Queen's gentle friend, the Princesse de Lamballe, who had come back to France to be near her, lost her life. The Queen was not told at once, and when the news came, she was overcome.

Let us leave the family there, behind the thick walls of the old tower, in the falling autumn of seventeen ninety-two, and in the last chapter we will walk with the Queen, gently and slowly, through her final year.
