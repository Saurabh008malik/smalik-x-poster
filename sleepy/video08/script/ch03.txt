## Chapter 3: The Long Road to France

In the middle of April, in the year seventeen seventy, the church of the Augustinians in Vienna was filled with candlelight. It was the church where the Habsburgs were married, close beside the palace, tall and white and Gothic inside. On the nineteenth of April, Antoine was married there by proxy. Her groom was far away in France, so her brother, the Archduke Ferdinand, stood in his place and spoke the vows on his behalf. It was an ordinary custom for royal weddings of the time. It also meant that, by the time she left her home, the girl was already, in law, a wife.

Two days later, on the twenty-first of April, she set out. It was early in the morning. The courtyards of the Hofburg were crowded with carriages and horses, servants and guards, and the people of Vienna lined the streets to watch her go. Her mother said goodbye. We do not know her exact words, though many versions were later written down. We do know that Maria Theresa had prepared a long letter of advice for her daughter, a list of rules for her new life. Antoine was to read it once a month. She was to say her prayers every morning, to read something serious each day, to be kind, to avoid gossip and to take no side in the quarrels of the French court. She was, above all, to please her husband and the old King, and to remember that she was a daughter of the house of Austria.

Then the carriages rolled out through the city gates and onto the road to the west.

The journey took more than two weeks. Imagine it, if you like, from the window of a slow, swaying carriage. Spring is coming to the countryside. The fields are pale green, the fruit trees along the road are starting to blossom, and every so often the procession winds down into a valley where a river runs fast and grey with melted snow. They stopped at the great abbey of Melk, high above the Danube, where the monks put on a small play for her. They passed through Bavaria and crossed into the lands of southern Germany, through Munich and Augsburg and on towards the Black Forest. At each town there were church bells, speeches, children with flowers, and long dinners with local nobles. And each night she slept in a new bed, a little further from home.

At last, in the first week of May, the procession came to the river Rhine, the old border between the German lands and France. On a small island in the middle of the river, near the city of Strasbourg, the French had built a special wooden pavilion for the handover. It had two sets of rooms, an Austrian side and a French side, joined by a hall in the middle. The idea was that the bride would enter from one bank as an Austrian archduchess and leave from the other as a French dauphine, the wife of the heir to the throne.

The ceremony took place on the seventh of May. According to later accounts by people at the French court, Antoine was undressed on the Austrian side and dressed again entirely in French clothes, so that she would carry nothing of Austria with her into France. She said goodbye to most of her Austrian attendants there. Then she walked through into the central hall, where her new French household was waiting to receive her, led by a stiff and very correct lady named the Comtesse de Noailles, who would be in charge of her daily life at Versailles.

There is a curious little footnote to that day. A young student from Frankfurt was studying in Strasbourg at the time, and he went to see the pavilion before the ceremony. He was shocked to find that its walls were hung with tapestries showing the ancient story of Jason and Medea, one of the unhappiest marriages in all of legend. Years later, that student became the great German writer Goethe, and he wrote about the tapestries in his memoirs. It is a strange detail, and a little sad, and it is told in his own words, so we can trust that he saw what he describes.

From the island, the new Dauphine crossed into France, and Strasbourg gave her a joyful welcome. The bells of its great red sandstone cathedral rang out, the streets were hung with garlands, and at the cathedral door she was greeted by a young churchman from one of the grandest families in France, Prince Louis de Rohan. Remember his name, softly, because he will return to her story many years later, in a way neither of them could have imagined.

Then on again, across eastern France, through Nancy and Châlons and Reims and Soissons, with crowds along the roads to catch a glimpse of the young girl from Vienna. And on the fourteenth of May, at the edge of the forest of Compiègne, north of Paris, the carriages stopped. Waiting there was the King of France, Louis the Fifteenth, now sixty years old, handsome still, with a long and complicated past. And beside him stood his grandson, Louis Auguste, the Dauphin, a tall, heavy, shy young man of fifteen.

According to the accounts, Antoine stepped down from her carriage and sank into a deep curtsey before the King. He raised her up and kissed her, and seemed delighted with her. The Dauphin, more awkwardly, kissed her on the cheek, as custom required. We do not know what passed through the minds of the two young people that afternoon. He was serious, timid and fond of hunting, locks and maps. She was lively, talkative and fond of music and dancing. They were, it is fair to say, not very much alike.

Two days later, on the sixteenth of May, seventeen seventy, they were married again, this time in person, in the Royal Chapel of the Palace of Versailles. The chapel is a tall, bright space of white stone and gold, with a painted ceiling high above. The bride wore a gown of white brocade, so it was said, which had been made a little too small, so that it did not quite close at the back. In the evening there was a great banquet in the new opera house of the palace, which had been finished just in time for the wedding. And then, according to the old custom, the young couple were put to bed in public, with the court looking on, the curtains drawn around them by the King himself. After that, the court went away, and two shy teenagers were left alone in the dark.

The celebrations went on for days. On the thirtieth of May, there was a great firework display in Paris, in the square then called the Place Louis the Fifteenth. That night, something went badly wrong. In the crowds pressing to leave, panic broke out, and many people lost their lives. When the Dauphin and Dauphine heard of it, they were deeply upset, and both of them sent money from their own allowance to help the families of those who had died. It was a sad beginning, and a small sign, perhaps, of the kind heart that she would show many times in her life.

Let us leave her there, at the start of her married life, in a palace far larger and far stranger than any she had known, and in the next chapter we will walk with her through the long, gilded days of a young Dauphine at Versailles.
