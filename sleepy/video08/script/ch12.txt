## Chapter 12: The Last Autumn, and Goodnight

The Temple had once belonged to a medieval order of knights. By seventeen ninety-two, what remained was a cluster of old buildings and a tall, square stone tower with pointed turrets at its corners, standing among gardens in the east of Paris. The royal family lived in a few rooms on the upper floors of that tower. The windows were partly covered, the walls were thick, and guards were always close by. But for a while, within those walls, they made a small and orderly family life.

We know a good deal about those months, because the King's valet, a loyal man named Cléry, later wrote an account of them, and because Madame Royale, many years afterwards, wrote down her own memories. The days followed a gentle routine. In the mornings the King taught his son, reading with him, and giving him lessons in geography, tracing the rivers and provinces of France on the map. The Queen taught her daughter, and the two of them did needlework together. With them was the King's younger sister, Madame Élisabeth, a kind and devout woman who had chosen to stay with her brother's family rather than escape abroad. In the afternoons, if the weather allowed, they walked a little in the garden of the Temple. In the evenings, they read aloud, or played quiet games, and the children were put to bed.

It was in those rooms, in December seventeen ninety-two, that the King learned he was to be put on trial. And in January seventeen ninety-three, he was condemned. On the evening of the twentieth of January, he was allowed to see his family one last time. They spent a long while together, and the children clung to him. He promised to see them again in the morning, though he knew he would not, wishing to spare them a second parting. The next day, the twenty-first of January, Louis the Sixteenth was executed. He was thirty-eight years old. He had been, whatever his failings as a ruler, a gentle and honest man, and he forgave those who had condemned him.

The Queen, now known simply as the Widow Capet, wore black from that day on.

There was one more parting still to come. In the summer, on the night of the third of July, seventeen ninety-three, officials came to take her son away from her, to be raised apart from his family. According to Madame Royale, her mother refused, and held him, and pleaded with them for a long time before she was made to let him go. Afterwards, it was said, she would stand for hours at a small opening high up in the tower, hoping to catch a glimpse of him as he was taken out to walk.

A few weeks later, in the early hours of the second of August, she too was taken from the Temple, away from her daughter and her sister-in-law, and brought to an old palace on an island in the river Seine, called the Conciergerie. There she was kept in a small, plain room, with a narrow bed, a table, a chair and a screen, and guards always near. A young servant girl named Rosalie, who looked after her there, later remembered that the Queen was always gentle and courteous with her, that she thanked her for every small kindness, and that she spent long hours sitting quietly, or mending her few clothes.

In the middle of October, she was brought before the Revolutionary Tribunal. The trial went on for two long days. She was tired and unwell. But she answered the questions calmly and carefully, with a dignity that surprised even some of those who had come to see her condemned. At one point she was asked to respond to a terrible accusation concerning her son. She stayed silent at first. Then, according to the record, she rose and said that she appealed to every mother in the room. And it is said that a murmur of sympathy passed through the crowd, even there. It did not change the verdict. In the early morning of the sixteenth of October, she was condemned.

Back in her room, before dawn, she wrote a last letter to her sister-in-law, Madame Élisabeth. It still survives, and it is one of the most moving documents of the age. In it she said that she was calm, as one is when one's conscience has nothing to reproach. She asked Élisabeth to look after her children. She asked that her son should never seek to avenge his parents' deaths. And she forgave all her enemies the harm they had done her. The letter, sadly, never reached Élisabeth. It was kept back, and found many years later.

Later that morning, the sixteenth of October, seventeen ninety-three, she was taken through the streets of Paris, past silent and crowded pavements, to the great square where her husband had died. She was thirty-seven years old. Witnesses said she was quiet and composed. One small story from that day is often told. As she climbed the steps, she stepped by accident on the foot of the executioner, and she turned to him and said, according to the account, pardon me, sir, I did not do it on purpose. They are said to have been her last words. And then, around midday, in the pale autumn light, her life came to its end.

Let us move on, gently, past that moment, and see what became of those she loved.

Her son, the little boy who had grown flowers in the garden of the Tuileries, did not live to grow up. He remained alone in the Temple, and died there in the summer of seventeen ninety-five, at the age of ten. But her daughter, Marie-Thérèse, survived. At the end of seventeen ninety-five, she was released and sent to Vienna, to her mother's family. She lived a long life, through many changes in France, and died in Austria in eighteen fifty-one, at the age of seventy-two. She carried her parents' memory with her always.

Axel von Fersen never forgot the Queen. He returned to Sweden, rose to high office, and kept her memory close until his own death, many years later. And in January eighteen fifteen, when a Bourbon king had returned to the throne of France, her remains and those of Louis the Sixteenth were carried in solemn procession to the ancient royal basilica of Saint-Denis, north of Paris, where the kings and queens of France lie at rest.

So who was she, in the end, this queen remembered for words she never said? She was not a saint, and she was not a monster. She was a lively, warm-hearted girl, poorly educated and sent very young into a world of enormous privilege and constant judgement. She spent too much, and she chose her friends unwisely, and she did not understand, until far too late, how her small pleasures looked to people who were hungry. But she was also a devoted mother, a loyal friend and a generous woman, who was slandered more cruelly than almost anyone of her time. And when everything was taken from her, she grew, quietly, into someone braver and wiser than the young Dauphine who had danced through the ballrooms of Versailles.

I wonder, as you drift towards sleep, what you think of her now. If you would like to, you can tell me in the comments, which part of her story will stay with you.

Let us end the way we began, in the little house at the bottom of the garden. It is a summer evening once more. The tall windows of the Petit Trianon are open, and the light has turned the colour of honey. Out beyond the lawns, the water is turning the wheel of the old mill, and the swallows are flying low over the pond. Somewhere a door closes softly. And in a quiet room, a young woman in a plain white dress sits at her harp, and plays a little tune she learned long ago, as a girl in Vienna.

The light fades. The gardens grow still. And there is nothing more you need to do tonight.

Goodnight. Sleep well.
