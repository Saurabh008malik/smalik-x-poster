## Chapter 8: The Painter and the Portraits

When we picture Marie Antoinette today, we usually see her through the eyes of one person. A soft, oval face. Large, pale blue eyes. Powdered hair, a little rose held in the fingers, a dress of shimmering silk, and an air of gentle, slightly distant grace. That picture was painted, again and again, by a remarkable woman named Élisabeth Vigée Le Brun.

She was born in Paris in seventeen fifty-five, the same year as the Queen, the daughter of a portrait painter who taught her to draw as a small child. Her father died when she was twelve, and before long she was painting portraits herself to help support her family. She was largely self-taught, and astonishingly gifted. By her early twenties she was one of the most fashionable portrait painters in Paris, at a time when very few women were allowed to work as professional artists at all.

In seventeen seventy-eight, she was called to Versailles to paint the Queen for the first time. The portrait was meant to be sent to Vienna, so that the Empress could see her daughter. Vigée Le Brun later wrote in her memoirs that she was terribly nervous, but that the Queen was kind and easy with her, and that they soon talked comfortably, often about music. They sang duets together, she said, though she added modestly that the Queen sang rather better than she did. Over the next ten years, she painted Marie Antoinette many times, perhaps around thirty portraits in all, including copies.

Her memoirs, written in old age, are full of affection for the Queen, and we should read them a little carefully, because by then she was looking back with great tenderness on a world that had vanished. But one small story from them has the feel of truth. Once, when the painter was expecting a child and came for a sitting, she dropped her paint brushes on the floor. As she bent to pick them up, the Queen stopped her, saying she was in no state to be stooping, and gathered up the brushes herself.

In seventeen eighty-three, Vigée Le Brun sent several portraits to the great public art exhibition in Paris, the Salon, held at the Louvre. Among them was a new picture of the Queen. In it, Marie Antoinette wore not a grand court gown, but a simple, loose dress of white muslin, tied with a sash, the kind of dress she wore in the gardens of the Trianon, with a soft straw hat on her head. It was a fresh, natural, lovely picture. And it caused a scandal.

To many visitors, the dress looked like an undergarment, a chemise. They were shocked to see the Queen of France displayed in public in what seemed to them her underclothes. Others complained that the light muslin came from abroad, and that the Queen was ruining the French silk weavers of Lyon by making such dresses fashionable. Within days, the portrait was taken down. Vigée Le Brun quickly painted a new version, in the same pose, but with the Queen now dressed in a proper gown of blue-grey silk, holding a rose. That second picture, Marie Antoinette with a Rose, became one of the best known of all her portraits. The episode was small. But it showed, again, how everything the Queen did could be turned against her.

That same year, with the Queen's support, Vigée Le Brun was admitted to the Royal Academy of Painting, one of only a handful of women ever to be accepted.

Meanwhile, the royal family was growing. On the twenty-seventh of March, seventeen eighty-five, the Queen gave birth to a second son, Louis Charles, who was given the title Duke of Normandy. He was a strong, lively, affectionate little boy, with fair curls and blue eyes, and his mother adored him. She called him, in her letters, her chou d'amour, her little cabbage of love, a tender French nickname. A year later, in July seventeen eighty-six, she had a second daughter, Sophie Hélène Béatrice.

The Queen was a careful, loving mother, more involved with her children than many royal mothers of her time. She chose their governesses thoughtfully. She wrote out instructions for their care, describing their characters, their fears and their little faults. She wanted them to be kind to servants and to the poor. On New Year's Day, it was said, she had toys brought in, showed them to her children, and then explained that this year the money would go instead to help poor families through the winter. Like so many stories, we have it from her friends. But it fits well with what we know of her as a mother.

There was sorrow, too. Little Sophie was never strong. In June seventeen eighty-seven, a few weeks before her first birthday, she died. The Queen was heartbroken. Those close to her said that she wept for a long time, and that for weeks afterwards she could hardly bear to speak of it.

At that very moment, Vigée Le Brun was at work on her most ambitious painting of the Queen, a large family portrait intended to show the French people a different Marie Antoinette, not a frivolous lady of fashion, but a dignified mother of the nation. In the finished picture, the Queen sits in a rich red velvet gown, with a dark feathered hat, in a room at Versailles. On her lap sits little Louis Charles. Madame Royale leans tenderly against her arm. And on the right, the young Dauphin, Louis Joseph, stands beside a cradle, lifting its cover and pointing inside. The cradle is empty. Baby Sophie had first been painted into the picture, and after her death, she was painted out. The empty cradle stayed. When the portrait was shown at the Salon in seventeen eighty-seven, many people were touched by it. It hangs at Versailles still, quiet and grave, with its little empty cradle in the corner.

The Queen herself had changed, too. She had lost some of her hair after the births of her children, and had adopted a simpler, lower style. She was over thirty now. The young girl of the poufs and feathers had become a mother, often tired, often worried, who preferred her children's company and a few close friends to the great balls of the court.

But by then, the picture that the French people had of her had already been painted, by other hands, and in much darker colours. In the next chapter, we will see how that happened, beginning with a necklace she never owned.
