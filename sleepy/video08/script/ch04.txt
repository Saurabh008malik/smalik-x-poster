## Chapter 4: The Young Dauphine and the Rules of Versailles

The Palace of Versailles, in seventeen seventy, was not so much a house as a small city. Several thousand people lived or worked there, nobles and servants, guards and cooks and gardeners, priests and musicians and clerks. Its main front stretched for hundreds of metres across the top of a gentle rise, and below it the gardens ran away towards the horizon in long avenues, terraces, fountains and a great cross-shaped canal. Inside, there were state rooms of coloured marble, ceilings painted with gods and victories, and the famous Hall of Mirrors, where seventeen tall windows looked out over the gardens and seventeen arches of mirrors gave the light back again.

It was magnificent. It was also, for a girl of fourteen, almost entirely without privacy.

Life at Versailles was arranged around a set of rules called etiquette, which had grown up over a century and which governed nearly everything. Who could sit in the presence of the royal family, and on what kind of chair. Who could hand the Dauphine her shift in the morning, and who had to stand aside if someone of higher rank walked in. Who could enter which rooms at which hours. The royal family dressed in front of courtiers, dined in front of courtiers, and even, on certain occasions, gave birth in front of courtiers. A person's rank was measured in the smallest details, and the courtiers watched those details very closely indeed.

In charge of making sure that the young Dauphine followed every rule was the Comtesse de Noailles. She was not unkind, but she was very strict, and Marie Antoinette, as we may now call her, found her exhausting. Before long, she had given the lady a nickname that went round the whole court. She called her Madame Etiquette.

We are lucky to have a good picture of her days in these years, because she wrote to her mother regularly, and some of those letters survive. In one of them, written in the summer of seventeen seventy, she described an ordinary day. She rose at about nine or ten, said her prayers and had breakfast. She went to visit the King's daughters, her husband's aunts, who lived in their own apartments in the palace. She went to Mass. She dined in public with her husband, while the people watched. In the afternoon there was some music, some reading, some needlework, a walk or a ride, cards in the evening, and then supper and bed. And she added, with a touch of humour, that she put on her rouge and washed her hands in front of the whole world.

Her mother's letters back were full of love, and full of instructions. Maria Theresa had another source of news, too. The Austrian ambassador in Paris, the Comte de Mercy-Argenteau, watched the young Dauphine closely and sent long, secret reports to Vienna about everything she did. So the Empress often knew more about her daughter's mistakes than her daughter realised, and her letters scolded her for things the girl thought nobody had noticed. Had she read anything serious this week? Was she being careful with her words? Why was she riding so much, when riding might spoil her chances of having a child?

That last question touched the deepest worry of those years. A royal marriage existed above all to produce an heir. But for a long time, and for reasons that historians still discuss, the young couple did not have the married life that would lead to children. The Dauphin was shy and inexperienced. There may have been a minor physical difficulty, or the problem may simply have been nerves and ignorance on both sides. We do not know for certain, and it is not our business to guess too much. What we do know is that it went on for seven years, that the whole court knew about it, that the gossip was cruel, and that the young Dauphine, who had been told that her whole value lay in giving France a prince, felt the weight of it very much.

There was another difficulty at court, and it had a name. Madame du Barry was the old King's favourite, his companion, a beautiful woman from a humble background who had risen to great influence. Marie Antoinette's aunts by marriage disliked her intensely, and the young Dauphine, wanting their friendship, took their side. She simply refused to speak to Madame du Barry. At Versailles, this was a serious matter. A lady of lower rank could not address the Dauphine first. She had to wait to be spoken to. And so the King's favourite waited, month after month, in silent humiliation, while the court looked on and took sides.

In the end, the King grew angry, the Austrian ambassador grew anxious, and Maria Theresa wrote firmly to her daughter that she must give way. On the first day of January, seventeen seventy-two, at the great New Year reception, Marie Antoinette at last turned towards Madame du Barry and spoke to her. The words she chose were famous ever after, because they were so small. She said, according to the accounts, that there were a great many people at Versailles today. That was all. But it was enough. The crisis passed. And the young Dauphine, privately, vowed that she would not speak to the woman again.

Not everything in these years was difficult. There were bright days too. She learned to ride, at first on donkeys and then on horses, and she followed the royal hunts through the forests around Versailles. She danced at the court balls, and danced well. She grew slowly fonder of her quiet husband, and he, in his awkward way, grew fond of her.

And on the eighth of June, seventeen seventy-three, she made her first formal visit to Paris. It was a warm summer day. The young couple drove into the city, heard Mass at the cathedral of Notre-Dame, and then went to the Tuileries Palace and stepped out onto a balcony over the gardens. Below them, a vast crowd had gathered, tens of thousands of people, cheering and waving their hats. One courtier, the Duc de Brissac, is said to have turned to her and told her, gallantly, that she had two hundred thousand admirers down there. She wrote to her mother afterwards that she had been deeply moved, and that the affection of the ordinary people, who had so many burdens of their own, was something precious.

It is worth pausing on that moment, because so much of her story would later turn on her relationship with the people of Paris. On that summer afternoon, at least, they loved her, and she loved them back.

Let us leave her on that balcony, in the warm June light, with the crowds cheering below, and in the next chapter we will see how, less than a year later, quite suddenly, the young Dauphine became a queen.
