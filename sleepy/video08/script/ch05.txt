## Chapter 5: A Queen at Eighteen

In the spring of seventeen seventy-four, the old King fell ill. It began, at the end of April, with a fever while he was staying at the small country house of the Trianon, in the gardens of Versailles. He was brought back to the palace, and within a day or two the doctors saw the marks they had feared. It was smallpox. Louis the Fifteenth was sixty-four years old, and he had never had the disease as a child.

For ten days the palace held its breath. The young Dauphin and Dauphine, who had never had smallpox either, were kept away from the sickroom for their own safety. The King's daughters nursed him bravely. And on the afternoon of the tenth of May, seventeen seventy-four, in a darkened room at Versailles, Louis the Fifteenth died.

According to a well-known account, a candle had been placed in the window of the King's room, and when it was put out, everyone knew. Then came a sound like distant thunder, the noise of hundreds of courtiers hurrying through the galleries towards the apartments of the new King and Queen. One of the Queen's ladies, Madame Campan, wrote many years later that the young couple fell to their knees and said together that they were too young to reign. We cannot be sure those were their exact words. Madame Campan was writing long afterwards, and she loved a good scene. But the feeling behind the story rings true. Louis the Sixteenth was not yet twenty. Marie Antoinette was eighteen.

What kind of king and queen would they be? The new King was a good man, honest and sincere, devout, interested in geography, science and the navy, and genuinely concerned for his people. He was also painfully hesitant. He found it hard to make up his mind, and harder still to stand by a decision once others began to argue with him. The new Queen was warm, charming and generous. She was also young, restless and, by her own later admission, very fond of pleasure.

In the summer of seventeen seventy-five, Louis was crowned in the ancient cathedral of Reims, where the kings of France had been crowned for centuries. It was the eleventh of June. The Queen was not crowned beside him, for that was not the custom of the time, but she watched from a special gallery. When the crown was placed on her husband's head and the great doors were opened to the people, and the cheering rolled in from the square outside, she was so moved that she burst into tears. She wrote to her mother afterwards that she had been unable to stop herself, and that the King had been pleased to see it. It was, she said, a day she would never forget.

Now that she was Queen, she could, for the first time, arrange her life a little more as she wished. And she began, gently at first and then more boldly, to push against the rules of Versailles.

She changed fashion. She worked closely with a talented dressmaker named Rose Bertin, a businesswoman from Picardy who had a shop in Paris, and who came to the palace twice a week with new designs. People began to call Rose Bertin, half admiringly and half mockingly, the Minister of Fashion. The Queen's hairdresser, a man known simply as Léonard, created towering arrangements of powdered hair, called poufs, decorated with feathers, ribbons, jewels and sometimes whole little scenes. One famous pouf, at the time of the King's smallpox inoculation, is said to have shown a serpent, a club and a rising sun. Whatever the Queen wore, the ladies of the court rushed to copy, and then the ladies of Paris, and then the ladies of every provincial town in France.

Her mother, far away in Vienna, was not impressed. When a portrait arrived showing her daughter with a great crown of feathers, the Empress wrote, rather sharply, that she had thought it was a picture of an actress, not of a queen.

There were other pleasures, too. The Queen loved the theatre and the opera, and she supported her old music teacher Gluck when his operas came to Paris, standing up for him against critics who preferred the Italian style. She loved the masked balls at the Paris Opera, and she sometimes stayed out until dawn. And she gambled at cards, especially at a fast game called faro, for stakes that worried her husband's ministers. None of this was unusual for a rich young noblewoman of the time. But she was not just any noblewoman. She was the Queen, and everything she did was watched, and talked about, and slowly added up.

She chose her own friends, too. One of them was the Princesse de Lamballe, a gentle, rather fragile young widow from the house of Savoy, who was given a high post in the Queen's household and who remained loyal to her to the very end. Another, a little later, was the Comtesse de Polignac, a charming, easy-going woman with a lovely, calm manner, around whom the Queen gathered a small circle of companions. With them, she could laugh and talk freely, away from the eyes of the court. But the Polignacs were not rich, and over the years the Queen's favour brought them positions, pensions and gifts. The rest of the court noticed, and resented it.

It is easy, looking back, to judge her harshly for these years. She was frivolous, and she spent too much. She knew it, too, in her calmer moments. But it is fair to remember how young she was, how lonely her marriage still was, and how little anyone had prepared her for the real work of being a queen. Her mother had trained her to please, to charm and to produce an heir. Nobody had trained her to think about the finances of a great kingdom, or about how her small pleasures might look to a family in Paris who could barely afford bread.

Let us leave her in the glittering ballrooms of those early years, with the music playing and the candles burning low, and in the next chapter we will follow her out of the palace altogether, down a long garden path, to a small house of her own.
