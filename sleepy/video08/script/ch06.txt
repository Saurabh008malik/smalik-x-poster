## Chapter 6: A Small House at the Bottom of the Garden

If you walk today from the great palace of Versailles, out past the fountains and down the long avenues of trees, you come after a while to a quieter part of the estate. The paths grow narrower. The formal hedges give way to lawns and groves. And among them stands a small, square house of pale stone, with tall windows and a flat roof and a simple row of columns on its front. It is called the Petit Trianon, the little Trianon. It was here, more than anywhere else, that Marie Antoinette made her home.

The house was not built for her. It was designed in the seventeen-sixties by the King's architect, Ange-Jacques Gabriel, for Louis the Fifteenth, who wanted a private retreat from the palace. It is often admired as one of the most perfectly balanced buildings of its age, calm and elegant, without a single unnecessary ornament. Soon after he became King, in seventeen seventy-four, Louis the Sixteenth gave it to his young wife as her own. A pretty story says that he told her she loved flowers, and so he had a bouquet for her, and that the bouquet was the Petit Trianon. Like so many stories, it may have been improved in the telling. But the gift was real, and she treasured it.

At the Petit Trianon, for the first time in her life, she was mistress of her own house. The usual rules did not apply there. Orders were given not in the name of the King, but in the name of the Queen. Courtiers could not simply come and go. They had to be invited. When she walked into a room, the ladies there did not have to stop what they were doing and stand. They could go on with their needlework, or their music, or their conversation. It sounds like a very small freedom. At the court of Versailles, it was almost a revolution.

Let us go inside, quietly. The rooms are light and not very large. The walls are panelled in soft colours, carved with delicate garlands of flowers, wheat ears and little baskets. In one small room, called the room of the moving mirrors, there was a clever mechanism. At the turn of a handle, large mirrored panels rose up from the floor to cover the windows, so that the room could be closed off from the world outside and filled with reflected candlelight. Upstairs there were small, simple bedrooms for her friends. Outside the windows, everywhere, there were flowers.

It was the gardens that she changed most of all. The old King had liked botanical gardens, with neat beds of rare plants laid out in rows. Marie Antoinette preferred the new fashion that had come over from England, the landscape garden, where nature seemed to have been left to itself, though in fact every curve and every tree had been carefully planned. With her architect, Richard Mique, and the painter Hubert Robert, she created a gentle, rolling garden of winding paths, a stream that seemed to flow by chance, little hills, a grotto hidden among rocks, and a lake with an island. On the island stood a round temple of white columns, the Temple of Love, with a statue of Cupid inside. On a low hill above the lake stood a small eight-sided pavilion called the Belvedere, its walls painted inside with delicate designs, where on summer evenings she gave little concerts and the gardens were lit with lanterns.

She built a theatre there, too, a small, intimate jewel of a theatre, painted blue and gold, hidden away so that it could hardly be seen from the paths. On its stage, the Queen herself acted in plays with her friends and family, for a tiny audience of invited guests. In seventeen eighty-five, she played the young heroine in a comedy by Beaumarchais, The Barber of Seville. The King came to watch, and laughed and applauded. One can imagine how it looked to the rest of the court. A queen of France was painting her face and acting on a stage, like a performer, before a small circle of favourites who had been let in while everyone else was kept outside.

And then, in the early seventeen-eighties, she began her most famous project of all. Beyond the lake, on the far side of the gardens, Richard Mique built for her a small village. It was called the Hameau, the hamlet. It was a cluster of a dozen thatched cottages around a pond, built to look old and simple and rustic, with crooked walls, little gardens, climbing plants, a water mill with a turning wheel, a dairy, a dovecote, and a tall stone tower called the Marlborough Tower, after a popular French song about the English Duke of Marlborough, from which fishing nets could be lowered into the pond.

It was not quite a toy. The hamlet had a real working farm attached to it, run by a farmer and his family, with cows, sheep, goats, hens and fields. Milk and eggs and vegetables from the farm went to the Queen's table. Her children could come and see the animals. But it was also, undeniably, a beautiful fantasy, a dream of country life seen by someone who would never have to live it. The famous story that the Queen dressed as a shepherdess and milked the cows herself is mostly legend. There was a pretty dairy where milk was served in fine porcelain, and she certainly enjoyed the pastoral setting, but the real work of the farm was done by the people who were paid to do it.

What did it all mean to her? We can only guess, but the guess is not difficult. At Versailles she was watched every moment. At the Petit Trianon she could walk in the morning in a plain white muslin dress and a straw hat, with no guards and no ceremony. She could play with her children on the grass, listen to music in the evening, and talk to her friends as an ordinary woman talks. It was the only place in France where she was free.

But there was a price. Every courtier who was not invited felt the insult. Every story of the theatre, the lanterns and the thatched cottages travelled outwards, to Paris and beyond, and grew in the telling. People said the Queen had covered the walls of the Trianon with diamonds. It was not true. But it was believed. And slowly, without quite noticing it, she was turning the most powerful people at court into enemies.

Let us leave her there, in the evening, on the little bridge beside the mill, with the water turning the wheel and the swallows flying low over the pond, and in the next chapter we will watch a long-hoped-for change come at last into her life.
