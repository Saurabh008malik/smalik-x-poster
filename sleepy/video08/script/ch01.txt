## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Queen Remembered for Words She Never Said

Hello, and welcome. Tonight we are going to spend a long, slow evening with a young woman who has been talked about for more than two hundred years, and who was very often misunderstood. Her name was Marie Antoinette. She was born a little archduchess in Vienna, she was sent to France at fourteen to marry a prince she had never met, and at eighteen she became Queen of France. She lived in the most famous palace in Europe, and she spent her happiest hours not in its great gilded rooms, but in a small, plain house at the bottom of the gardens, among her flowers and her children.

Let us begin there, in that small house, on an evening in late summer. Imagine it with me. The air is warm and still. The tall windows of the Petit Trianon are open, and the light outside has turned the colour of honey. Somewhere beyond the lawns there is a little stream, and an artificial lake, and a cluster of thatched cottages built to look like a country village. A few sheep are being walked home. Inside, a young woman in a simple white dress, with her powdered hair loosely tied, is sitting at a harp, picking out a tune she learned as a girl in Vienna. There are no courtiers here unless she invites them. There is no crowd watching her eat. For an hour or two, she is not the Queen. She is simply a woman in a quiet room at the end of a summer day.

That scene is imagined, of course. We do not know which evening it was, or which tune she played. But we do know that she loved that house, that she played the harp and sang, and that she went there to escape the endless ceremony of the palace. Hold that picture gently in your mind, because we will come back to it before the night is over.

Now here is the strange thing about her, the puzzle at the centre of her life. Ask almost anyone what Marie Antoinette said, and they will tell you that when she heard the poor had no bread, she answered, let them eat cake. It is the most famous line in her story. And there is no good evidence that she ever said it. A very similar remark, about a great princess and some poor peasants, appears in the memoirs of the writer Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in a passage he wrote in the seventeen-sixties, when Marie Antoinette was still a girl in Austria, before she ever set foot in France. The story was older than she was. It was simply attached to her name, the way old stories so often attach themselves to famous people.

So, a queen remembered for words she never spoke. That is the question we will carry with us tonight, quietly, the way you might carry a candle down a long corridor. Who was she, really, underneath all the stories? A spoiled girl who cared only for diamonds and dances? Or a young woman placed, very early, in a role far too large for her, who made real mistakes, who grew, and who faced the end of her life with more courage than anyone had expected? As we will see, the honest answer is a little of the first, and a great deal of the second.

If you enjoy calm stories like this one, you are very welcome to subscribe, so that you can find the next one when you need it. But for now, there is nothing at all you need to do. You might like to pull the blanket a little closer, let your shoulders soften, and breathe out slowly. There is no need to remember the names or the dates. If you drift away halfway through, that is exactly as it should be. The story will keep walking on beside you.

Wherever the record is uncertain, I will tell you so. Many of the stories told about Marie Antoinette were written by her enemies, in angry pamphlets meant to destroy her. Many others were written by her friends and servants, years later, in memoirs meant to make her a saint. The truth usually lies somewhere in between, and sometimes we simply do not know. And though her life ended in a hard time, we will pass through those last chapters gently, without lingering on anything harsh. This is a story for rest.

Now, let us go back to the beginning, to a city on the river Danube, and to a November day in the year seventeen hundred and fifty-five.

Vienna in those years was the heart of a great empire. It was a city of church bells and bakeries, of narrow streets that wound in towards a vast cathedral with a tall, needle-thin spire. At its centre stood the Hofburg, the winter palace of the Habsburg family, a sprawling place of courtyards and staircases and long, cold galleries that had grown, wing by wing, over hundreds of years. And in that palace lived one of the most remarkable rulers of her age, the Empress Maria Theresa.

Maria Theresa had come to the throne as a young woman, and she had held her lands together through years of war, when it seemed that every neighbour in Europe wanted a piece of them. She was clever, stubborn, devout and hard-working. She read state papers late into the night. She was also a mother on a scale that is hard to imagine today. With her husband, the Emperor Francis Stephen, she had sixteen children. She loved them, in her way, but she also saw them as part of her great work. Her daughters, especially, were to be married into the royal houses of Europe, so that the family of Habsburg would be bound by ties of blood to its old rivals.

On the second of November, seventeen hundred and fifty-five, in the Hofburg, the Empress gave birth to her fifteenth child, a little girl. She was christened with a long string of names, as royal children were, Maria Antonia Josepha Johanna. At home she would be called Antonia, or simply Antoine. The French form of her name, Marie Antoinette, still lay far in the future.

The day before her birth, the first of November, a terrible earthquake had struck the city of Lisbon, far away in Portugal. The news took days to travel across Europe, so nobody in Vienna knew of it on the morning she was born. But later, people who liked to look for signs remembered the date, and said that it had been an unlucky beginning. We do not need to believe in omens to notice how the story of her life was already being shaped, even then, by what other people chose to say about her.

For now, though, she was simply a new baby in a very large family, wrapped in linen, carried through the cold corridors of the palace to a nursery full of older brothers and sisters. Outside, the first frosts of winter were settling on the rooftops of Vienna. The bells of the cathedral rang the hours. And the little archduchess slept, knowing nothing at all of France.

Let us leave her there for a moment, sleeping, while the snow begins to fall over the city, and in the next chapter we will watch her grow up, in the palaces and gardens of an imperial childhood.
