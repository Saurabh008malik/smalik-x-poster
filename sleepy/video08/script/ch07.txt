## Chapter 7: A Brother's Visit, and the Gift of Children

In the spring of seventeen seventy-seven, a traveller arrived at Versailles under a false name. He called himself the Count of Falkenstein, and he travelled simply, with very few servants, staying at an ordinary inn in the town rather than in the palace. Everyone knew perfectly well who he was. He was Joseph the Second, Holy Roman Emperor, Marie Antoinette's eldest brother, and he had come, at his mother's request, to see for himself how his little sister was living, and to try to help.

Joseph was fourteen years older than the Queen. He was intelligent, serious, rather severe, and full of ideas about how kingdoms ought to be run. He disliked the luxury and ceremony of Versailles, and he was not shy about saying so. He watched his sister for several weeks. He went with her to the Paris Opera, to the gaming tables, to her little suppers with her friends, and he did not like what he saw. Before he left, he wrote her a long, frank letter of advice, gently but firmly telling her that she was wasting her time and her reputation on pleasures, and that one day, if she did not change, there could be a terrible reckoning. It was a remarkable warning, and it was not entirely wrong.

But Joseph also did something more helpful. He spoke privately with his brother-in-law the King, man to man, about the difficulty in the royal marriage. We do not know exactly what was said. Joseph's own letters suggest that the problem had been mostly a matter of ignorance and nerves on both sides. Whatever the advice was, it worked. In the summer of seventeen seventy-seven, more than seven years after their wedding, Louis and Marie Antoinette became husband and wife in the full sense at last. The Queen wrote joyfully to her mother that she was now truly happy, and the King, it was said, told one of his aunts how much he was enjoying married life.

About this time, a young Swedish nobleman returned to the court of Versailles. His name was Count Axel von Fersen. He was tall, handsome, quiet and courteous, and he had first met the Queen several years earlier, at a masked ball at the Paris Opera in seventeen seventy-four, when she was still the Dauphine and he was a young man on a grand tour. Now, in seventeen seventy-eight, he came back, and the Queen welcomed him warmly. They became friends, and over the years that friendship grew very deep.

How deep, exactly, has been one of the great quiet questions of her life. Fersen's letters and diaries show that he was devoted to her, and in recent years scholars have used new techniques to read lines that had been blacked out in some of their letters, which suggest a strong and tender attachment. Whether it was ever more than that, we do not know, and the honest answer is that we probably never will. What is certain is that he was one of the most loyal friends she ever had, and that he would risk his life for her.

He did not stay long, at first. France had entered the American War of Independence, allied with the new United States against Britain. The famous American envoy Benjamin Franklin was living just outside Paris, at Passy, and was received by the King at Versailles in the spring of seventeen seventy-eight, in a plain brown coat and without a wig, to the delight of the fashionable world. Fersen joined the French army that sailed for America, served as an aide to its commander, the Comte de Rochambeau, and was present at the great victory at Yorktown in seventeen eighty-one.

Meanwhile, at Versailles, something wonderful had happened. In the spring of seventeen seventy-eight, the Queen discovered that she was expecting a child.

The birth took place on the nineteenth of December, seventeen seventy-eight. And it took place, as royal births always had, in public. When the moment came, the Queen's bedroom filled with courtiers, officials and curious onlookers, so many that some climbed onto the furniture to see. The windows had been sealed against the winter cold. The room grew hot and airless, and after the baby was born, the exhausted mother fainted. According to Madame Campan, the King himself, with great strength, tore open the sealed windows to let in the cold air. Mother and child both recovered. And after that day, the King made sure that future royal births would be watched by far fewer people.

The baby was a girl. She was named Marie-Thérèse Charlotte, after her grandmother the Empress, and she was given the title Madame Royale. France had hoped for a boy, an heir to the throne. But her mother loved her at once. Madame Campan later wrote that the Queen held the baby and told her softly that a son would have belonged to the nation, but that a daughter would belong to her. Whether or not those were her exact words, it is clear that motherhood changed her. She became calmer, more serious, more often at home.

Two years later came a great sorrow. On the twenty-ninth of November, seventeen eighty, the Empress Maria Theresa died in Vienna, at the age of sixty-three. Mother and daughter had not seen each other for more than ten years. They had written constantly, sometimes lovingly, often anxiously, and the Empress had worried about her youngest daughter to the very end. When the news reached Versailles, the Queen was overcome with grief. She shut herself away for days. Whatever their quarrels, she had loved her mother deeply, and now the voice that had guided her, scolded her and comforted her across all those miles had fallen silent.

And then, less than a year later, came the joy that France had waited for. On the twenty-second of October, seventeen eighty-one, the Queen gave birth to a son. The baby was named Louis Joseph, and as the King's eldest son, he became the Dauphin, the heir to the throne. The bells of Versailles rang, and then the bells of Paris, and cannons were fired, and the city celebrated with fireworks and dancing in the streets. Trade guilds came to the palace in procession to offer their congratulations, with music and gifts. Many said that the King, usually so reserved, was openly in tears with happiness.

For a little while, everything seemed to be well. The Queen had a daughter and a son. Her marriage had grown into a real partnership of affection. And the people of Paris were, once again, cheering her name.

Let us rest in that happy autumn, with the bells still ringing faintly over the rooftops, and in the next chapter we will meet the painter who gave us the faces of the Queen and her children, and look a little more closely at how those faces were seen.
