## Chapter 3: The Blacking Warehouse and the Marshalsea

This chapter holds the hardest season of Charles Dickens's childhood. I will tell it gently, because it was a sad time, but I will not leave it out, because without it we cannot really understand him, or the books he would go on to write.

By the end of eighteen twenty-three, John Dickens's debts had grown far beyond anything his salary could cover. The family had moved again, this time to a larger house in Gower Street, where Elizabeth Dickens hoped to open a small school for young ladies. A brass plate was fixed to the door. Charles, by his own later account, went round the neighbourhood pushing leaflets through letterboxes. Not a single pupil ever came. And meanwhile, the bills went on arriving.

It was at about this time that a relative of the family, a young man named Lamert, who was involved in a business making boot blacking, offered to take Charles on at the works. Blacking was a kind of polish, sold in little stone pots, used to make boots shine. The works stood at Hungerford Stairs, beside the Thames, just off the Strand, near where Charing Cross station stands today. The boy's parents accepted.

Charles was about twelve years old. The usual date given is early in eighteen twenty-four, just after his twelfth birthday, although one recent historian, studying old court papers, has argued that he may have started a few months earlier, while he was still only eleven. Either way, he was a child.

He later described the building in a private account he wrote for his closest friend, John Forster. It was a crazy, tumbledown old house, he said, its wainscoted rooms and rotten floors and staircase overrun with rats, which could be heard squeaking and scuffling on the old stairs. It stood right over the river, and the tide came and went beneath it. His work was to cover the pots of blacking, first with a piece of oiled paper and then with a piece of blue paper, to tie them round with string, to clip the paper neatly, and then to paste on a printed label. He did this for about ten hours a day, six days a week, sitting at a table near a window. He was paid, as he remembered it, six or seven shillings a week.

The other boys who worked beside him were kind enough. One of them was named Bob Fagin. He looked after Charles once when the old pain in his side came on, filling empty blacking bottles with hot water to warm him. Dickens never forgot that kindness. And yet, many years later, he gave the name Fagin to one of the darkest characters he ever created. Nobody knows exactly why. It is one of the small puzzles of his life.

Then, only a short time after Charles began at the works, the blow fell. In February of eighteen twenty-four, John Dickens was arrested for debt. He was taken to the Marshalsea, an old prison in Southwark, south of the river, where people who could not pay what they owed were held until the debt was settled. It was a strange, half-closed world, with its own shops and rooms, where whole families might live together behind the high wall. Before long, Elizabeth Dickens and the younger children moved into the prison too, to be with him. That was common at the time. It saved the cost of a separate home.

But Charles did not go with them. He was found a lodging, first in Camden Town, with an elderly lady who took in children, and later in a little back attic in Lant Street, in Southwark, much nearer to the prison. Each morning he walked to the blacking works and spent the day tying up pots. On Sundays he collected Fanny from the Royal Academy of Music, and they went together to spend the day with the family in the Marshalsea. Some evenings he would go to the prison and have supper with them before the gates were locked.

He managed his own small wages. He bought his own bread, and sometimes a slice of pudding from a cook's shop, or a cup of coffee and a slice of bread and butter at a coffee shop. He wandered the streets in the evenings, looking into shop windows, watching people, feeling, he wrote later, utterly neglected and without hope. He remembered being so young and so small that, when he went into a public house to ask for a glass of beer, the landlord and his wife looked at him with a kind of wonder.

The Marshalsea was a place he would return to in his writing again and again, most fully in his novel Little Dorrit, nearly thirty years later, whose heroine is born and raised inside its walls. The image of a debtors' prison, of a family caught by money and shame, runs through his books like a dark thread.

After a few months, the family's fortunes turned. John Dickens's mother died, and left him a legacy of four hundred and fifty pounds. With that, and with legal help to arrange his affairs, he was able to leave the Marshalsea. He was released at the end of May, eighteen twenty-four, after something like fourteen weeks.

You might expect that Charles, too, would be released at once from the blacking works. But he was not. He went on working there for some months more, after the business moved to new premises in Chandos Street, in Covent Garden. There, by his own account, he and Bob Fagin worked in a window facing the street, and passers-by would sometimes stop to watch how quickly their hands moved.

At last, after a quarrel between his father and the Lamert relative, John Dickens took his son away and said he should go back to school. And here is the line that has moved so many readers. Elizabeth Dickens, it seems, tried to smooth over the quarrel and to have Charles sent back to the works. Dickens wrote, many years afterwards, that he never afterwards forgot, and never could forget, that his mother was warm for his being sent back.

The whole time at the blacking works may have lasted around a year, perhaps a little less, perhaps a little more. Dickens himself said he had no idea how long it had been. But to a child, it felt endless.

And here is the most remarkable thing of all. For almost the whole of his life, Charles Dickens told nobody about it. Not his wife at first, not his children, not the public who came to know him as the cheerful, famous author of Pickwick. Only when he was in his middle thirties did he write it down, in that private account for John Forster. His father and mother never spoke of it. He himself avoided the old streets near Hungerford Stairs for years. The secret became known to the world only after his death, when Forster published it in his biography.

And yet, though he hid the facts, he never hid the feeling. It is there in Oliver Twist, the workhouse orphan asking for more. It is there in David Copperfield, the small boy labelling bottles in a dark warehouse by the river. It is there in the poor children who crowd the edges of so many of his books. And it is there, perhaps most of all, in two thin, hungry figures who would appear one Christmas night from beneath the robe of a great spirit, a boy and a girl, whom the spirit would call Ignorance and Want.

Let us leave Charles now, at the end of that hard year, walking away from the blacking works for the last time. He is twelve or thirteen years old, a little thinner, a little quieter, carrying something inside him that he will not name for more than twenty years. In the next chapter, he goes back to school, and begins, step by step, to climb.
