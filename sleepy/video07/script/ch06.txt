## Chapter 6: Mr. Pickwick, Sam Weller, and a Sorrow in Doughty Street

The idea behind Pickwick was simple, and at first it was not really Dickens's idea at all. The publishers wanted a series of comic pictures of a club of foolish London gentlemen going out into the country to hunt and fish and shoot, and getting into scrapes. The artist was to be Robert Seymour, a well-known illustrator of sporting scenes. Dickens was to write the words around the pictures.

But Dickens, from the very start, gently turned the plan around. He said he knew little about sport, and that it would be better if the pictures followed the story, rather than the story following the pictures. And so it was the words that led. He invented Mr. Samuel Pickwick, a round, kindly, bald old gentleman in gaiters and spectacles, the founder of the Pickwick Club, who sets out with three companions to travel about England and observe its people. There is Mr. Tupman, who falls in love too easily. Mr. Snodgrass, who writes poetry. And Mr. Winkle, who claims to be a great sportsman and cannot ride a horse or fire a gun.

The first numbers sold only modestly. Then came a sad interruption. In April eighteen thirty-six, the artist Robert Seymour, who had been in poor health and low spirits, died suddenly. The series nearly stopped. A new illustrator was found, a young man named Hablot Knight Browne, who signed his work with the name Phiz, to go with Boz. He would go on to illustrate many of Dickens's books over the next twenty years.

And then, in the fourth number, published in the summer of eighteen thirty-six, Mr. Pickwick walks into the yard of an old coaching inn in Southwark, called the White Hart. And there, cleaning boots, is a young man named Sam Weller.

Sam is quick, cheerful, sharp-tongued and endlessly loyal. He speaks in a London accent that turns every v into a w, and he has a habit of comic sayings, each one starting with a familiar phrase and ending somewhere quite unexpected. Mr. Pickwick takes him on as his manservant, and from that moment the book comes alive. The friendship between the innocent old gentleman and the worldly young Londoner became one of the most loved partnerships in English fiction.

The sales leapt. The first numbers had been printed in small quantities. By the end of the series, in the autumn of eighteen thirty-seven, Chapman and Hall were printing around forty thousand copies of each monthly part. People read Pickwick everywhere. Shopkeepers read it behind their counters. Judges read it in their rooms. There were Pickwick hats, Pickwick canes, Pickwick cigars, and countless imitations and pirated stage versions. A story, perhaps exaggerated, told of a clergyman who had comforted a dying man, and heard him murmur, as the visitor left, that at least the next number of Pickwick would be out in a few days.

Charles Dickens, aged just twenty-five, had become famous.

He did not slow down. Far from it. Even before Pickwick was finished, he had agreed to edit a new monthly magazine called Bentley's Miscellany. And in its pages, from early in eighteen thirty-seven, he began a second story, running at the same time as the first. It was very different in tone. It began in a workhouse, in a small unnamed town, with the birth of an orphan boy. It was called Oliver Twist.

Most of us know at least one scene from Oliver Twist. The hungry boys in the workhouse, drawing lots to decide which of them must ask for more. Oliver, small and pale, walking up to the master with his bowl in his hand. Then London, and the Artful Dodger, and the den of young pickpockets, and the frightening figure of Fagin, and the violent Bill Sikes, and kind-hearted, doomed Nancy. Dickens was angry about the new Poor Law of eighteen thirty-four, which had made the workhouses deliberately harsh, to discourage the poor from asking for help. Oliver Twist was, in part, his reply.

We should say plainly here that the figure of Fagin has troubled readers for a long time. Dickens called him again and again simply the Jew, and drew him in the ugly prejudices of his age. Years later, after a Jewish reader, Eliza Davis, wrote to him to say how much harm such portraits did, Dickens made changes to later printings of the book, cutting many of those references, and created a gentle, good Jewish character, Mr. Riah, in his novel Our Mutual Friend. It is a part of his story that deserves to be told honestly.

Meanwhile, at home, life was changing too. In January eighteen thirty-seven, his first child was born, a son, named Charles after his father. And in the spring, the family moved from the cramped rooms at Furnival's Inn to a proper house at forty-eight Doughty Street, in Bloomsbury. It was a tall, handsome, flat-fronted Georgian house, in a quiet street with a gate and a porter at each end. Today it is the Charles Dickens Museum, and it is the only one of his London homes that still stands.

Catherine's sister Mary Hogarth came to live with them there. She was seventeen, bright, warm and full of affection, and Dickens adored her. She seems to have been the kind of person who made a house feel lighter simply by being in it.

Then, on a May evening in eighteen thirty-seven, only a few weeks after they had moved in, Charles, Catherine and Mary went together to the theatre. They came home late, happy and tired. Mary went up to her room. And soon afterwards, she collapsed. A doctor was called. Through the night they did everything they could. But the next afternoon, the seventh of May, she died in Charles's arms. She was seventeen years old. Nobody knows for certain what illness took her so suddenly. It may have been a weakness of the heart.

Dickens was shattered. For the only time in his working life, he missed his deadlines. That month, no new number of Pickwick appeared, and no new chapter of Oliver Twist. He took a ring from Mary's finger and wore it for the rest of his life. He wrote the words for her gravestone himself. And for many years afterwards, he hoped that he might one day be buried beside her.

Her memory never left him. Many readers believe that the pure, gentle young women in his novels, and above all Little Nell, the heroine of a book he would write a few years later, owe something to Mary Hogarth.

It is late now, and the house in Doughty Street is quiet. Let us leave Charles there for a little while, at his desk by the window, a young man suddenly famous and suddenly grieving, picking up his pen again because there are readers waiting and a family to support. In the next chapter, the books keep coming, one after another, like carriages along a busy road.
