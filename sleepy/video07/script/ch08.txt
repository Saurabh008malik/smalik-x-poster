## Chapter 8: A Winter Crossing, and Six Months in America

On the fourth of January, eighteen forty-two, Charles and Catherine Dickens sailed from Liverpool on a paddle steamer called the Britannia, one of the first ships of the new Cunard line. With them went Catherine's maid, Anne Brown. The four children stayed behind in London, in the care of Catherine's family and of Dickens's close friend, the actor William Macready. Before they left, Dickens had the children's portrait painted by his friend Daniel Maclise, and he took the little picture with him, and set it up wherever they stayed.

It was a terrible crossing. The Atlantic in January is not a gentle sea, and the Britannia rolled and pitched through storm after storm for more than two weeks. Dickens later described their tiny cabin, which he compared to a box, and the beds that were little more than shelves, and the long nights of seasickness when the whole ship seemed to stand on end. One night, in a great gale, the paddle boxes were damaged and a lifeboat was smashed. Catherine was badly frightened. Dickens, though he was sick too, tried to keep everyone's spirits up, and organised card games in the saloon when the weather allowed.

At last, on the twenty-second of January, the ship came into Boston harbour. And the welcome was overwhelming. Crowds gathered to see him. Young men followed him through the streets. People wrote to him, called on him, stared at him through windows, and snipped bits of fur from his coat as souvenirs. There were dinners and balls and receptions in his honour, one after another. At one great ball in New York, some three thousand people crammed into a theatre to see him. Nothing like it had happened before to a visiting writer.

He liked a great deal of what he saw at first. He admired Boston very much, its clean streets and handsome houses, and above all its public institutions: its hospitals, its schools, and an institution for the blind, where he met a young woman named Laura Bridgman, who had lost her sight and hearing as a small child and had been taught to read and to communicate by touch. He wrote about her with deep admiration. He visited the mill town of Lowell, in Massachusetts, where young women worked in the cotton factories, and was impressed by how well dressed and healthy they seemed, and by the fact that they ran their own literary magazine. He made warm friendships, including with the poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow and the Harvard professor Cornelius Felton.

But soon, things began to go wrong.

Dickens had come to America with one practical aim. There was no international copyright law at that time. That meant American publishers could print British books without paying the authors anything at all. Dickens's novels sold in vast numbers in the United States, and he earned nothing from them. In his speeches, he began to raise the subject, politely but firmly. He said that it was unjust, and that it harmed American writers too, because publishers preferred to print free British books rather than pay their own authors.

Many Americans were offended. Newspapers attacked him. They said he was greedy, that he had come as an honoured guest and was now complaining about money. Dickens was hurt and angry. His friends in Britain, at his request, sent a letter of support signed by many well-known authors. But nothing changed. An international copyright agreement between Britain and America would not come until eighteen ninety-one, more than twenty years after his death.

As the weeks went by, Dickens grew tired of the crowds and the attention. He wrote home that he could hardly do anything at all, not even take a quiet walk, without a crowd gathering around him. He disliked the habit of chewing and spitting tobacco, which seemed to be everywhere, in hotels, in courtrooms, on the floors of trains and steamboats. He found some of the newspapers coarse and cruel. And then he travelled south, to Washington, where he met President John Tyler, and briefly into Virginia, where he saw slavery for himself. What he saw there filled him with horror and disgust. He cut short his travels in the South and turned west.

They went by rail and canal boat and stagecoach across the Allegheny Mountains, and by steamboat down the Ohio River to Cincinnati and Louisville, and on to St. Louis on the Mississippi. He saw the great flat prairie, which he found rather disappointing. Then they travelled north again, to the Great Lakes, and to the place that moved him most of all on the whole journey: Niagara Falls.

He wrote that he felt there, standing near the great fall, a sense of peace and calm, and of being close to his Creator. He thought of Mary Hogarth, and felt that she might have been there with him. They stayed for more than a week, and he went again and again to look at the falls, at every hour of the day, and in different lights.

Let us stay there with him for a moment, beside the great falls, with the endless roar of water, and the clouds of spray rising, and rainbows hanging in the mist. Let the sound wash over you, steady and calm.

From Niagara, they crossed into Canada, to Toronto and Kingston and Montreal, where Dickens, who had loved acting since boyhood, organised and starred in some amateur theatricals with officers of the British garrison, and Catherine took part too. And then, on the seventh of June, they sailed from New York for home.

They arrived back in London at the end of June, eighteen forty-two, and went straight to the house where the children were staying. It is said that the youngest, little Walter, still a baby, did not recognise them, and that the excitement was so great that one of the children fell ill with it. Dickens was overjoyed to be home.

He wrote a travel book about the journey, called American Notes, which came out that autumn. It was honest about the things he disliked, and some American readers were angry again. Then he began a new novel, Martin Chuzzlewit, in which the young hero sails to America and has a series of miserable adventures there. Its American chapters were savagely funny about American boastfulness and greed. American readers were furious.

But at home, Martin Chuzzlewit, for all its brilliance, did not sell as well as his earlier books. Its monthly sales were far below Pickwick's. His publishers grew worried. There was talk of reducing the money they paid him each month. Catherine was expecting their fifth child. His father was still borrowing money and asking for more. And by the autumn of eighteen forty-three, Charles Dickens, the most famous author in the English-speaking world, was seriously worried about how he was going to pay his bills.

It was out of those worries, and out of something much deeper, that his most famous story was about to be born.
