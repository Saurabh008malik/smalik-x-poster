## Chapter 9: Six Weeks in the Dark Streets: Writing A Christmas Carol

We have come to the heart of our story. Let us slow right down now, and take our time, because this is the season of Charles Dickens's life that most of the world remembers, even people who have never read a single one of his novels.

It is the year eighteen forty-three. Dickens is thirty-one. He lives with Catherine and their four children in the house at Devonshire Terrace, beside Regent's Park, and a fifth child is on the way. Martin Chuzzlewit is coming out in monthly numbers, and it is not selling well. Money is tight. And all around him, in the city he walks through every day, he sees poverty that troubles him deeply.

Earlier that year, a government commission had published a report on the employment of children in mines and factories. It described children of five, six and seven years old working long hours in terrible conditions, in coal mines, in potteries, in mills. Dickens read it, and was shaken. He wrote to one of the commissioners that he was so stricken by it that he was thinking of writing a pamphlet, an appeal to the people of England on behalf of the poor man's child. He thought about it for a while. And then he wrote back to say that he had changed his mind. He had thought of something that would strike, he said, with twenty thousand times the force.

He did not say what it was.

That same September, he visited a school in one of the poorest parts of London, in a street called Field Lane, near Saffron Hill. It was a ragged school, one of a new kind of free school set up by volunteers, in rented rooms, to teach the poorest children of the streets to read and write, and to give them a little kindness. The children who came there were often hungry, dirty and wild. Many had no homes at all. Dickens was horrified by what he saw, and moved by the patience of the teachers. He wrote to his friend Angela Burdett-Coutts, a very rich young woman who gave much of her fortune to good causes, asking her to help.

Then, early in October, he travelled north to Manchester, the great city of cotton mills. His sister Fanny lived there now, with her husband and children. One of her little boys, named Henry, was a sickly, disabled child. Some writers believe that this small nephew gave Dickens the first idea for a character we all know. We cannot be sure. But it is a gentle thought.

On the fifth of October, eighteen forty-three, Dickens spoke at a great evening meeting at the Manchester Athenaeum, a club and institution set up to give working people a place for reading, lectures and learning. He shared the platform with two politicians, Richard Cobden and Benjamin Disraeli. And he spoke, with great feeling, about the importance of education for the poor, and about how ignorance was the root of so much misery and crime.

Somewhere in those days, the idea came to him. It seems that it came all at once, or nearly so. And when he returned to London, he began to write.

He wrote, as far as we can tell, from about the middle or end of October to the end of November, in roughly six weeks. And all the while, Martin Chuzzlewit was still going on. Each month he had to break off and write the next number of that, too.

He later described those weeks in a letter to his American friend Cornelius Felton. Writing about himself, playfully, as if he were someone else, he said that he had wept and laughed, and wept again, and had excited himself in a most extraordinary manner while he wrote. And he said that, thinking about it, he had walked about the black streets of London, fifteen and twenty miles, many a night when all sober folks had gone to bed.

Let us walk with him for a little while.

Imagine it is late at night, in November. The fog is coming up from the river, yellow and thick, and the gas lamps make blurred haloes in it. The streets are nearly empty. A watchman passes with his lantern. A cab rattles by. Somewhere a dog barks. And a slight man in a heavy coat walks quickly, on and on, through the City, past the shuttered counting houses where clerks have spent the day copying figures by candlelight, past the dark churches, along the river, out into the poorer streets beyond. His lips are moving, perhaps. He is talking to himself, or to the characters in his head. He is seeing a cold, bare office, and an old man bent over his money. He is seeing a door knocker change into a face.

And at home, at his desk at Devonshire Terrace, the pages pile up.

The story he wrote is one most of us know almost by heart. It begins, famously, with the words: Marley was dead, to begin with. Jacob Marley was the business partner of a hard, cold, grasping old man named Ebenezer Scrooge, who keeps a counting house in the City of London. Scrooge pays his clerk, Bob Cratchit, almost nothing, and lets him have only one tiny coal for his fire. When Scrooge's cheerful nephew Fred comes to wish him a merry Christmas, Scrooge answers with two of the most famous words in English: Bah, humbug.

Then, on Christmas Eve, alone in his gloomy rooms, Scrooge is visited by the ghost of Marley, wrapped in a long chain made of cash boxes, keys, padlocks and ledgers. Marley warns him that he will be visited by three spirits. And so they come. The Ghost of Christmas Past, who shows Scrooge his lonely boyhood, and the kind old employer, Mr. Fezziwig, who gave a wonderful Christmas dance, and the young woman Scrooge once loved, and lost through his love of money. The Ghost of Christmas Present, a great, jolly giant in a green robe, who shows him the Cratchit family's tiny, happy Christmas dinner, with their small, frail son, Tiny Tim. And the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come, silent and dark, who shows him a future in which he dies unloved, and Tiny Tim's chair stands empty by the fire.

And then, Scrooge wakes, on Christmas morning, and finds that the spirits have done it all in one night, and that there is still time to change. He laughs. He sends a great prize turkey to the Cratchits. He goes to his nephew's house for dinner. He raises Bob Cratchit's wages. And to Tiny Tim, who does not die, he becomes a second father.

There is one small, dark moment in the story that we should not pass by, because it holds so much of what Dickens had seen that year. Beneath his great green robe, the Ghost of Christmas Present shelters two children, a boy and a girl, thin, ragged and wolfish. The spirit tells Scrooge that the boy is Ignorance and the girl is Want, and that he should beware them both, but most of all the boy. Here, in one picture, are the hungry children of the mines and the factories, and the wild children of the ragged school in Field Lane.

But mostly, the book is full of warmth. Of fires and candles and holly, of puddings and roasting chestnuts, of games and dancing and laughter, of a family crowded together around a very small goose, making it seem a great one. It ends with Tiny Tim's simple blessing: God bless us, every one.

Let us rest here for a little while, in the warmth of that small, crowded room, with the fire glowing and the chestnuts spitting on the hearth. In the next chapter, the book goes out into the world, just a few days before Christmas, and something rather wonderful happens.
