## Chapter 7: Nickleby, Little Nell, and a House by Regent's Park

There is a quiet rhythm to the next few years of Charles Dickens's life, and we can let it carry us along. A new monthly number to write. A new child in the nursery. A new house. Long walks. Late nights at his desk. Friends to dinner. And then another number, and another, and another.

Before he had finished Oliver Twist, he had begun his next novel. In the winter of eighteen thirty-eight, he and his illustrator Hablot Browne, Phiz, travelled north together by coach through deep snow, into Yorkshire. They were going to see, with their own eyes, the cheap boarding schools of that county, which had a dreadful reputation. Advertisements in London newspapers offered to take unwanted boys, often illegitimate sons or stepchildren, and board and educate them far away, with no holidays, for a small yearly sum. Some of those schools were decent. Many were not. Boys were badly fed, beaten, and sometimes left to sicken and die.

Dickens visited one such school at Bowes, run by a man named William Shaw, who had been taken to court some years before after boys in his care lost their sight through neglect. He also saw, in the churchyard at Bowes, the gravestone of a boy who had died at the school. He went home with the material for a novel.

Nicholas Nickleby began appearing in April eighteen thirty-eight. Its young hero takes a job as an assistant teacher at a Yorkshire school called Dotheboys Hall, run by a one-eyed, brutal schoolmaster named Wackford Squeers. There Nicholas meets a poor, broken, gentle boy named Smike, and at last, in a scene that readers cheered, he turns on Squeers and thrashes him with his own cane, and escapes with Smike into the wide world. The book was a huge success. And it had a real effect. Within a few years, many of the Yorkshire schools had closed, as parents and guardians learned what went on in them.

Nickleby is also full of comedy and warmth. There is the travelling theatre company of Mr. Vincent Crummles, with its infant phenomenon. There are the kind twin merchants, the Cheeryble brothers. And there is Nicholas's mother, Mrs. Nickleby, who talks without stopping in long, wandering sentences that go everywhere and nowhere. Many people who knew the family thought that Mrs. Nickleby was, at least in part, a teasing portrait of Dickens's own mother, Elizabeth. It is said that she never recognised herself.

At the end of eighteen thirty-nine, with his family still growing, Dickens moved from Doughty Street to a larger house, at one Devonshire Terrace, near the York Gate of Regent's Park. It had a big garden, high walls and a fine view of the park. The family would live there for more than ten years. He had his study fitted out with bookshelves, and in the garden there were games of battledore and shuttlecock, and parties on summer evenings.

Next, he had an idea for a new kind of publication. It was to be a weekly magazine, written almost entirely by himself, called Master Humphrey's Clock. It was meant to hold short stories and sketches, introduced by a lonely old gentleman who keeps his papers in the case of a tall clock. The first numbers sold enormously, and then, when readers found it was not a novel, sales fell away. So Dickens did what he did best. He let one of the short stories grow, week by week, until it became a novel in its own right. It was called The Old Curiosity Shop.

Its heroine is a girl of thirteen named Nell Trent, who lives with her grandfather in a dim old shop full of curiosities in London. Her grandfather, out of love for her and a desperate wish to make her rich, has been secretly gambling, and has lost everything to a hideous, cruel dwarf called Daniel Quilp. And so the old man and the girl set off together, on foot, out of London, to wander across England, through fields and fairs and factory towns, looking for a quiet place to rest.

The weekly numbers of The Old Curiosity Shop were read by an enormous public, perhaps a hundred thousand people each week. And as the story went on, readers began to fear, more and more, that Little Nell was going to die. Letters poured in to Dickens, begging him to save her. He himself, while he was writing the last chapters, wrote to friends that he was utterly miserable, and that the story was breaking his heart. He was thinking, he said, of Mary Hogarth.

There is a famous story that, in New York, crowds gathered at the docks as a ship from England came in, carrying the latest number, and called out to the sailors to ask whether Little Nell was dead. It is a lovely story. But we should say that it comes from much later accounts, and nobody can be quite sure it happened in just that way. What we do know is that when Nell's death was published, early in eighteen forty-one, readers on both sides of the Atlantic wept over it. Some, later, laughed at it, and found it far too sentimental. But in its own time, it moved people very deeply.

After The Old Curiosity Shop came Barnaby Rudge, also in the weekly Clock, a historical novel set during the anti-Catholic riots that swept through London in seventeen eighty. It has a gentle, simple hero, Barnaby, and his talking pet raven, named Grip. The raven was based on Dickens's own pet ravens. He kept two, one after the other, at Devonshire Terrace, and the first of them really did talk. When it died, Dickens wrote a long, comic letter describing its last moments. Today, that first raven, stuffed and mounted, is kept in a library in Philadelphia. And some people believe that it was Dickens's Grip who gave the American poet Edgar Allan Poe the idea for his poem The Raven, though that can never quite be proved.

By the time Barnaby Rudge ended, late in eighteen forty-one, Charles Dickens was not yet thirty years old. He had written five novels and a book of sketches in a little over five years. He had four children. He was a household name across Britain, and across the ocean in America, too, where his books were read by millions, in cheap editions printed without his permission and without paying him a penny.

He was tired. He wanted a rest from writing in monthly and weekly parts, and he wanted to see the young republic across the sea, which he imagined as a land of freedom and hope. And so, in the last weeks of eighteen forty-one, he made a decision that would change him. He and Catherine would leave the four children in the care of friends and family, and sail together for America.
