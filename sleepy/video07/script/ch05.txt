## Chapter 5: A Reporter in the Gallery, and the Birth of Boz

In the early eighteen thirties, the reporters' gallery of the old House of Commons was a cramped, dark, uncomfortable place. The reporters sat squeezed together on a narrow bench high above the chamber, balancing their notebooks on their knees, straining to hear the speeches below by the light of candles and oil lamps. It was hot in summer, cold in winter, and always crowded. And it was here, at about the age of twenty, that Charles Dickens began work as a parliamentary reporter.

He worked first for a paper called the Mirror of Parliament, which his uncle helped to run, and which printed the debates word for word. Then, for a short while, for a paper called the True Sun. And then, in eighteen thirty-four, he joined the staff of the Morning Chronicle, one of the leading newspapers in London. He was very good. Other reporters said he was among the fastest and most accurate in the gallery.

These were stirring years in Parliament. The great Reform Act of eighteen thirty-two had just changed who could vote and how the country was represented. Laws were debated about factories, about the poor, about the abolition of slavery in the British colonies. Dickens heard it all. He was not always impressed. He came away from those years with a lasting distrust of long-winded politicians and of the slow, grinding machinery of government. Later, in Little Dorrit, he would invent a government department called the Circumlocution Office, whose whole purpose seemed to be to find out how not to do things.

The work also took him out of London. When there were elections, or important speeches in the country, the Chronicle sent him by coach to report them. He raced through the night along muddy roads, writing up his notes by the light of a lantern, sometimes on his knees in the swaying coach, and then sent the copy back to London as fast as a horse could carry it. Years later, in a speech, he remembered being upset in almost every kind of carriage then in use, and stuck in the mud, and soaked through, far from home in the middle of the night, and yet always getting the copy back in time. He loved it. The speed, the pressure, the sense of being part of something happening. He would bring that same restless energy to everything he did.

But he wanted more than reporting. He wanted to write.

And so, one evening late in eighteen thirty-three, he did something brave and private. He had written a little comic sketch about a London family, called A Dinner at Poplar Walk. He made a clean copy of it, and one evening at twilight, as he later put it, with fear and trembling, he dropped it into a dark letter-box in a dark office up a dark court off Fleet Street. It was the office of a magazine called the Monthly Magazine. He did not sign his name.

Some weeks later, in a bookshop on the Strand, he picked up the new issue of the magazine. And there, in print, was his sketch. In the preface to a later edition of Pickwick, he described how he walked down to Westminster Hall and turned into it for half an hour, because his eyes were so dimmed with joy and pride that they could not bear the street, and were not fit to be seen there.

Let us stay with that picture for a moment. A young man of twenty-one, standing alone in the great old hall at Westminster, with its high wooden roof, its stone floor worn smooth by centuries of feet, holding a magazine and quietly crying with happiness. It is a lovely thing to remember about him.

The Monthly Magazine did not pay him anything. But he kept writing for it. And after a few more sketches, in the summer of eighteen thirty-four, he began to sign them with a strange little pen name: Boz. It came, he said, from a family joke. He had a younger brother named Augustus, whom he had nicknamed Moses, after a character in Goldsmith's Vicar of Wakefield. A small child in the family, trying to say Moses through a cold in the nose, had turned it into Boses, and then Boz. And so Boz he became.

Over the next two years, Boz wrote dozens of sketches, for the Monthly Magazine and then for newspapers, including the Morning Chronicle and its sister paper, the Evening Chronicle. They were short, sharp, funny and sometimes sad portraits of London life. A pawnbroker's shop. A visit to Newgate prison. The crowds at Greenwich Fair. A ride on an omnibus. Gin shops, theatres, old houses, shabby-genteel people keeping up appearances. He walked the streets constantly, notebook in his head if not in his hand, watching everything.

The Evening Chronicle was edited by a Scottish journalist and music critic named George Hogarth. He liked young Dickens very much, and invited him to his home in Chelsea. And there, Charles met Hogarth's daughters.

The eldest was Catherine, who was nineteen or twenty. She was pretty, gentle, with heavy-lidded blue eyes and a calm, quiet manner. She was, in many ways, quite unlike Maria Beadnell. Charles courted her through eighteen thirty-five, and they became engaged. His letters to her from that time survive. They are affectionate, playful, sometimes a little bossy. He calls her by pet names, and he scolds her gently when she is cross with him for working too late, and he tells her, again and again, that he is working hard for their future.

In February eighteen thirty-six, on the day after his twenty-fourth birthday, his first book came out. It was called Sketches by Boz, Illustrative of Every-day Life and Every-day People. It was published in two volumes, with illustrations by George Cruikshank, who was then the most famous comic artist in England. It was well reviewed, and sold well. People began to wonder who this Boz might be.

And then, only a few days later, a young publisher named William Hall, of the firm Chapman and Hall, climbed the stairs to Dickens's rooms at Furnival's Inn in Holborn. He had a proposal. It was for a series of comic stories, to come out in monthly parts, to go with pictures of sporting gentlemen by a well-known artist. It would pay fourteen pounds a month.

Dickens said yes. And, in a strange little coincidence that he noticed himself, he recognised William Hall as the very man who had sold him the copy of the Monthly Magazine, more than two years before, that had held his first sketch in print.

On the second of April, eighteen thirty-six, at St. Luke's Church in Chelsea, Charles Dickens married Catherine Hogarth. He was twenty-four. She was twenty. They had a short honeymoon in a cottage in the village of Chalk, in Kent, not far from the Rochester he loved, and then they came back to his rooms in Furnival's Inn. Catherine's younger sister Mary, a lively, sweet-natured girl of sixteen, came to stay with them often.

The young couple did not have much. But Charles had a wife, a book in the shops, and a new monthly serial about to begin. Its first number had already gone on sale, two days before the wedding. It had a long title, The Posthumous Papers of the Pickwick Club. It started slowly. Nobody, not even its author, could have guessed what it was about to become.
