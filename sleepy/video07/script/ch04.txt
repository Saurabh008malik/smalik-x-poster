## Chapter 4: School Again, a Lawyer's Office, and a Girl Named Maria

In the spring or summer of eighteen twenty-five, Charles Dickens went back to school. It was called Wellington House Academy, and it stood on the Hampstead Road, not far from the family's lodgings in Camden Town. It was not a grand school. In later years he would describe its headmaster as a rather ignorant man, quick with a cane, and the teaching as patchy. But to a boy who had spent a year tying up pots of blacking, it must have felt like a door opening back into the world.

His schoolfellows remembered him as a bright, lively, well-dressed boy, full of fun. He wrote little stories and plays. He and his friends invented a kind of secret language, and amused themselves by pretending to be poor boys begging in the street, then breaking into laughter when kind ladies stopped to help them. Nobody at the school, it seems, had any idea where he had been the year before. He did not tell them.

He stayed there for about two years. Then, in the spring of eighteen twenty-seven, when he was fifteen, money ran short again. The family was briefly in trouble once more for unpaid rates. And so Charles left school for good, and went to work.

This time, the work was respectable. He became a junior clerk in a firm of solicitors, first briefly in one office, and then with a firm called Ellis and Blackmore, at Gray's Inn, one of the old Inns of Court, where lawyers have worked since the Middle Ages. He copied documents, carried messages, and kept the petty cash book. He was paid a modest weekly wage, a little over ten shillings at first, rising as time went on.

Let us imagine him there for a moment, in a dusty upper room looking out over old brick courtyards and plane trees, a slim young man with bright eyes and long brown hair, perched on a high stool. Imagine the scratch of quill pens, the stacks of papers tied in red tape, the clerks gossiping, the smell of old ink. One of his fellow clerks later remembered that, during the dull hours, the young men amused themselves by dropping cherry stones from the window onto the hats of people passing below. Whether that memory is quite exact, we cannot say. But it sounds very like him.

Those law offices gave him a store of material that he would draw on for the rest of his life. The clerks, the solicitors, the strange, slow, endless workings of the law. Many years later, in Bleak House, he would write about a lawsuit in the Court of Chancery that went on for so many years that the whole of a family's fortune was swallowed up in legal costs. The fog in the opening pages of that book, rolling through the streets around the courts, is some of the most famous writing in English.

But Charles did not mean to stay a lawyer's clerk. He had seen that his father, who after leaving the navy office had begun working as a newspaper reporter, could earn a better living with a pencil and a notebook. And so the young man set himself a task that many people found too hard to finish. He taught himself shorthand.

The system he used was called Gurney's, and it was fiendishly difficult. In David Copperfield, he later described how his hero struggled with it, with its dots that meant one thing in one place and something quite different in another, its curves like flies' legs, its strokes that changed meaning by their position. He practised for hours. He went to the gallery of the law courts and to debates, and took down what he heard, and then tried to read it back. Slowly, through sheer hard work, he became very quick and very accurate.

By about eighteen twenty-nine, when he was seventeen, he was good enough to set up as a freelance shorthand reporter at Doctors' Commons, a cluster of old courts near St. Paul's Cathedral that dealt with wills, marriages and church matters. He rented a box there, with a cousin, and waited for work. Some of it was dull. Some of it was very odd indeed. Again, he stored it all away.

On his eighteenth birthday, in February eighteen thirty, he applied for a reader's ticket at the British Museum, and received it. From then on, he spent many hours in the old reading room, reading history, Shakespeare, essays and anything else he could find. It was, in a way, the university he never had.

He also, at about this time, fell deeply in love.

Her name was Maria Beadnell. She was the daughter of a banker who lived in Lombard Street, in the City of London. She was small and pretty, with dark ringlets, and she was a year or two older than Charles. They met in about eighteen thirty, probably through a friend, and he was utterly taken with her. For three or four years, he courted her. He wrote her poems. He sent her letters. He waited outside her house, so the story goes, simply to see her window.

Her parents did not approve. A young shorthand reporter, the son of a man with a history of debts, was not their idea of a good match. Maria herself seems to have been fond of him, and then less fond, and then uncertain. She blew warm and cold. At one point she was sent away to finish her education in Paris, and after that, things between them cooled. At last, in the spring of eighteen thirty-three, Charles wrote to say that it was over, and returned her letters.

He was hurt, much more than he showed. Years later, in letters to friends, he admitted how deeply that first love had marked him, and how much of his youthful earnestness he had poured into it. Some readers think that the determination he showed in his work over the next few years, the ferocious energy of a young man who means to make his way, had a little of Maria Beadnell in it. He meant to show the world what he could do.

There is a quiet sequel to this story, which we may as well tell now, so that it does not trouble us later. More than twenty years afterwards, when Dickens was world-famous, a letter arrived from Maria, now a married woman with a family of her own. He was thrilled. He wrote back warmly, and arranged to meet her. And when he did, he found, a little sadly, that the woman he met was no longer the girl he remembered. Like many of us, he had been in love with a memory. He drew a gently comic portrait of her, years later, as Flora Finching in Little Dorrit, a kind-hearted, rambling woman whom the hero had adored in his youth.

But all of that is far ahead. For now, it is the early eighteen thirties. Charles Dickens is twenty or twenty-one years old, quick, hard-working and a little heartbroken. He can write shorthand faster than almost anyone in London. And he is about to step into the reporters' gallery of the House of Commons, and into the world of newspapers, where his real career was about to begin.
