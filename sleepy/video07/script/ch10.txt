## Chapter 10: Five Shillings, Red and Gold, and a Christmas That Changed

Dickens had decided to publish A Christmas Carol in an unusual way. Instead of letting Chapman and Hall pay him a fee or a share, he would pay for the printing himself, and keep the profits himself. He wanted it to be beautiful. And so it was.

It was a small book, bound in reddish-brown cloth, with gilt lettering on the cover and gold edges on the pages. The title page was printed in two colours, and the endpapers were tinted. Inside were eight illustrations by the artist John Leech, a friend of Dickens and one of the best comic illustrators of the day. Four of them were small black-and-white wood engravings set into the text. And four were full-page etchings, each one coloured by hand. There was Mr. Fezziwig's ball, with the fiddler playing and the couples dancing. There was Marley's ghost, rising in Scrooge's room. There was the great, jolly Ghost of Christmas Present, sitting among his heaps of food. And there was Scrooge, at the end, sharing a bowl of steaming punch with Bob Cratchit.

Dickens took great trouble over every detail. He argued with the printers over the colours. Some early copies had to be redone. All of this made the book very expensive to produce. And yet he insisted that it should be sold cheaply enough for ordinary people to buy it. The price was set at five shillings.

A Christmas Carol was published on the nineteenth of December, eighteen forty-three, six days before Christmas. The whole first printing of six thousand copies had sold out by Christmas Eve.

Let us pause, and picture that week. Booksellers' windows in the Strand and Fleet Street with the little red books stacked inside, under the gas light. People hurrying home through the cold, with a parcel under one arm. A father reading aloud by the fire on Christmas Eve, the children sitting close, and a mother pausing in her sewing at the part where Tiny Tim's chair stands empty. We cannot know, of course, which homes those six thousand copies went to. But we can be fairly sure that many of them were read exactly like that, aloud, by the fire, in a family circle.

The reviews were warm. Even writers who did not usually admire Dickens were moved. The novelist William Makepeace Thackeray, who was in some ways a rival, wrote that the book seemed to him a national benefit, and to every man or woman who read it, a personal kindness. The poet Thomas Hood praised it. Ordinary readers wrote to Dickens to tell him how it had moved them, and how it had made them want to be kinder.

There were reprints, one after another. The book was printed again before the year was out, and again and again in the year that followed.

And yet, here is a strange little twist that Dickens himself would have found comic, if it had happened to someone else. Because the book was so costly to make, and because he had insisted on selling it so cheaply, the profits were far smaller than he had hoped. He had imagined it might bring him a thousand pounds. The first accounts showed only a little over two hundred. Even after a year of sales, the total came to something like seven hundred and thirty or forty pounds. Dickens was bitterly disappointed. He wrote to Forster that it was the most wonderful thing, that such a great success should occasion him such intolerable anxiety and disappointment.

To make things worse, almost at once a cheap pirate version appeared in a magazine, lightly changed. Dickens took the publishers to court, and won. But they declared themselves bankrupt, and he ended up paying a large share of the legal costs himself.

So A Christmas Carol did not, at first, solve his money troubles. But it did something much greater. It changed Christmas.

That might sound too grand a thing to say. Let us be careful. Dickens did not invent Christmas, of course, or the Christmas tree, or the Christmas card. Many of the old Christmas customs had been fading in the hurry of the new industrial towns, where factory hands worked long hours and families had moved far from their villages. But a revival was already underway in those years. The first commercial Christmas card was printed in London that very same year, eighteen forty-three, for Dickens's acquaintance Henry Cole. And a few years later, a picture of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert with their children around a decorated tree helped make the Christmas tree fashionable in English homes.

What Dickens did was to give that revival a heart, and a story. He took the old idea of Christmas as a time for feasting and merriment, and joined it to the idea of Christmas as a time for kindness, charity and family, for remembering the poor and the lonely, and for a change of heart. In his book, Christmas is not just a holiday. It is a moral season. When Scrooge's nephew speaks of Christmas, he calls it a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time, when men and women seem to open their shut-up hearts freely, and to think of people below them as if they really were fellow passengers to the grave.

That is the idea that spread. And it spread quickly. People began to speak of a Dickens Christmas. Phrases from the book passed into everyday speech. The name Scrooge became a word for any miser. Charities found that giving went up at Christmastime. There is even a story, from much later, of a factory owner in America who, after hearing the Carol read aloud, closed his factory on Christmas Day, and sent each of his workers a turkey. That story is told in more than one form, and we cannot vouch for it. But it is exactly the kind of thing the book was meant to do.

Dickens went on to write a Christmas book every year for the next few years: The Chimes, The Cricket on the Hearth, The Battle of Life and The Haunted Man. They were popular in their day. But none of them ever came close to the first.

And A Christmas Carol has never been out of print. It has been read, staged, filmed, set to music, and turned into cartoons and puppet shows, more times than anyone can count. Each December, all around the world, someone opens it again, and reads those first quiet words: Marley was dead, to begin with.

Before we leave that first Christmas of eighteen forty-three behind, let us remember one more line from the end of the book. Scrooge, waking on Christmas morning, makes a promise. He says that he will honour Christmas in his heart, and try to keep it all the year. And the narrator tells us that he did. It was always said of him, the book says, that he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge.

It is a good place to rest. In the next chapter, we follow Dickens into the busy, crowded middle years of his life: to Italy and Switzerland, to a newspaper and a magazine, to a home for women who needed a fresh start, and to the book he loved most of all.
