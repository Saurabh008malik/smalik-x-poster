## Chapter 11: The Busy Middle Years: Italy, Copperfield, and Household Words

In the summer of eighteen forty-four, partly to save money and partly from sheer restlessness, Dickens let the house at Devonshire Terrace, packed the whole family into a great travelling carriage, and set off for Italy. They crossed France slowly, by road and river, and settled for the best part of a year in Genoa, on the Mediterranean coast. For a time they lived in a crumbling old villa which Dickens called the pink jail, and then in a vast palace, the Palazzo Peschiere, with frescoed ceilings, terraced gardens, orange trees and fountains, and a view across the city to the sea.

There, in the autumn, he wrote his second Christmas book, The Chimes. He was kept awake, at first, by the clanging of the church bells of Genoa, and the bells found their way into the story. He was so eager to read it to his friends that he travelled all the way back to London in the late autumn, across the Alps in winter, to read it aloud in John Forster's rooms. Some of his listeners wept. It was a sign of something that would matter very much later on. Dickens loved reading his work aloud, and he was very, very good at it.

The family travelled on through Italy in the new year, to Rome, Naples and Florence, and came home in the summer of eighteen forty-five. The next few years were among the fullest of his life. In eighteen forty-six he became the first editor of a new daily newspaper, the Daily News, though he gave it up after only a few weeks, finding that he did not have the patience for it. He went abroad again, to Lausanne in Switzerland and then to Paris, and wrote another long novel, Dombey and Son, about a proud, cold businessman and the daughter he neglects.

He also began some real and lasting work for others. With his friend Angela Burdett-Coutts, the heiress who had helped the ragged schools, he set up a home in Shepherd's Bush, in west London, called Urania Cottage. It was for young women who had been in prison, or living on the streets, and who wanted a fresh start. Dickens ran it, in practice, for about ten years. He chose the women, hired the staff, wrote the rules, worried over the accounts, and chose bright, cheerful fabrics for the dresses, because he thought the women had seen enough grey. Many of them, with help, sailed for new lives in Australia, Canada or South Africa. It is one of the quieter, kinder things in his life, and it was almost unknown in his own time.

And then, from the spring of eighteen forty-nine, came the book that he would later call his favourite child. It was David Copperfield.

David Copperfield is the life story of a young man, told by himself. He is born in a small house in Suffolk, after his father has died. His gentle mother remarries a cold, hard man named Mr. Murdstone. And when his mother dies, David is sent to London, still a child, to work in a warehouse by the river, washing and labelling bottles. He lodges with the Micawber family, whose father is kind, hopeful, grandly spoken, and always in debt, and who is soon taken to a debtors' prison. You will recognise all of this. Dickens took the private account of the blacking warehouse, the one he had written for Forster and shown to nobody else, and gave it, almost word for word in places, to his young hero.

Mr. Micawber, many readers believe, owes a good deal to Dickens's own father, John Dickens, with his long words and his endless hopes that something will turn up. Nobody knows for certain what John Dickens himself made of it.

David runs away from the warehouse, and walks all the way to Dover to find his only relative, his great-aunt Betsey Trotwood, a fierce, kind, eccentric old lady who takes him in. From there the story follows him through school and into the law, through his love for the pretty, childish Dora, and at last to a quiet, deep love with the wise and patient Agnes. Along the way we meet Uriah Heep, the creeping, hand-rubbing clerk who is always so very humble, and the Peggotty family in their upturned boat on the beach at Yarmouth, and the great storm at sea, which many readers think is one of the finest pieces of writing Dickens ever did.

In eighteen fifty, as David Copperfield was finishing, Dickens started a new weekly magazine of his own, called Household Words. He wrote for it, edited it, and chose every piece that went in. It was meant for ordinary families, at a price of twopence, and it mixed stories and poems with articles about real life: about sanitation and housing, about education, about the dangers of factory work and the hardship of the poor. He kept it going for nine years, and then replaced it with another weekly, All the Year Round, which ran for the rest of his life. Many of his later novels came out first in its pages.

In the early eighteen fifties, the family moved to a large house in Tavistock Square, called Tavistock House. And there he wrote three of his greatest novels, one after the other. Bleak House, with its fog, its endless lawsuit, and its little crossing sweeper, Jo, who is always being told to move on. Hard Times, set in a grim northern mill town called Coketown, where children are taught only facts. And Little Dorrit, which returns, at last, to the Marshalsea prison of his own childhood.

These were also years of sorrow. In the spring of eighteen fifty-one, his father, John Dickens, died after an operation. Only two weeks later, his baby daughter, Dora, who was just eight months old, died suddenly. Dickens heard the news while he was making a speech at a charity dinner, and was not told until it was over. By the eighteen fifties, he and Catherine had had ten children in all, seven sons and three daughters, and nine of them lived to grow up. He was a loving, playful, demanding father, who made up nicknames for every child, organised the Christmas games and the theatricals, and inspected their rooms for tidiness every morning.

He was also, by now, very tired, and very restless. The man who had walked the streets of London all night while writing the Carol now walked further and faster than ever. He could not sit still. His marriage, too, had grown unhappy. Catherine, after so many children, was often unwell, and he had begun to grow impatient with her, in ways that are hard to read about now.

Let us leave him there for the moment, on a winter evening in Tavistock House, with the children's voices on the stairs and the fire burning in the study, writing quickly in his clear, slanting hand, with a pot of blue ink beside him. In the next chapter, the last, the shadows of his later life come in, and so does a great deal of light. And at the end of it, we will say goodnight.
