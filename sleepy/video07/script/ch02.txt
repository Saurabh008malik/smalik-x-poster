## Chapter 2: Golden Years by the Medway

In the spring of eighteen hundred and seventeen, when Charles was five years old, the Dickens family arrived in Chatham. It was a busy, noisy, salty place. The Royal Dockyard stretched along the River Medway, full of the sound of hammers and saws, the smell of tar and rope, and the sight of great wooden warships rising on the slipways. Soldiers drilled on the lines above the town. And just beside Chatham, so close that the two places ran into one another, stood the old city of Rochester, with its narrow high street, its Norman castle keep standing broken against the sky, and its quiet grey cathedral.

The family took a house in Ordnance Terrace, on the high ground above the town, with a view over fields and the river. Later, when money grew tighter, they moved to a smaller house in St. Mary's Place, down nearer the dockyard. The household grew. There were new babies, a nursemaid, and a crowd of visitors and neighbours. John Dickens loved to entertain, and the house was often full of talk and laughter and singing.

Charles was happy here. In later life he would return to these streets again and again in his imagination. Rochester became Cloisterham in his last, unfinished novel. The coaching inns, the castle, the marshes along the river, the convict hulks moored out in the mud, would all find their way into his books. When he wrote the opening pages of Great Expectations, more than forty years later, with a small boy alone in a churchyard on the marshes at dusk, he was drawing on the flat grey country he had known as a child.

Let us picture, gently, a few of the things he did in those years. He went to school, first at a small dame school, and then at a school run by a young Baptist minister named William Giles, who saw that the boy was clever and took trouble over him. He went to the theatre in Rochester, a small and rather shabby playhouse, and watched the actors with complete delight. He sang comic songs, standing on a table at the local inn, while his father's friends applauded. His sister Fanny was musical, and the two of them would perform together. He was, in a small way, already a performer, and he would remain one all his life.

But the most important thing that happened to Charles in Chatham happened upstairs, in a small room at the top of the house. His father owned a little collection of books, and they were kept in that room, where nobody much disturbed them. Charles found them. And he read them, over and over, alone.

He later listed them, with great affection. There was Robinson Crusoe. There was The Arabian Nights, with its genies and magic lamps and flying carpets. There was Don Quixote, with its foolish, noble knight. There were the comic novels of the eighteenth century, by Henry Fielding and Tobias Smollett, full of travelling heroes, wayside inns and strange characters, and Oliver Goldsmith's gentle story The Vicar of Wakefield. He read the essays of the Tatler and the Spectator. He read them on summer evenings, while other boys played outside, and he imagined himself into them. He would play at being his favourite characters for weeks at a time. Years later, through the voice of David Copperfield, he wrote that these books had kept alive his fancy and his hope of something beyond that place and time. It is one of the most tender passages he ever wrote, and many readers believe it describes his own boyhood almost exactly.

There is one more story from these years, and it is perhaps the most famous of all. It comes from Dickens himself, told long afterwards, so we should hold it lightly, as a memory rather than a record. On the road from Rochester towards Gravesend, on a rise called Gad's Hill, there stood a handsome red-brick house with a bell turret on its roof. On walks with his father, the small boy would stop and look at it with longing. And his father, so the story goes, told him that if he worked hard and persevered, he might one day come to live in a house just like that.

We will come back to Gad's Hill Place much later in our story. For now, let us simply leave the picture there: a man and a small boy standing in the road on a fine day, looking at a house, and the boy believing every word.

Those Chatham years were not perfect, of course. His father's debts were already growing. The family's move from the comfortable house on the hill to the smaller house in the town was one sign of it. But to a child, these were the years of the sun. There were walks in the country, boating on the river, Christmas parties and Twelfth Night cakes, magic lanterns and pantomimes. Some readers have wondered whether his lifelong love of a warm, crowded, merry Christmas began here, in a house full of children and music. We cannot know that for certain. But it is a pleasant thought to keep.

Then, in eighteen twenty-two, when Charles was ten, his father was recalled to London. The family packed up its things. Charles stayed behind in Chatham for a short while, to finish his term at Mr. Giles's school, and then he followed them alone. He travelled up to London by stagecoach, packed in, as he remembered it, like game being sent to market, with damp straw around him. He later wrote that it rained hard all the way, and that he thought life was sloppier than he had expected to find it.

The coach rolled into a city that was very different from the river town he loved. London, in the eighteen twenties, was already enormous, and growing every year. It was a place of fog and smoke, of crowded courts and alleys, of rich squares and terrible slums only a few streets apart. The family settled in a small, cramped house in Bayham Street, in Camden Town, which was then a rather shabby new suburb on the northern edge of the city.

And there, for a time, nothing much happened to Charles at all. He was not sent back to school. There was no money for it. His sister Fanny, who had real musical talent, won a place at the new Royal Academy of Music, and that was a great thing for the family, but it cost money too. Charles, meanwhile, ran errands, cleaned boots, looked after his younger brothers and sisters, and wandered the streets. He stared at the great city from a distance, from the hills of Camden Town, and he wondered what would become of him.

Let us leave him there for now, a bright, lonely boy of ten or eleven, with his head full of Robinson Crusoe and the Arabian Nights, standing on a hill in the London smoke. What was about to happen to him would be the hardest thing in his whole life. It would also, in a strange way, give him the deepest root of everything he would later write.
