## Chapter 12: Gad's Hill, the Last Readings, and a Gentle Goodnight

In the spring of eighteen fifty-six, a remarkable thing happened. The handsome red-brick house on Gad's Hill, on the road between Rochester and Gravesend, the house that a small boy had once gazed at on walks with his father, came up for sale. Charles Dickens, now forty-four and famous all over the world, bought it. He wrote to a friend that he used to look at it as a wonderful mansion when he was a very odd little child, with the first faint shadows of all his books in his head. And he told the story, in one of his later essays, of a queer small boy who once stood in the road and looked at that house, and was told that if he worked hard he might one day live there. He had worked hard. And now he did.

At first it was a summer house. Then, from the early eighteen sixties, it became his only home. He loved it. He planted trees, added rooms, built a tunnel under the road to a little garden on the other side, and set up there a small wooden chalet, sent to him as a gift by a friend, a Swiss chalet in pieces packed in boxes. In the upper room of that chalet, among the leaves, with mirrors on the walls reflecting the trees and the river and the distant sails on the Thames, he did much of his writing in his last years.

But we must now walk, gently and honestly, through the darkest part of his later life.

In eighteen fifty-seven, Dickens put on a play, a drama of Arctic exploration called The Frozen Deep, written with his friend Wilkie Collins. When it moved to a large theatre in Manchester, professional actresses were hired. Among them was a young actress named Ellen Ternan. She was eighteen. Dickens was forty-five. He fell in love with her.

The next year, eighteen fifty-eight, Dickens separated from Catherine, after twenty-two years of marriage and ten children. He behaved badly. He had a partition built in their bedroom. He allowed rumours to spread about the reasons for the separation, and then, extraordinarily, he published a statement about his private life on the front page of Household Words, and allowed a still harsher letter to appear in newspapers. He wrote of Catherine in cruel and unfair terms. She went to live in a house in London, with an allowance, and their eldest son, Charley, went with her. The other children stayed with their father. Catherine's sister Georgina Hogarth, who had lived with the family for many years and helped to raise the children, stayed too, and ran his household for the rest of his life.

What exactly the relationship between Dickens and Ellen Ternan was, over the next twelve years, has been argued about ever since. He took great care to keep it secret, and most of their letters were destroyed. Most modern biographers believe that they were very close, and that he supported her and her family, and visited her often in the houses he arranged for her. Beyond that, we cannot be certain. Catherine lived on for nine years after his death. Before she died, she gave the letters he had written to her, in the happy years of their marriage, to their daughter Kate, and asked that they be published, so that the world would know he had once loved her. They were.

In the same year as the separation, Dickens began something new. He started giving public readings from his own books, for money. He had read for charity for some years. Now he began a long series of paid tours, first in London and then all across Britain and Ireland. He stood alone at a reading desk, with a dark screen behind him and gas lights above, and he became each character in turn. He did not simply read. He acted. Audiences were spellbound. They laughed and wept and cheered. And the piece he read more often than any other, at nearly every series, was A Christmas Carol.

Amid all this, the great books kept coming. A Tale of Two Cities, in eighteen fifty-nine, set in London and Paris during the French Revolution, with its famous opening: It was the best of times, it was the worst of times. Then, in eighteen sixty and sixty-one, Great Expectations, with Pip, the orphan boy on the Kentish marshes, the convict Magwitch, the strange Miss Havisham in her yellowed wedding dress among the stopped clocks, and the cold, beautiful Estella. Many readers think it is his finest novel. And then Our Mutual Friend, a dark, rich book about the River Thames and the dust heaps of London.

On the ninth of June, eighteen sixty-five, Dickens was travelling back from France by train, with Ellen Ternan and her mother. Near the village of Staplehurst, in Kent, workmen repairing a bridge had removed a section of the rails, misreading the timetable. The train came off the line as it crossed. Several carriages fell into the riverbed below, and ten people were killed. Dickens's own carriage was left hanging over the edge. He climbed out, helped Ellen and her mother to safety, and then went back down into the wreck, again and again, with his hat full of water and a flask of brandy, to help the injured and the dying. Then he climbed back into his own hanging carriage to rescue the manuscript of the next number of Our Mutual Friend. He was badly shaken. For the rest of his life he was nervous on trains.

His health was beginning to fail, but he would not rest. In the late autumn of eighteen sixty-seven he crossed the Atlantic again, twenty-five years after his first visit, for a great reading tour of America. This time there was no quarrel. Crowds queued all night in the snow for tickets. He gave more than seventy readings, from Boston to Washington, earning a great deal of money. But he was often ill, with a heavy cold that would not leave him, and lame in one foot, and sleepless. At a dinner in New York before he sailed home, he spoke generously of the changes he had seen in the country, and promised that what he had once written about America would be balanced by what he said that night.

Back home, against the advice of his doctors, he began a farewell reading tour of Britain. He added to it a terrible, violent new piece, the murder of Nancy from Oliver Twist, which he performed with such fury that his pulse raced and people in the audience fainted. In the spring of eighteen sixty-nine, after a mild stroke, his doctors stopped the tour. He was allowed a final short series in London, early in eighteen seventy. And on the fifteenth of March, eighteen seventy, at St. James's Hall in Piccadilly, he gave the very last reading of his life. He read A Christmas Carol, and the trial scene from Pickwick. At the end, with tears on his face, he told the audience that from these garish lights he would vanish now for evermore, and he bid them a heartfelt, grateful, respectful and affectionate farewell.

That spring, he was writing a new mystery novel, set in a cathedral city very like the Rochester of his childhood. It was called The Mystery of Edwin Drood. He had finished about half of it.

On the eighth of June, eighteen seventy, a warm summer day, he worked in the chalet at Gad's Hill, among the trees. In the evening, at dinner, he was taken ill. Georgina tried to help him lie down on the floor. He said, quietly, on the ground. Those were among the last words anyone heard him say. He never woke again. On the next evening, the ninth of June, five years to the day after the accident at Staplehurst, Charles Dickens died. He was fifty-eight years old.

He had asked to be buried simply and privately, without fuss. But the nation wanted him in Westminster Abbey. And so, early on the morning of the fourteenth of June, with only a handful of family and friends present, he was laid to rest in Poets' Corner, near the memorials of Shakespeare and Chaucer. The grave was left open for a few days afterwards, and thousands upon thousands of people came, from every walk of life, to file past it. Many of them threw flowers. By the end, it is said, the grave was filled to the brim.

There is a story, told later, that a little girl in London, when she heard the news that Dickens had died, asked whether Father Christmas would die too. Like many of the stories we have heard tonight, we cannot be sure that it happened just like that. But it tells us something true about what he had come to mean.

Let us end the way we began, quietly. Think of the small boy in Chatham, reading Robinson Crusoe in an upstairs room. Think of the child tying up pots of blacking by the cold river, and the young reporter crying with joy in Westminster Hall. Think of the man walking through the foggy midnight streets, fifteen and twenty miles, with Scrooge and Tiny Tim and the three spirits turning in his head. And think of that little red book, going out into the world just before Christmas, and finding its way into home after home, year after year, until today.

The fire is low now. The streets outside are quiet. The fog is settling softly over the river, and the gas lamps have long since gone out. You have listened well, and there is nothing more you need to do tonight.

As Tiny Tim said, and as Charles Dickens has been saying to his readers ever since: God bless us, every one.

Goodnight. Sleep well.
