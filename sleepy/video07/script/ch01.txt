## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Winter Birth in Portsmouth

Hello, and welcome. Tonight we are going to spend a long, quiet evening with one of the most loved storytellers who ever lived. His name was Charles Dickens. He gave the world Oliver Twist and David Copperfield, Mr. Pickwick and Little Dorrit, Pip and Estella and Miss Havisham. And, in one cold autumn when he was thirty-one years old, he wrote a small book about a lonely old man, three spirits, and a single Christmas night. He called it A Christmas Carol. It has never been out of print since.

So let us settle in. You might like to pull the blanket a little higher, let your shoulders drop, and breathe out slowly. There is no need to remember any of the dates I mention. There is no test at the end. If you drift away halfway through a chapter, that is exactly as it should be. The story will keep walking on beside you, at a gentle pace, through the streets of a city that has long since changed.

Here is the path we will follow. We will begin with a baby born in a naval town on the south coast of England. We will spend a few golden childhood years with him beside a river in Kent, and then follow him to London, where his family's money ran out and a hard season of work changed him for ever. We will watch him become a reporter, then a sketch writer, then, almost overnight, the most famous young author in the country. We will cross the Atlantic with him, and come home again. And then, near the middle of our journey, we will slow right down, and stay with him through the six weeks in which he wrote A Christmas Carol, walking the dark streets of London at night with the story turning in his head. After that we will follow him through the busy middle of his life, its great books and its real shadows, the public readings that filled halls on both sides of the ocean, and at last a summer evening in a house he had loved since he was a boy.

Wherever the record is uncertain, I will tell you so. Some of the best-known stories about Dickens come from his own memory, written down years later, or from friends who heard them from him. They may be true in their outline but softened or sharpened in the telling. And where his life was not admirable, I will not pretend that it was. He was a generous man, and a man who could be hard and unfair to the people closest to him. Both things are part of the story.

Now, let us go back to the beginning.

The year is eighteen hundred and twelve. Britain has been at war with Napoleon's France, on and off, for most of twenty years. Along the south coast, the great naval town of Portsmouth is crowded with ships, sailors, dockyard workers, clerks and chandlers. And in a small, neat brick house in a new district called Landport, at what was then Mile End Terrace, a young couple are waiting for their second child.

The father is John Dickens. He works as a clerk in the Navy Pay Office, which means he helps to count out and record the wages of the men who build and supply the fleet. He is cheerful, sociable and fond of long, elaborate sentences. He likes good company, good dinners and a comfortable home. He is not, sadly, very good at keeping his spending within his income. That small weakness will shape much of his son's life.

The mother is Elizabeth Dickens, born Elizabeth Barrow. She is lively and quick, with a gift for noticing the comic side of people, and for imitating them. Many people who knew the family later felt that her son inherited that sharp, amused eye from her.

Their first child, a daughter named Frances, whom everyone called Fanny, was already a baby of about eighteen months. And on the seventh of February, eighteen hundred and twelve, a Friday, their son was born. They named him Charles John Huffam Dickens. The Huffam came from his godfather, a man connected with the fitting out of ships in London.

We do not know what the weather was like that February morning. We can imagine, if we like, a grey winter light over the harbour, the creak of masts, the cry of gulls, and the distant sound of the dockyard bell. But that is only imagining. What we do know is that the house still stands. Today it is a small museum, and visitors can stand in the room where, by family tradition, he was born.

Charles was not a Portsmouth child for long. John Dickens's work moved him from place to place, and the family moved with it. When Charles was only a few months old they moved to a smaller house nearby, and then, when he was about three, the family went up to London for a time. Then, when he was about five years old, John Dickens was posted to the naval dockyard at Chatham, in Kent, on the River Medway. And it was there, in Chatham and the old cathedral city of Rochester beside it, that Charles Dickens spent the happiest years of his childhood.

Before we follow them to Kent, let us pause for a moment over the small boy himself. Those who remembered him described a slight, pale child, not very strong, who was sometimes troubled by spasms of pain in his side that kept him from rough games. While other boys ran and fought in the fields, he often watched, or read, or listened. He noticed everything. The way a man tilted his hat. The way a woman said a particular word. The smell of a shop, the colour of a door, the sound of rain on a particular kind of roof. Years later, he would write that he had been a very observant child, and that the faces and places of those early years stayed with him with startling clearness.

That gift of noticing is the thread we will follow through the whole of his life. It is what made his books feel crowded and alive, as if every page were a busy street. And it began, as such things often do, with a quiet child sitting a little apart, watching the world go by.

There is one more thing to set beside the cradle before we move on. The England Charles was born into was changing fast. Steam engines were beginning to drive mills and pumps. Towns were swelling as people left the countryside to look for work. Roads were being improved, and in his lifetime the railways would arrive and cover the country with iron lines. London would grow into the largest city the world had ever seen. Charles Dickens would become, more than any other writer, the voice of that crowded, smoky, restless new world, with its great wealth and its terrible poverty sitting side by side.

But all of that lies far ahead. For now, he is a baby in a small brick house near the sea, with an elder sister, a cheerful, careless father and a quick-witted mother. Let us leave the family there for a little while, settling into their new life, and in the next chapter we will travel with them along the coast road into Kent, to a town of ships and soldiers, an old castle, a cathedral, and a little boy's secret library in an upstairs room.
