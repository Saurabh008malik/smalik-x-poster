## Chapter 8: A House Divided

To understand what happened next, we need to take a few quiet minutes to look at the great problem of Lincoln's time.

When the United States was founded, slavery was legal in many of the states. Over the years, the Northern states had ended it, one by one, while in the Southern states it had grown, and become the foundation of a whole way of life, built on the forced labour of millions of enslaved men, women and children. By the eighteen fifties, there were about four million enslaved people in the South. The question that troubled the country more and more was what would happen in the new lands of the West, as they filled with settlers and became new states. Would slavery be allowed to spread into them, or not?

For a long time, there had been an agreement, called the Missouri Compromise, that slavery would not be allowed in the northern part of the western lands. But in eighteen hundred and fifty-four, Congress passed a new law, called the Kansas-Nebraska Act. It was the work of Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois, the same short, energetic man who had once called on Mary Todd. The new law said that the settlers in the territories of Kansas and Nebraska should decide for themselves whether to allow slavery. Douglas called this popular sovereignty. But it meant that the old line was gone, and slavery might spread into lands where it had been forbidden for over thirty years.

Lincoln was deeply troubled. He later said that this law roused him as he had never been roused before. He began to study the history of slavery and the founding of the nation, reading in the state library, filling notebooks, thinking hard. And in October of eighteen hundred and fifty-four, in the town of Peoria, he gave a long speech, more than three hours, answering Douglas. He said that he hated the spread of slavery because of the monstrous injustice of slavery itself. He said that no man is good enough to govern another man without that other's consent. It was the most powerful thing he had ever said, and people across Illinois began to pay attention.

Over the next two years, the old Whig party broke apart, and a new party was formed by those who wanted to stop the spread of slavery. It was called the Republican Party. In eighteen hundred and fifty-six, Lincoln joined it, and soon became one of its leaders in Illinois.

We should also be honest about Lincoln's views at this time, because they were not the views we hold today. He believed slavery was wrong, and that it must not spread. He believed that the words of the Declaration of Independence, that all men are created equal, included Black Americans too, in their right to life, liberty and the fruits of their own work. But he did not, in the eighteen fifties, call for ending slavery immediately in the states where it already existed, because he believed the Constitution did not allow it. And like most white Americans of his time, he did not then believe in full social and political equality between Black and white Americans. He said so, more than once, in his speeches. For years he also favoured a plan to encourage freed people to settle in other countries, a plan that Black leaders strongly opposed. His views would grow and change during the war years, as we will see. But it is right to remember where he started.

In June of eighteen hundred and fifty-eight, the Republicans of Illinois chose Lincoln as their candidate for the United States Senate, against Stephen Douglas himself. And on the evening he was chosen, in the hall of the state house in Springfield, Lincoln gave a speech that began with words borrowed from the Bible. A house divided against itself cannot stand, he said. He believed that the government could not endure permanently half slave and half free. He did not expect the Union to be dissolved, or the house to fall, he said, but he did expect it would cease to be divided. It would become all one thing, or all the other. Some of his friends thought the speech was too bold. But he would not change it.

That summer and autumn, Lincoln and Douglas travelled all over Illinois, speaking to crowds. And they agreed to hold seven great debates, in seven towns, one in each of the state's congressional districts. Their names were Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy, and Alton.

Let us imagine one of those days. It is late summer. The little town is full to bursting. Since early morning, people have been pouring in from all the farms for miles around, in wagons and buggies and on horseback, whole families with picnic baskets. There are brass bands playing, and flags, and banners, and cannons fired in salute. Clouds of dust rise from the roads. In the town square, or in a field nearby, a wooden platform has been built, and thousands of people crowd around it, standing in the sun for hours.

And there on the platform are the two men. They make a strange pair. Douglas is very short, only about five feet four inches, but with a big chest and a deep, booming voice. People call him the Little Giant. He is a famous senator, rich and well-dressed, and he travels in a private railway car. Lincoln is about a foot taller, thin and bony, in a plain black suit that does not fit him very well, with a high, rather thin voice that carries surprisingly far across a crowd. At first, people smile at the contrast. But as Lincoln talks, slowly and clearly, explaining his reasons step by step, the crowd goes quiet and listens.

Each debate lasted three hours. One man spoke for an hour, the other answered for an hour and a half, and then the first replied for half an hour. Newspapers printed every word, and people read them all across the country. Douglas said that the people of each territory should decide about slavery for themselves, and that he did not care whether it was voted up or voted down. Lincoln said that the real question was whether slavery was right or wrong. He said that Douglas did not care, but that the Republicans did think it was wrong, and wanted to treat it as a wrong, and keep it from growing, so that in time it would come to an end.

In those days, senators were not chosen directly by the people, but by the state legislature. When the votes were counted in November, Lincoln's Republicans had won slightly more of the popular vote across the state, but the Democrats kept more seats in the legislature. And in January of eighteen hundred and fifty-nine, the legislature chose Douglas. Lincoln had lost.

He was disappointed, of course. When a friend asked him how he felt, he said, according to a story told later, that he felt like the boy who had stubbed his toe: it hurt too much to laugh, and he was too old to cry. But he also wrote to a friend that he was glad he had made the race. It had given him a hearing on the great question of the age, which he could have had in no other way. And though he might now sink out of view and be forgotten, he believed he had made some marks which would tell for the cause of civil liberty long after he was gone.

He did not sink out of view. The debates had made his name known across the whole country. And people were beginning to ask whether this tall, plain prairie lawyer might be the man for something even larger.

Let us leave him now, at the end of that long campaign, on a cold November night in Springfield. He is walking home from the telegraph office in the dark, along a path that is slippery with rain. As the story was told afterwards, one foot slipped out from under him, and he caught himself, and he thought: it is a slip, and not a fall.
