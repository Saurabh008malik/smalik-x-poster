## Chapter 9: The Road to the White House

In the winter of eighteen hundred and sixty, Lincoln was invited to give a speech in New York City. It was a great chance. New York was the biggest city in the country, and the most important Republicans of the East would be there, watching to see what this prairie lawyer from Illinois was really like.

He prepared the speech with great care, spending weeks in the state library in Springfield, studying the votes and words of the men who had written the Constitution, to show what they had thought about slavery in the territories. He travelled east by train, and on the evening of the twenty-seventh of February, in a snowstorm, he stood up in the great hall of a college called the Cooper Union, before an audience of about fifteen hundred people.

At first, they were not sure what to make of him. He was very tall and thin, in a new black suit that was wrinkled from the journey, and when he began to speak his voice was high, and his accent was that of the frontier. But as he went on, carefully, calmly, laying out his argument point by point, the hall became very still. And by the end, the audience was on its feet, cheering. He finished with words that many people remembered: let us have faith that right makes might, and in that faith, let us, to the end, dare to do our duty as we understand it.

That same day, in New York, Lincoln had his picture taken at the studio of a famous photographer, Mathew Brady. It shows him standing beside a column, one hand resting on a pile of books, looking serious and dignified. The photograph was copied and printed in newspapers and on posters all over the country. Lincoln later said, it is told, that Brady and the Cooper Union speech made him President.

In May of eighteen hundred and sixty, the Republican Party held its national convention in Chicago, in a huge wooden building put up specially for the occasion, called the Wigwam. The favourite to be chosen as the party's candidate for President was a famous senator from New York, William Seward. Lincoln did not go to Chicago. It was not the custom then for candidates to appear at conventions. He stayed in Springfield and waited for news, while his friends worked hard for him in Chicago.

To help make Lincoln seem like a man of the people, his supporters brought two old fence rails into a state convention, earlier that month, with a banner saying they had been split by Abraham Lincoln, back in eighteen hundred and thirty, near Decatur. Lincoln was asked about it. He said he could not swear he had split those very rails, but he had certainly split a great many better ones. From then on, he was known as the Rail Splitter.

In Chicago, on the eighteenth of May, the voting began. On the first ballot, Seward was ahead. On the second, Lincoln had nearly caught up. And on the third, delegates began to change their votes, one state after another, and the Wigwam erupted with cheering, and cannons were fired from the roof. Abraham Lincoln was the Republican candidate for President.

In Springfield, a messenger came running to Lincoln with a telegram. He read it quietly, and then, so the story goes, he said that there was a little woman down at the house on Eighth Street who would like to hear the news, and he went home to tell Mary.

In those days, candidates for President did not travel around giving speeches. So Lincoln stayed at home in Springfield all that summer and autumn, while his friends and supporters campaigned for him. There were parades with torches, and songs, and rallies, and young men marching in the streets at night in shiny capes, calling themselves the Wide Awakes. Visitors came from all over the country to Springfield to see him, and he met them in a room at the state house, shaking hands and talking with everybody.

One autumn day, he received a letter from an eleven-year-old girl in the state of New York named Grace Bedell. She told him that she thought he would look a great deal better if he let his whiskers grow, because his face was so thin, and that ladies liked whiskers, and would tease their husbands to vote for him. Lincoln wrote her a kind reply. He said that he had never worn whiskers, and asked whether people would think it a piece of silly affectation if he began now. But soon afterwards, he began to grow a beard. And the face that we all remember today, the bearded face, began then.

The election was held on the sixth of November, eighteen hundred and sixty. There were four candidates, because the Democrats had split into two parties, one in the North and one in the South. Lincoln spent the evening at the telegraph office in Springfield, waiting for the results to come in over the wires. Late in the night, the news came: he had won. He had won almost every state in the North, and none in the South. He went home in the early hours and told Mary, Mary, we are elected.

But it was not a happy time. Many in the South feared that Lincoln, who said slavery was wrong, would threaten their way of life. Within weeks, South Carolina declared that it was leaving the Union. By the time Lincoln left Springfield, six more Southern states had followed. They formed a new government of their own, which they called the Confederacy. The country was coming apart, even before he had taken office.

Lincoln spent those winter months quietly preparing. He went to visit his old stepmother, Sarah, in the little cabin in Coles County, to say goodbye. She told him she was afraid that something would happen to him, and that she would never see him again. He also visited his father's grave nearby. Then he packed up the house on Eighth Street, rented it out, sold some of the furniture, and gave away the family dog, a yellow dog named Fido, to a neighbour, because they thought he would not like the long journey on the train.

On the morning of the eleventh of February, eighteen hundred and sixty-one, the day before his fifty-second birthday, Lincoln stood on the back platform of a train at the little railway station in Springfield. It was a grey, cold morning, with a light rain falling. A crowd of about a thousand of his friends and neighbours had come to see him off. He had not planned to speak. But looking out at them, he spoke a few words, and he spoke them from his heart.

My friends, he said, no one, not in my situation, can appreciate my feeling of sadness at this parting. To this place, and the kindness of these people, I owe everything. Here I have lived a quarter of a century, and have passed from a young to an old man. Here my children have been born, and one is buried. I now leave, not knowing when, or whether ever, I may return, with a task before me greater than that which rested upon Washington.

He asked them to pray for him, and he bid them an affectionate farewell. Then the train pulled slowly out of the station, and the crowd stood quietly in the rain, watching it go, until it disappeared across the grey prairie towards the east.
