## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Cabin by a Spring

Welcome. Let us settle in together. If you can, turn the lights down low, and let your head sink a little deeper into the pillow. There is nothing you need to do tonight. You do not have to remember any names or any dates. You can close your eyes whenever you like, and the story will carry on quietly beside you. We will go slowly, and we will not hurry anywhere.

Tonight we will keep company with Abraham Lincoln. He was born in a one-room log cabin on the edge of the American wilderness, and he grew up with very little schooling and a great hunger for books. He split rails and steered a flatboat down the Mississippi River. He kept a small store, carried the mail in his hat, measured land with a chain, and taught himself the law by reading late into the night. He became a country lawyer who rode from town to town across the Illinois prairie. And then, in the most dangerous years his country ever knew, he became its sixteenth President.

It is a long and serious story, and parts of it are sad. But it is also a story of patience, of humour, of a gentle voice and a steady heart. And it happens to be a good story for this time of year. Because it was Abraham Lincoln, in the autumn of eighteen hundred and sixty-three, who asked the whole nation to keep a day of thanksgiving together, on the last Thursday of November. We will come to that, much later on. For now, let us go back to the very beginning.

Let us begin in the winter of eighteen hundred and nine, in the hills of Kentucky.

In those days, Kentucky was still a young state, only a few years past being a frontier. Much of the land was covered in forest. There were narrow roads, rough fields cut out of the woods, and scattered farms where families lived a long way from their neighbours. In the hilly country south of the town of Elizabethtown, there was a farm with a lovely name. It was called the Sinking Spring farm, because of a cool spring that bubbled up at the bottom of a hollow, in a shady place among the rocks, and then seemed to sink away again into the ground.

Near that spring stood a small cabin made of logs. It had one room, a dirt floor, a single door, and a fireplace made of stone and clay. It was the home of a carpenter and farmer named Thomas Lincoln, his wife Nancy, and their little daughter, Sarah, who was not yet two years old.

And there, on a Sunday, the twelfth of February, eighteen hundred and nine, a baby boy was born. His parents named him Abraham, after his grandfather, who had been killed on the frontier many years before.

We do not know very much about that morning. Nobody wrote it down at the time. But we can imagine the cabin in winter, the logs dark with cold, the fire burning on the hearth, the smell of wood smoke, and outside the bare trees of the Kentucky hills standing still in the frost. We can imagine Nancy Lincoln holding her son close by the fire, and little Sarah watching her new brother with wide eyes.

Thomas Lincoln was a steady, quiet man of about thirty. He had very little schooling. He could sign his name, but not much more. He was a good carpenter and a hard worker, and he was known in the neighbourhood as an honest man who liked to tell a story. Nancy Hanks Lincoln was tall and slender, with dark hair. People who knew her remembered her later as gentle and thoughtful, and much brighter than her circumstances. Almost nothing she wrote survives, and nobody painted her picture, so she remains something of a shadow in the story, a quiet figure standing in a cabin doorway.

The farm at Sinking Spring was not a good farm. The soil was thin and stony, and there were arguments about who really owned the land. In Kentucky at that time, land titles were often muddled, and a family could clear a field and build a house only to find that someone else held a better claim. When Abraham was about two years old, the family moved a few miles away, to another farm, on a stream called Knob Creek.

Knob Creek is the first place that Abraham Lincoln could remember. It lay in a narrow valley between steep, rounded hills, with a clear stream running along the bottom. The family had a little field there, and a cabin, and a garden. Many years later, Lincoln remembered small things from that valley. He remembered a big field where the family planted corn, and a time when a heavy rain came down from the hills and washed the seeds and the soil right out of the ground.

He remembered something else, too. Near the farm ran an old road, the main road between Louisville and Nashville. Along it went all kinds of travellers. There were farmers driving pigs and cattle, families with wagons heading west, pedlars, preachers, soldiers, and sometimes, sadly, groups of enslaved people being driven south to be sold. Kentucky was a slave state. Thomas and Nancy Lincoln belonged to a small Baptist church that did not approve of slavery, and later, Abraham would say that he could not remember a time when he did not think slavery was wrong. We will come back to that thought many times in our story, because it became one of the great questions of his life.

At Knob Creek, Abraham and Sarah went for a short while to a little school, a log schoolhouse a few miles away, where children of all ages sat on rough benches and recited their lessons out loud, all at once, so that it was sometimes called a blab school. There, he began to learn his letters. He did not go for long. School on the frontier came and went, a few weeks at a time, whenever there was a teacher and whenever the children could be spared from work.

There was sorrow at Knob Creek, too. A baby brother was born there, named Thomas after his father, but the little boy lived only a short time. In those days, on the frontier, such losses were common, and families bore them quietly, and went on.

Life in the cabin followed the seasons. In spring there was planting, and in summer there was weeding and hoeing in the heat. In autumn there was the harvest, and the gathering of nuts and berries in the woods, and the smoking of meat for the winter. In winter there was the fire, and the long dark evenings, and the stories that Thomas Lincoln liked to tell. Young Abraham loved those stories. All his life, he would be a teller of tales, and perhaps it began there, by a cabin fire in a Kentucky valley, listening to his father talk.

By the time Abraham was seven years old, Thomas Lincoln had grown tired of Kentucky. The land titles were still uncertain, and there was a lawsuit over the farm at Knob Creek. And across the Ohio River, to the north, lay Indiana, which was just becoming a state. There, the land had been properly surveyed by the government, and a man could buy it and know that it was truly his. And there was no slavery in Indiana.

So, in the late autumn of eighteen hundred and sixteen, the Lincoln family packed what they could carry, said goodbye to the valley of Knob Creek, and set out for the north. Let us go with them, slowly, through the bare November woods, towards the great river.
