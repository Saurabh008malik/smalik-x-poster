## Chapter 10: A House at War, and a Cottage on a Hill

Lincoln's train journey to Washington took twelve days. It wound through Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York and New Jersey, stopping in town after town, where crowds gathered at the stations to see the new President. In the little town of Westfield, in New York, the train stopped, and Lincoln asked if Grace Bedell was in the crowd. She was brought forward, and he stepped down from the train, and told her that he had let his whiskers grow for her. And then, as the story was remembered, he bent down and gave her a kiss on the cheek.

On the fourth of March, eighteen hundred and sixty-one, Abraham Lincoln was sworn in as the sixteenth President of the United States, on the steps of the Capitol in Washington. Behind him, the great dome of the Capitol was still unfinished, with a tall crane standing on top of it, and scaffolding all around. It seemed a little like the country itself, half built, and uncertain.

In his speech that day, Lincoln spoke to the people of the South. He said that he had no purpose to interfere with slavery in the states where it existed. But he also said that the Union could not be broken, and that he would hold the government's forts and property. And at the end, he spoke with great gentleness. We are not enemies, he said, but friends. We must not be enemies. Though passion may have strained, it must not break our bonds of affection. And he hoped that the mystic chords of memory, stretching from every battlefield and patriot grave to every living heart and hearthstone, would yet swell the chorus of the Union, when again touched, as surely they will be, by the better angels of our nature.

But it was not to be. Five weeks later, in April, Confederate guns opened fire on Fort Sumter, a Union fort in the harbour of Charleston, South Carolina. After a day and a half of bombardment, the fort surrendered. Lincoln called for volunteers to put down the rebellion. Four more Southern states left the Union. And the long and terrible Civil War began.

We will not follow the battles one by one tonight. It is enough to know that the war lasted four years, and that it was the bloodiest war in American history. Hundreds of thousands of young men died, from both North and South, in battle and from disease. And for those four years, the weight of it rested on Lincoln's shoulders.

He worked very long hours. He read reports and telegrams from the generals late into the night. He often walked over to the War Department, next to the White House, and sat in the telegraph office for hours, waiting for news from the battlefields. He met with hundreds of people, generals and politicians, soldiers and their mothers and wives, people asking for jobs and people asking for mercy. He signed many pardons for young soldiers who had been sentenced for falling asleep on guard duty or running away in fear. He said he did not think it would do the boys any good to shoot them.

The Lincoln family moved into the White House, and the boys, Willie and Tad, brought it to life. They ran through the corridors and climbed on the roof. They kept pets, ponies, goats, rabbits, and a dog. They burst into important meetings. Robert, the eldest, was away at college at Harvard. But Willie and Tad, who were ten and seven when they arrived, were the joy of their parents' lives. Willie was a gentle, thoughtful boy, very much like his father, people said. Tad was lively and full of mischief.

Then, in February of eighteen hundred and sixty-two, both boys fell ill with a fever, probably typhoid, which was thought to come from the poor water in Washington. Tad slowly recovered. But Willie grew weaker and weaker. And on the twentieth of February, eleven-year-old Willie Lincoln died, in a room in the White House.

Lincoln was heartbroken. He went into the room where his son lay, and those who were there remembered that he said, my poor boy, he was too good for this earth. And he covered his face and wept. Mary was overcome with grief, and for a long time she could not bear to go into the room where Willie had died, or see his friends. Lincoln and Tad grew closer than ever. Tad often fell asleep in his father's office in the evening, while Lincoln worked, and Lincoln would carry him off to bed.

There was one place where the family could find a little peace. About three miles from the White House, on a hill to the north of the city, there was a home for old and disabled soldiers, called the Soldiers' Home, with green lawns and shady trees. On its grounds stood a large, comfortable cottage with pointed gables. And in the summers of eighteen hundred and sixty-two, sixty-three, and sixty-four, the Lincolns lived there, to escape the heat and the crowds and the sadness of the city.

Let us go there now, on a summer evening. The air on the hill is cooler than down in the city. From the porch, you can see the distant dome of the Capitol, rising a little higher each year, catching the last of the light. Crickets are singing in the long grass. Lincoln has just ridden out from the White House on his horse, as he does every evening, and he is sitting on the porch, his long legs stretched out, reading a book of poetry, or perhaps one of Shakespeare's plays, which he loves. Inside, Mary and Tad are having their supper. And for a little while, the war seems very far away.

It was at this cottage that Lincoln did much of his thinking about the greatest decision of his presidency. As the war went on, his thoughts about slavery had grown and changed. He came to believe that ending slavery was not only right, but necessary to win the war and save the Union. Enslaved people were escaping in great numbers to the Union armies, and Black leaders like Frederick Douglass urged him to act.

In September of eighteen hundred and sixty-two, after a Union victory at a creek called Antietam, in Maryland, Lincoln announced that on the first of January, eighteen hundred and sixty-three, all enslaved people in the states still in rebellion would be declared free. And on that New Year's Day, after shaking hands with hundreds of visitors at a reception in the White House for hours, he sat down at his desk to sign the Emancipation Proclamation. His hand was tired and stiff from all the hand-shaking, and he waited a moment, so that his signature would not look shaky, and people would not think he had hesitated. Then he signed his full name. He said that if his name ever went into history, it would be for this act, and his whole soul was in it.

The Proclamation did not free every enslaved person at once. It applied only to the areas in rebellion, and it could only take effect as Union armies moved forward. But it changed the meaning of the war. From now on, the war was not only to save the Union, but to bring freedom. And it allowed Black men to join the Union army and navy, and nearly two hundred thousand of them served before the war ended.

There are hard things to remember from these years, too. In late eighteen hundred and sixty-two, after a war in Minnesota between the Dakota people and settlers, a military court sentenced more than three hundred Dakota men to death. Lincoln reviewed the records himself and reduced the number greatly. But he still allowed thirty-eight men to be hanged, on the day after Christmas, in the largest mass execution in American history. It is a sad and serious part of his story, and it belongs here, too.

Let us leave him now on the porch of the cottage on the hill, as the evening grows dark. The fireflies are rising from the grass. Far away, the lights of Washington are coming on, one by one. And a tired man sits quietly, with a book closed on his knee, looking out into the summer night.
