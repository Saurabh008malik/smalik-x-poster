## Chapter 2: Little Pigeon Creek

The Lincoln family crossed the Ohio River in the cold at the end of the year, eighteen hundred and sixteen. The river was wide and brown, and on the far side the land rose up in thick forest. This was southern Indiana, and it was still very much a wilderness. There were bears and wolves in the woods, and panthers too, and the trees grew so close together that in places the sun barely reached the ground.

The family travelled the last miles on foot and by wagon, cutting a path through the trees, until they reached a piece of land near a stream called Little Pigeon Creek. Here, Thomas Lincoln meant to build a farm and a new life.

Lincoln later remembered this as a wild region, with many bears and other wild animals still in the woods. There was no house waiting for them. According to the family's later memories, they spent that first hard winter in a rough shelter, closed on three sides and open on the fourth, with a fire burning at the open side to keep them warm. Whether or not that is exactly how it was, it was certainly a cold and difficult beginning. In time, Thomas built a proper log cabin, and the family settled in.

Abraham was only seven, but he was tall for his age, and already strong. His father put an axe into his hands. And for the next many years, Lincoln would later say, he was almost constantly handling that most useful tool. He chopped trees and split logs, cleared brush, and helped turn the forest into fields. It was hard, steady work, the kind that goes on day after day and season after season. It made him very strong. And it gave him, perhaps, a great deal of time to think.

Let us picture the farm at Little Pigeon Creek on a quiet morning. Smoke rises from the cabin chimney into the cold air. The clearing is full of tree stumps, with rows of corn planted in between them. Around the clearing, the woods stand tall and dark. Somewhere a woodpecker is knocking on a tree. A boy with long arms and legs is walking towards the woods with an axe on his shoulder, his breath steaming in the air.

For a while, life was hard but hopeful. Relatives came to join them. Nancy's aunt and uncle, Elizabeth and Thomas Sparrow, settled nearby, with a young cousin named Dennis Hanks, who became Abraham's friend.

But in the autumn of eighteen hundred and eighteen, a terrible sickness came to the settlement. People called it the milk sickness. Nobody understood it then. We know now that it came from a woodland plant, white snakeroot, which cows sometimes ate in the forest, and the poison passed into their milk. Thomas and Elizabeth Sparrow fell ill and died. And then Nancy Lincoln became sick as well.

She died on the fifth of October, eighteen hundred and eighteen. Abraham was nine years old. Sarah was eleven.

Thomas Lincoln made her coffin himself, from wood he cut and planed by hand. She was buried on a little rise among the trees, not far from the cabin. There was no minister in the settlement then to say the words over her grave. Some months later, according to later accounts, a travelling preacher came by, and a service was held for her at last.

We will not stay too long with that sad autumn. But we should know that Abraham Lincoln carried it with him all his life. Many years later, writing to a young girl who had lost her father, he told her that in this sad world, sorrow comes to all, and to the young it comes with bitterest agony, because it takes them unawares. He knew that from his own childhood, in a cabin in the Indiana woods.

The winter after Nancy died was a lonely one. Thomas Lincoln, Sarah, Abraham, and their cousin Dennis lived on together in the cabin, and things grew rough and untidy. Sarah, only twelve, did her best to keep house. The children's clothes wore thin. The cabin grew dark and cheerless.

And then, in the late autumn of eighteen hundred and nineteen, Thomas Lincoln went back to Kentucky. He went to Elizabethtown, where he had known a widow named Sarah Bush Johnston. She had three children of her own. On the second of December, eighteen hundred and nineteen, they were married. And a little while later, a wagon came rolling up through the Indiana woods to the cabin at Little Pigeon Creek, carrying a new mother, three new children, and a load of furniture, bedding, pots and pans, and household things the Lincoln children had never had.

Sarah Bush Lincoln was, by every account, a warm and kind woman. She looked at the two thin, ragged children in that dark cabin, and she set about making things better. She washed them and dressed them in clean clothes. She had the floor laid and the cabin made snug. She brought order and cheerfulness into the house. And she loved Abraham as if he had been her own son.

She saw something in him, too. She saw that he loved to read, and she encouraged him. Years later, after Lincoln was gone, she said that he was the best boy she ever saw, and that he never once gave her a cross word. And Lincoln, for his part, always spoke of her with great tenderness. Friends remembered, after his death, that he once spoke of all he owed to his angel mother. Nobody is quite sure whether he meant Nancy or Sarah. Perhaps, in his heart, it was both.

Abraham's schooling in Indiana came, as it had in Kentucky, in small pieces. There were a few short terms at little log schoolhouses nearby, a few weeks one winter, a few weeks another. Lincoln later wrote that all his schooling put together did not amount to one year. And yet he learned to read, and write, and do his sums. And above all, he learned to love books.

Books were rare on the frontier, and he read every one he could find. He is remembered reading the Bible, Aesop's Fables, Robinson Crusoe, and The Pilgrim's Progress, and a life of George Washington. There is a well-known story that he once borrowed a book about Washington from a neighbour, and the rain came through the cabin wall in the night and soaked it. He worked for the neighbour for several days, pulling corn, to pay for the damaged book. It may be partly legend, but it fits the boy that everyone remembered.

His stepmother and cousins said later that he would read by the light of the fire in the evenings, lying on the floor or sitting in the corner. He carried a book with him into the fields, and when he stopped to rest the horse at the end of a furrow, he would sit on the fence and read a page or two. He copied passages that he liked onto a board, or onto scraps of paper, so that he could remember them. And when he had read something, he liked to tell it again in his own words, to make it plain. He would stand on a tree stump and give speeches to the other children, imitating the travelling preachers, and making everyone laugh.

Not everyone thought this was a good use of time. His father sometimes thought the boy was lazy, reading when there was work to be done. There was a coolness between Thomas and Abraham that never quite went away. But Abraham did his work. And he kept on reading.

So the years went by at Little Pigeon Creek. The forest was slowly pushed back. The fields grew wider. The tall boy with the axe grew taller still, until he was well over six feet, with big hands and a long, thoughtful face. And beyond the woods, not far to the south, the great Ohio River went flowing quietly by, towards the Mississippi and the sea, carrying boats and goods and travellers to places he had never seen.

Let us leave him there for a moment, a young man sitting on a fence rail at the edge of a field in the evening, a borrowed book open in his hands, while the light fades over the Indiana woods.
