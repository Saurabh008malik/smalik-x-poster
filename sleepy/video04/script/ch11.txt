## Chapter 11: A Day of Thanksgiving, and a Few Words at Gettysburg

Let us come now to the autumn of eighteen hundred and sixty-three, and to the part of the story that belongs especially to this season of the year.

For many years, Americans had held days of thanksgiving. The Pilgrims and other early settlers had kept them, and so had many states, each on its own chosen day. George Washington had once proclaimed a national day of thanks. But there was no regular national holiday. Each state did as it pleased, some in November, some at other times, and some not at all.

There was one woman who had decided to change that. Her name was Sarah Josepha Hale. She was the editor of a very popular magazine for women, called Godey's Lady's Book, and she was a writer and poet, too. Many people say she wrote the nursery rhyme about Mary and her little lamb. For about seventeen years, she had been writing editorials and letters to governors and Presidents, asking for a single national day of thanksgiving, held by everyone, all across the country, on the same day, the last Thursday of November. She believed it could help bring the nation together.

The country had never needed bringing together more than it did in eighteen sixty-three. That summer, there had been a great and terrible battle, over three days at the beginning of July, around a small town in Pennsylvania called Gettysburg. The Union army had won, and at almost the same time, far to the west, the city of Vicksburg on the Mississippi had surrendered to General Grant. People in the North dared to hope, for the first time, that the war might be turning. But the cost had been enormous.

In late September, Sarah Josepha Hale wrote once more, this time to President Lincoln himself. And this time, someone listened.

On the third of October, eighteen hundred and sixty-three, a proclamation was issued in Lincoln's name. The words were drafted, it seems, by his Secretary of State, William Seward, and signed by the President. It spoke of the year that was drawing to its close, and how it had been filled with the blessings of fruitful fields and healthful skies. It spoke of how, even in the midst of a great civil war, the fields had been ploughed and the harvests gathered, and the mines and the shipyards had kept working, and the population had grown. And it invited the people of the United States, in every part of the country, and those at sea, and those living abroad, to set apart and observe the last Thursday of November as a day of Thanksgiving and Praise.

It asked them, too, to remember with tender care all those who had become widows, orphans, mourners or sufferers in the war, and to pray for the healing of the wounds of the nation.

That year, the last Thursday of November fell on the twenty-sixth. And from that year to this, Americans have kept a national Thanksgiving Day every year, in the late autumn, when the harvest is in and the leaves have fallen.

Let us imagine one of those first Thanksgiving Days, in eighteen sixty-three. In a farmhouse somewhere in Ohio, or in New England, a family is gathered around a table. There is a fire in the hearth. There is a turkey, perhaps, and bread, and pies made from the pumpkins and apples of the autumn harvest. Outside, the fields are brown and bare, and a cold wind is blowing. There may be an empty chair at the table, for a son or a brother who is away at the war. And there is a quiet moment, before the meal begins, when they bow their heads and give thanks for what they have, and remember those who are missing.

There is a little family story from the White House, too, told many years later. It is said that the Lincolns were once given a live turkey to be cooked for a holiday dinner, and that Tad grew so fond of it that he begged his father to spare its life. And his father, who could never refuse Tad anything, wrote out a reprieve for the turkey. We cannot be sure exactly when it happened, or even whether every detail is true. But it is a gentle story, and it sounds very much like them.

Meanwhile, at Gettysburg, a new cemetery was being made for the thousands of Union soldiers who had died there in July. It was laid out on a gentle hill just outside the town, called Cemetery Hill. And a ceremony was planned to dedicate it, on the nineteenth of November, one week before that first national Thanksgiving.

The main speaker was to be Edward Everett, a famous orator, a former senator and governor and college president, who was known for his long and beautiful speeches. Lincoln was invited too, almost as an afterthought, to say a few appropriate remarks at the end.

He travelled to Gettysburg by train the day before. That night, he stayed in the house of a local lawyer, David Wills, on the town square. Crowds filled the streets, and there were bands and singing late into the night. Lincoln worked a little on his speech in his room, and then he went to bed. Tad had been ill when he left Washington, and Lincoln was worried about him. The next morning, a telegram came to say that Tad was a little better.

On the morning of the nineteenth of November, Lincoln rode in a procession to the new cemetery, on a horse that was rather too small for him, so that his long legs nearly reached the ground. A crowd of about fifteen thousand people had gathered on the hill. It was a cool, clear autumn day. Around them stretched the fields and woods and ridges where the battle had been fought, now quiet, with the trees turning gold and brown.

Edward Everett spoke for about two hours. His speech was learned and graceful, and full of history and detail. Then a hymn was sung. And then the President was introduced.

Lincoln stood up, put on his spectacles, held a page or two in his hand, and began to speak in his high, clear voice. Four score and seven years ago, he said, our fathers brought forth on this continent a new nation, conceived in liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.

The whole speech was only about two hundred and seventy words long. It took about two minutes. Some in the crowd had barely realised he had begun before he had finished. He spoke of the great war, testing whether a nation so conceived and so dedicated could long endure. He said that they could not truly dedicate or hallow that ground, because the brave men who had struggled there had already done it, far above their poor power to add or take away. He said that the world would little note nor long remember what they said there, but it could never forget what those men did there. And he ended by asking the living to dedicate themselves to the great task remaining, so that the nation, under God, should have a new birth of freedom, and that government of the people, by the people, for the people, should not perish from the earth.

Then he sat down. There was some applause. Lincoln, it is said, worried that the speech had not been a success. But the next day, Edward Everett wrote him a kind letter. He said he would be glad if he could flatter himself that he had come as near to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as the President had in two minutes.

Today, the world has not forgotten those few words. They are carved in stone in Washington, and learned by heart by schoolchildren across the country.

A week later, on the twenty-sixth of November, the nation kept its first Thanksgiving Day together. And Lincoln, back in Washington, was in bed with a mild case of smallpox, which he had caught around the time of the journey to Gettysburg. He joked, according to a story told later, that at last he had something he could give to everybody.

Let us leave him there, resting, in a quiet room in the White House, on a grey November afternoon, while across the country families sit down together at their tables, and give thanks.
