## Chapter 6: Mary, and a House on Eighth Street

Mary Todd was born in Lexington, Kentucky, in eighteen hundred and eighteen, into a wealthy and well-known family. Her father was a banker and a politician. She had grown up in a large brick house with servants, and had gone to good schools, where she studied French, literature, music and dancing. She was clever and well-read, she loved politics, which was unusual for a young woman of her time, and she had a sharp wit and strong opinions. She was small, with blue eyes and light brown hair, and when she laughed, people said, the whole room seemed brighter.

In eighteen hundred and thirty-nine, she came to Springfield to live with her older sister, Elizabeth, who was married to Ninian Edwards. The Edwards house, up on a hill, was one of the grandest in town, and the centre of Springfield society. Many young men came calling on Mary. One of them was Stephen Douglas, a short, energetic, ambitious lawyer and politician, who would one day become Lincoln's great rival. But the one she came to care for was the tall, awkward, funny lawyer from the backwoods.

On the surface, they seemed a strange match. She was from a rich family. He had grown up in a log cabin. She was small and quick, and he was tall and slow and thoughtful. She loved parties and fine clothes, and he hardly noticed what he was wearing. But they had things in common, too. They had both been born in Kentucky. They both loved poetry. And they both loved politics, and were both members of the Whig party, and both of them were ambitious. Mary told friends, according to stories remembered later, that she would marry a man who would one day be President.

Their courtship was not smooth. At some point around the end of eighteen hundred and forty, they became engaged. But then something went wrong. On the first of January, eighteen hundred and forty-one, the engagement was broken off. Nobody knows exactly why. Perhaps Lincoln had doubts about whether he could make her happy, or support her in the way she was used to. Perhaps her family disapproved. Whatever the reason, Lincoln fell into a very low and dark mood for weeks afterwards. He wrote to his law partner that he was the most miserable man living. His friend Joshua Speed, who was moving back to Kentucky, took him there to stay for a while, on the Speed family's farm near Louisville, to rest and recover.

Time passed. And in the summer of eighteen hundred and forty-two, a friend's wife arranged for Lincoln and Mary to meet again, quietly, at her house. Slowly, gently, they came back together. And on the fourth of November, eighteen hundred and forty-two, they were married in the parlour of the Edwards house, in the evening, with a small group of friends and family, while rain fell outside. Lincoln gave Mary a gold ring engraved with the words, love is eternal.

They began their married life in a single rented room at a boarding house called the Globe Tavern, in Springfield. It cost them four dollars a week, for room and meals. It was not the kind of home Mary was used to, with the noise of travellers coming and going, and a bell that rang loudly whenever a stagecoach arrived. But they were happy there. And on the first of August, eighteen hundred and forty-three, their first son was born. They named him Robert, after Mary's father.

In eighteen hundred and forty-four, they bought a house of their own. It stood on the corner of Eighth Street and Jackson Street, a few blocks from the square. It was a neat, simple wooden house, painted a light brown colour, with green shutters, and at first it had only one and a half storeys. They paid one thousand five hundred dollars for it, and it was the only house Abraham Lincoln ever owned. They lived there for seventeen years. Today, it still stands, and you can walk through its rooms.

Let us step inside it, on a quiet evening. There is a parlour at the front, with a horsehair sofa and a carpet with a pattern of flowers. There is a stove in the sitting room, and a little kitchen at the back. Upstairs, in time, there are bedrooms, one for Abraham and one for Mary, as was common in those days, and rooms for the children. Lincoln likes to lie on the floor in the sitting room in the evening, with his head propped on an upturned chair, reading aloud, sometimes to himself, sometimes to whoever is listening. Mary sometimes finds this habit very trying. A clock ticks on the mantelpiece. Outside, a horse and buggy go slowly by in the street, and somewhere a dog is barking.

The family grew. A second son, Edward, called Eddie, was born in March of eighteen hundred and forty-six.

That same year, Lincoln was elected to the United States Congress, as a Whig. In the autumn of eighteen hundred and forty-seven, the whole family travelled east to Washington, the capital, by stagecoach and train and steamboat. For a while, they lived together in a boarding house near the Capitol, but Mary found it lonely and dull, and she took the boys to stay with her family in Kentucky. Lincoln missed them. He wrote her letters about little things, about the boys, and asked her to tell the children not to forget their father.

His time in Congress was short, only one term. He opposed the war with Mexico, which was being fought at that time, and asked pointed questions of President Polk about how it began. That was not popular back home in Illinois, and he did not run again. But while he was in Washington he also tried, quietly and carefully, to put forward a plan to end slavery gradually in the District of Columbia, with payment to the slaveholders and a vote of the local people. It did not get far. But it showed which way his heart was leaning.

There is one more curious thing from those years. On his way home from Washington, travelling on the Great Lakes, Lincoln saw a boat stuck on a sandbar, and remembered his own flatboat stuck on the dam at New Salem. So, being a practical man, he invented a device to help lift boats over shallow places, using inflatable chambers along the sides of the hull. He made a little wooden model of it, and in eighteen hundred and forty-nine, he was granted a patent. It was never used. But he remains the only President of the United States to hold a patent.

Back home in Springfield, sorrow came again. In the winter of eighteen hundred and fifty, little Eddie fell ill. He was nearly four years old. For about fifty days, his parents nursed him, and then, on the first of February, he died. Mary was overwhelmed with grief, and Lincoln, too, grieved deeply. A few days later, a poem appeared in a local newspaper about the little boy, and its last line was, of such is the kingdom of heaven. We do not know for sure who wrote it.

But the house on Eighth Street did not stay sad forever. In December of that same year, eighteen hundred and fifty, another baby boy was born. They named him William, and he was called Willie. And in April of eighteen hundred and fifty-three, there came one more, a lively little boy named Thomas, after Lincoln's father. Because he had a large head when he was born, and wriggled like a tadpole, his father called him Tad, and the name stuck.

Let us leave the Lincoln house now, on a winter's night. The lamps are lit in the windows. In the sitting room, a tall man lies on the floor with a book, while two small boys climb over him. Mary is sewing by the stove. And outside, soft snow is falling on the quiet streets of Springfield.
