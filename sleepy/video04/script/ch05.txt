## Chapter 5: Springfield, and a Room Above the Store

Springfield, in the spring of eighteen hundred and thirty-seven, was a muddy little town of about fifteen hundred people. Its streets were unpaved, and when it rained, which it often did, they turned into rivers of thick black mud. Pigs wandered about freely, rooting in the street. But it was growing fast, and it had just been chosen as the new capital of Illinois, so there was a feeling of hope and bustle about it. New buildings were going up around a central square, where a grand new state house would soon rise.

Lincoln rode into town on the fifteenth of April with his saddlebags, and went to a general store on the square, run by a young man named Joshua Speed. He asked how much it would cost to buy the things he needed for a bed: a mattress, sheets, a blanket and a pillow. Speed added it all up. It came to about seventeen dollars. Lincoln said it was probably cheap enough, but that he did not have the money. He asked if Speed would give him credit until Christmas, and said, with a sad face, that if he did not do well as a lawyer, he might never be able to pay.

Speed later remembered that Lincoln's face was so gloomy that he felt sorry for him. He said there was a simpler way. Upstairs, above the store, he had a large room with a big double bed, and Lincoln was welcome to share it. Lincoln asked where the room was. Speed pointed to the stairs. Lincoln picked up his saddlebags, went up, put them down on the floor, came back down, and said, with a broad smile, well, Speed, I'm moved.

That is how Speed told the story many years later, and whether or not every word is exact, it is how their friendship began. Lincoln and Joshua Speed lived together in that room for nearly four years, and Speed became perhaps the closest friend Lincoln ever had. In the evenings, a group of young men would gather around the fire in the back of the store, talking about politics, telling stories, and arguing about everything under the sun. Lincoln was always at the centre of it, with his long legs stretched out towards the fire, telling some tale that made them all roar with laughter.

Lincoln's legal career began with John Todd Stuart, the man who had encouraged him to study law. Stuart took him on as a junior partner. Their office was a small room, with a few chairs, a table, a bookcase, and a bed. Lincoln did much of the work while Stuart was busy with politics. Later, Lincoln became partners with another lawyer, Stephen Logan, a careful and precise man from whom he learned a great deal about preparing cases properly. And in eighteen hundred and forty-four, he took on a junior partner of his own, a young man named William Herndon. Lincoln and Herndon stayed partners for the rest of Lincoln's life. Their office was famously untidy, with papers stuffed everywhere. Lincoln kept a bundle of papers with a label on it that said, if you can't find it anywhere else, look in this.

Being a lawyer in Illinois in those days meant a great deal of travel. The courts did not sit all year in one place. Instead, a judge rode from county to county, holding court for a few days in each county seat, and the lawyers rode along with him. This was called riding the circuit. Twice a year, in spring and autumn, Lincoln would set off across the prairie on horseback, or later in a buggy, and be gone for weeks at a time. We will ride the circuit with him a little later in our story.

Meanwhile, he kept working in the legislature. Springfield was now the capital, and the members of the legislature met there. Lincoln was a member of the Whig party, which believed in what it called internal improvements, building roads, canals and railways, and in banks to help businesses grow. He worked hard to help bring the capital to Springfield. He also made some mistakes. The state spent a great deal of money on canals and railways that were never finished, and was left deep in debt for years. Lincoln had supported those plans, and he learned from it.

In eighteen hundred and thirty-seven, the legislature passed resolutions condemning the groups who wanted to end slavery, called abolitionists. Lincoln was one of only a few members who voted against those resolutions. And together with one other member, Dan Stone, he wrote a short protest, which said that slavery was founded on both injustice and bad policy. It was a cautious statement. It also said that spreading abolitionist ideas tended to make things worse rather than better. But it was a statement on the record, at twenty-eight years old, that slavery was unjust. It was a seed of something that would grow.

In January of eighteen hundred and thirty-eight, Lincoln gave a speech to a young men's society in Springfield, called the Lyceum. It was about the dangers of mob violence, and about how important it was for people to respect the laws and the Constitution, so that free government could last. It was a young man's speech, full of grand words. But in it you can hear, already, the idea that would guide him all his life: that the American experiment in self-government was precious and fragile, and had to be protected.

Springfield in those years was a lively little town. There were dances and parties, especially when the legislature was in session and the town filled up with politicians and their families. Lincoln was awkward at parties. He was very tall and thin, with a long neck and big hands and feet, and he did not dance well. He was much more comfortable telling stories to the men in the corner than talking to the young ladies. But he went along to the parties anyway.

And at one of those parties, at the house of a man named Ninian Edwards, the son of a former governor, Lincoln met a lively young woman from Kentucky, who had come to stay with her sister. Her name was Mary Todd.

Let us pause here, in the soft lamplight of a Springfield parlour, with the sound of a piano and voices in the next room, and a tall, shy lawyer standing in the doorway, looking across at a young woman with bright eyes and a quick laugh. Their story is our next chapter.
