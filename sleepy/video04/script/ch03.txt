## Chapter 3: Down the Great River

As Abraham grew into a young man, his sister Sarah grew up too. She was a cheerful, capable young woman, and in the summer of eighteen hundred and twenty-six she married a neighbour, Aaron Grigsby, and went to live on a farm nearby. For a little while she was happy. But in January of eighteen hundred and twenty-eight, Sarah died in childbirth, and her baby with her. She was not yet twenty-one.

It was a second great loss for Abraham, coming ten years after his mother. Those who were there remembered that when he heard the news he sat down on a log and put his face in his hands. He was nineteen years old. The two children who had kept each other company in the cabin by Little Pigeon Creek, after their mother died, were now only one.

Let us move gently on from that sorrow, as Abraham himself had to do, and go down to the river.

The Ohio River ran past the southern edge of the county, and on it went all the traffic of the West. There were great paddle-wheel steamboats, puffing smoke, and there were flatboats. A flatboat was a simple thing. It was a long, wide, flat-bottomed box made of heavy planks, with a little cabin or shelter in the middle, and long oars, called sweeps, at the sides and the back for steering. A flatboat could not go upstream. It was built to go one way only, floating down with the current, carrying corn, pork, flour and other goods from the farms to the markets in the South. When it reached the end of its journey, the boat itself was broken up and sold as lumber, and the boatmen walked home or took a steamboat back up the river.

In eighteen hundred and twenty-eight, when he was nineteen, Abraham Lincoln was hired by a local merchant, James Gentry, to help take a flatboat full of goods all the way down to New Orleans. With him went Gentry's son, Allen. The two young men loaded the boat, pushed off into the Ohio, and let the river take them.

Imagine that journey. Day after day, the brown water slides gently under the boat. The banks go by slowly, first the green hills of Kentucky and Indiana, then the wide flat lands where the Ohio joins the Mississippi. The Mississippi is enormous, a mile wide in places, with islands and sandbars and long curving bends. The two young men take turns at the steering oar. They tie up at night along the bank, and cook their supper over a small fire, and listen to the frogs and the night birds, and watch the stars come out over the water. In the mornings there is mist on the river, and the sun comes up pale and red through it.

They floated south past towns and plantations, past Natchez on its high bluff, past Baton Rouge, until at last they came to New Orleans. It must have seemed like another world to a young man from the backwoods. It was one of the busiest ports in America, crowded with ships from all over the world, full of strange languages, French and Spanish and many others, and markets and churches and narrow streets with iron balconies.

New Orleans was also one of the largest slave markets in the country. We do not know exactly what Lincoln saw there on this journey, and some of the stories told about it later are uncertain. But it is likely that he saw enslaved people being bought and sold, and he never forgot what slavery looked like up close.

When the goods were sold, the young men made their way home, back up the river by steamboat, to Indiana.

Two years later, the Lincoln family moved again. In the late winter of eighteen hundred and thirty, there was talk of more milk sickness in the Indiana settlement, and Thomas Lincoln had relatives in Illinois who wrote that the land there was good. So once more the family packed up everything they owned. This time they travelled with several families together, the Lincolns, the Johnstons, and the Hankses, with wagons pulled by oxen. Abraham, who had just turned twenty-one, drove one of the teams.

They travelled for about two weeks, through the early spring mud, across rivers swollen with melting snow, until they reached the prairie country of central Illinois, near the town of Decatur. Here Abraham helped his father build yet another cabin, clear yet another field, and split rails for a fence around it. Those rails, split by his own hands that summer, would become famous many years later, in a way nobody could then have imagined.

That first winter in Illinois was one that people talked about for the rest of their lives. They called it the Winter of the Deep Snow. The snow began falling at the end of December, and it kept on falling, until it lay two or three feet deep across the prairie, with drifts much higher. Then a hard freeze came and put a crust of ice on top. It lasted for weeks and weeks. Deer and other animals were trapped in the snow. Families were shut in their cabins, short of food and firewood. Let us imagine the Lincoln cabin then, with the white prairie stretching away on every side under a grey sky, silent and still, and the smoke from the chimney rising straight up in the frozen air.

When spring finally came, Abraham Lincoln was ready to go his own way. He was twenty-two now, a grown man, and the law said his time of working for his father was over. He would always help his father and stepmother when he could, all his life. But he was not going to be a farmer.

In the spring of eighteen hundred and thirty-one, a trader named Denton Offutt hired Abraham, along with his cousin John Hanks and his stepbrother John Johnston, to take another flatboat down to New Orleans. The young men built the boat themselves on the Sangamon River, and loaded it with barrels of pork and corn and with live hogs, and pushed off.

They had not gone far when something happened that would change Lincoln's life. Near a little village on a bluff above the river, a place called New Salem, there was a mill with a dam across the Sangamon. The flatboat floated onto the top of the dam, and stuck there. The front of the boat hung out over the edge, and the back filled up with water. The boat was in danger of sinking, cargo and all.

The people of New Salem came down to the riverbank to watch. And they saw a very tall young stranger take charge. According to the account that was remembered later, he had the cargo moved to the shore to lighten the boat, and then he borrowed a tool and bored a hole in the front of the boat, where it hung over the dam. The water ran out through the hole, the boat lifted and tipped forward, and slid down over the dam into the river below. Then he plugged the hole, the cargo was loaded back on, and off they went.

The people of New Salem never forgot it. And Denton Offutt, who was a great talker with big plans, was very impressed. He had in mind to open a store in New Salem, and he told Lincoln that when he came back from New Orleans, there would be a job waiting for him there.

So the flatboat floated on, down the Sangamon to the Illinois, down the Illinois to the Mississippi, and down the Mississippi once again to New Orleans. And in the summer of eighteen hundred and thirty-one, Abraham Lincoln came walking back into the little village on the bluff, with everything he owned, ready to begin.
