## Chapter 4: The Village on the Bluff

New Salem was a small place. When Lincoln came to live there in the summer of eighteen hundred and thirty-one, it had perhaps a hundred people, or a little more, and two dozen or so log buildings strung along a single dusty street on a bluff above the Sangamon River. There was the mill down by the river, a tavern, a blacksmith, a cooper who made barrels, a doctor or two, a few stores, and the homes of the families who lived there. Around it lay woods and farms, and beyond them the open prairie.

Today, the village has been rebuilt as it was, with log cabins and rail fences, and visitors can walk down that same street. But let us imagine it as it was then, on an ordinary summer morning. The smell of wood smoke and fresh-cut timber. Chickens scratching in the road. A horse tied up outside the store. Children running down towards the river. And a very tall young man, in trousers that were always a few inches too short, standing behind the counter of Denton Offutt's new store, weighing out sugar and coffee and talking with the customers.

Lincoln was a clerk in Offutt's store, and he slept in the back of it. He also helped run Offutt's mill. And very quickly, he became known to everyone in the village. He was friendly and funny and always ready with a story. He was strong, too. There was a rough group of young men from a nearby settlement called Clary's Grove, who liked to test newcomers, and their leader, Jack Armstrong, challenged Lincoln to a wrestling match. Nobody is quite sure, now, who won it. Some said it was a draw. But Lincoln held his own, and afterwards, the Clary's Grove boys became his loyal friends.

People also came to know that he was honest. Stories were told about it later, and some of them may have grown in the telling. One says that he once found he had given a customer a few cents too little in change, and walked several miles after the store closed to give it back. Whether that exact story is true, the people of New Salem began calling him Honest Abe, and the name stayed with him.

He kept on reading, too. A schoolmaster in the village, Mentor Graham, helped him study English grammar, and Lincoln walked some miles to borrow a grammar book, and worked through it on his own. He joined a little debating society that met in the village, and when he first stood up to speak, the others were surprised. He began awkwardly, with his hands in his pockets, but as he went on, he spoke so clearly and sensibly that by the end they were all listening closely.

In the spring of eighteen hundred and thirty-two, when he was twenty-three, Lincoln did something bold. He announced that he would run for a seat in the Illinois state legislature. He wrote a long letter to the people of the county, explaining what he believed in, which was mostly better roads and better rivers for travel, and better schools. And he ended it modestly, saying that he was young and unknown, and that if the good people in their wisdom saw fit to keep him in the background, he had been too familiar with disappointments to be very much chagrined.

But before the election came, there was a war. In the spring of that year, a band of Sauk and Fox people, led by a chief named Black Hawk, crossed the Mississippi back into Illinois, into lands they had been made to give up. The governor called for volunteers, and the young men of New Salem joined up. Lincoln was among them. And the men of his company elected him their captain.

He never fought in a battle. He later joked about the war, saying that the only blood he lost was to the mosquitoes. But he also saw the aftermath of fighting, and helped bury men who had been killed. And all his life he said that being chosen as captain by his neighbours gave him more pleasure than any success he had afterwards. It was the first time a crowd of ordinary people had looked at him and said, we trust you.

The war was short, and he came home only a couple of weeks before the election. He lost. He came eighth out of thirteen candidates across the county. But in his own village, New Salem, almost every man who voted cast his vote for Lincoln. The people who knew him best wanted him.

The next few years in New Salem were years of trying many things. Offutt's store had failed, and Offutt had gone away. So Lincoln and a partner, William Berry, bought a store of their own. But it did not do well either, and when Berry died, Lincoln was left with the debts. He called it, with a sad smile, his national debt. It took him years to pay it all back. But he did pay it.

In eighteen hundred and thirty-three, he was made the postmaster of New Salem. It was a small job. The mail came only once or twice a week. But it meant that he could read all the newspapers that came through the post office, which he loved. It is said, and it may well be true, that when he went out, he carried the letters in his hat, so that if he met someone on the road, he could give them their mail on the spot.

That same year he also became a deputy surveyor. He knew nothing about surveying at first, so he borrowed books and studied mathematics and geometry until he could do it. Then he rode out across the county with a compass and a long chain, measuring farms and laying out roads and marking the boundaries of new towns. It was good, quiet, outdoor work, and it took him all over the county, where he met many people.

And in eighteen hundred and thirty-four, he ran for the legislature again. This time, he won. He was twenty-five years old. He borrowed money to buy a proper suit, and that winter he went off to the state capital, which was then at Vandalia, to begin his career as a lawmaker. He would be re-elected three more times.

In Vandalia, a fellow member of the legislature, a lawyer named John Todd Stuart, encouraged Lincoln to study law. So he did, in the way he did everything, mostly by himself. He borrowed law books, including the great commentaries of William Blackstone, and he read them under the trees, or lying on the counter of the store, or walking along the road. It is said that people in New Salem would see him lying on his back in the shade with his long legs up against a tree, reading. In September of eighteen hundred and thirty-six, he was licensed to practise as a lawyer.

There is one more story from New Salem, a tender and uncertain one. In the village lived a young woman named Ann Rutledge, the daughter of the tavern keeper. Many years later, after Lincoln's death, some of the old people of New Salem said that he had loved her, and that she had died of a fever in the summer of eighteen hundred and thirty-five, and that he was so stricken with grief that his friends worried about him. Historians still are not sure how much of this is true, because it all comes from memories gathered thirty years afterwards. We can only say that Ann Rutledge was real, that she died young, and that the people who knew Lincoln remembered him as deeply sad that summer.

By the spring of eighteen hundred and thirty-seven, New Salem was fading. The river was too shallow for steamboats, and the main roads went elsewhere. People were drifting away to other towns. And the state capital was about to move to Springfield, only twenty miles away.

So, on a spring day in eighteen hundred and thirty-seven, Abraham Lincoln borrowed a horse, packed everything he owned into a pair of saddlebags, and rode away from the village on the bluff. He was twenty-eight, a lawyer with very little money and a good many debts, and a great many friends. Let us ride with him, quietly, along the road through the spring woods to Springfield.
