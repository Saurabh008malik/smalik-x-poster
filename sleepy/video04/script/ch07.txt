## Chapter 7: Riding the Prairie Circuit

After his single term in Congress, Lincoln came home to Springfield and, for a while, stepped back from politics. He turned his whole attention to the law. And for the next several years, from about eighteen hundred and forty-nine to eighteen hundred and fifty-four, he became one of the best-known lawyers in Illinois.

A great deal of that work happened on the road. Lincoln belonged to what was called the Eighth Judicial Circuit, a huge area of central Illinois that stretched across many counties. In the spring and again in the autumn, the judge set off to hold court in each county seat in turn, and a little band of lawyers travelled along with him. Lincoln rode the circuit for many years, and he loved it. Some of the other lawyers went home to their families on weekends. Lincoln usually stayed out on the circuit the whole time.

Let us travel with him for a while. It is a morning in early spring. The prairie stretches away on every side, flat and wide, under an enormous sky. In those days, much of it was still wild grassland, with tall grass waving in the wind, and wild flowers, and here and there a grove of trees along a creek. The road is little more than a muddy track. A tall man in a black coat and a tall hat sits in a small buggy pulled by his horse, called Old Tom in some of the old circuit stories, or sometimes rides on horseback, with his saddlebags behind him, and a faded green umbrella with his name sewn inside it. In the saddlebags are a few clothes, a few law papers, and a book or two.

The days on the circuit were long, and the travel was slow. Sometimes they had to cross creeks swollen with spring rain, and the horses splashed through the brown water up to their bellies. Sometimes it was so muddy that they had to get down and push. At night they stayed at small country inns or taverns, and the lawyers often had to share rooms, and even beds, as was the custom then. Lincoln was the best company of all. In the evenings, the lawyers and the judge and the local people would gather around the fire at the inn, and Lincoln would tell stories, one after another, until people were wiping tears of laughter from their eyes.

His stories were often about country people he had known in Indiana and Illinois, and he told them with a straight face, in their own way of talking. He liked to use a little story to make a point, both in the courtroom and outside it. People who heard him said he could make a jury laugh one minute and think hard the next.

In each county seat, court was held in the courthouse, usually a plain building on the town square. For a few days, the little town would be crowded with farmers and their families, who had come in from the countryside to bring their cases, or to watch. A court session was a kind of entertainment in those days. People would sit and listen as the lawyers argued, and they would talk about the best speeches for weeks afterwards.

Most of Lincoln's cases were ordinary ones. There were arguments over debts, over land, over boundaries, over a horse that had been sold, or a fence that had been moved, or a pig that had wandered into the wrong field. He handled thousands of cases in his career, big and small. He also argued many cases before the Illinois Supreme Court, and later, he worked on important cases for the railways, which were spreading across the state in the eighteen fifties, bringing new towns and new business wherever they went.

People came to trust him because he was known to be fair. He usually tried to persuade people to settle their quarrels without going to court at all, if they could. He once wrote some notes for a lecture to young lawyers, and in them he advised that they should discourage people from going to law whenever they could, and should persuade their neighbours to compromise. As a peacemaker, he wrote, the lawyer has a superior opportunity of being a good man. He also wrote that a lawyer should resolve to be honest at all events, and if he could not be an honest lawyer, he should be honest without being a lawyer.

There is one famous case from these years, from eighteen hundred and fifty-eight. A young man named William Armstrong, called Duff, was accused of murder after a fight at a camp meeting at night. He was the son of Jack and Hannah Armstrong, the old friends from Clary's Grove, near New Salem, who had looked after Lincoln when he was a poor young man. Jack had died, and Hannah came to Lincoln and asked for his help. He took the case without asking for any payment. A witness said he had seen the fight clearly, by the light of the moon. Lincoln brought out an almanac, a little book of the calendar and the moon and the weather, which showed that on that night the moon had been low in the sky, near setting, and that the witness could not have seen as much as he said. The jury found Duff Armstrong not guilty.

Lincoln's family life went on in Springfield while he was away. Mary ran the house and looked after the boys. She did not always like his long absences, and she was nervous when he was gone, especially during storms. But when he came home, it was always a joyful time. Lincoln was a very indulgent father. He let the boys do almost anything they liked. Herndon, his law partner, complained that on Sundays Lincoln would bring Willie and Tad to the law office, and the little boys would pull the books off the shelves, empty the inkwells, scatter the papers, and Lincoln would only smile and go on with his work.

In the evenings at home, he would take the boys for walks, or pull them along in a little wagon, or carry them on his shoulders. Neighbours remembered seeing him walking down the street with a child on his shoulders and another holding his hand, deep in thought, sometimes not even noticing who he passed.

And there was another kind of quiet in these years. Lincoln liked to read and to think about things deeply. He studied geometry on the circuit, working through the books of the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, by candlelight in country inns, to train his mind to reason clearly and prove things step by step. He wanted to understand exactly what it meant to demonstrate something, so that no one could doubt it. All of this would show, before long, in the way he argued and spoke.

So the seasons turned. Spring on the circuit, with the prairie green and wet. Summer in Springfield, with the heat and the dust. Autumn on the circuit again, with the grass turning gold and the wind blowing cold across the plains. Winter at home, with the fire and the boys and the books.

Lincoln was in his forties now, a successful and respected lawyer, a family man, and he thought perhaps his life in politics was over. But in eighteen hundred and fifty-four, something happened in Washington that brought him back, wide awake, into public life. Let us leave him on a quiet autumn evening on the prairie, the buggy creaking along the road, the sun going down red over the tall grass, and Lincoln, deep in thought, letting the old horse find the way.
