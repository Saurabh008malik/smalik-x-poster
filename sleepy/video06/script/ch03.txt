## Chapter 3: Plays in the Barn, and Stories in Little Notebooks

The Austen family loved a performance. When the older boys were home from Oxford, and the pupils were away, the rectory sometimes turned into a little theatre. They acted plays in the dining room, and in the summer they put on plays in the barn, which was cleared out and fitted up with a stage and scenery. James, the eldest, wrote clever prologues and epilogues in verse to be spoken before and after. Friends and neighbours came to watch.

They performed comedies that were popular in London at the time, plays full of mistaken identities, scheming servants, and lovers hiding behind screens. Jane was still a child during most of these theatricals, and she probably watched far more than she acted. But she watched closely. Many years later, in her novel Mansfield Park, a group of young people decide to put on a play in a country house, and the rehearsals stir up all kinds of flirtations and jealousies. She knew very well how much excitement, and how much trouble, a family play could cause.

One of the brightest lights of these performances was a visitor from abroad. She was the Austens' cousin, Eliza, the daughter of Mr Austen's sister. Eliza had grown up partly in India and partly in France, and had married a French nobleman, a count, so that she was now the Comtesse de Feuillide. She came to stay at Steventon wearing the latest fashions, speaking French, playing the piano forte, and flirting charmingly with her cousins, especially with James and Henry. To two country girls in a Hampshire rectory, she must have seemed like a bird of paradise.

Eliza's story would later turn sad. During the French Revolution, her husband was arrested and executed in Paris, in the year seventeen ninety-four. Eliza was safe in England by then, with her little son, Hastings. Some years later she married her cousin Henry, the lively one, and became Jane's sister-in-law. Jane was fond of her, and Eliza's bright, worldly, slightly mischievous spirit seems to drift through several of the more sparkling women in Jane's fiction.

But the most important thing happening at Steventon in those years was not on the stage. It was in a set of notebooks.

From about the age of eleven or twelve, Jane began to write stories. She wrote them to amuse her family, and she read them aloud, and her family laughed. And because they laughed, she wrote more. Later, she copied the best of these early pieces carefully into three notebooks, which she called, with mock grandness, Volume the First, Volume the Second, and Volume the Third. The notebooks still exist today, and you can see her small, neat handwriting on the pages.

These early pieces are wonderfully silly. They are short, wild, and full of jokes. Heroines faint at the smallest provocation. Strangers fall in love in a single minute and marry the next morning. People are knocked down in carriage accidents and then sit up to complain about the weather. Many of the stories make fun of the sentimental novels that were fashionable at the time, novels in which every feeling is enormous and every heroine is perfect. Jane was teasing those books, and the people who took them too seriously.

Each little piece was dedicated, with a great flourish, to someone in the family: to a brother, to Cassandra, to a cousin, even to a baby niece. The dedications are full of affectionate nonsense, promising undying gratitude and humble devotion, and they show how much the stories were part of the family's own private jokes.

One of the longest of these early pieces is a story told in letters, called Love and Freindship, with the word friendship spelled in a deliberately wrong way. Jane wrote it when she was about fourteen. In it, two young ladies with exquisitely delicate feelings go through a series of absurd adventures, fainting alternately on a sofa, and robbing their relations without the slightest guilt, because their hearts are so very sensitive. It is a clever parody, and it is still funny today.

Another piece, written when she was fifteen, is called The History of England. It claims to be written by a partial, prejudiced, and ignorant historian, and it covers the kings and queens of England from Henry the Fourth to Charles the First. It is full of cheerful bias. Jane takes the side of Mary, Queen of Scots, at every opportunity, and is very rude about Queen Elizabeth. She admits she has hardly any dates in it. And the little book is illustrated with small round portraits of the monarchs, painted in watercolour by her sister Cassandra. Some of the kings look rather like members of the family. It is the work of two sisters giggling together at a table, and you can almost hear them.

What these notebooks tell us is that the young Jane Austen was not a dreamy, solemn girl writing about her feelings. She was sharp and funny and bold. She was already noticing what was false and silly in the world, and making it laugh at itself. She was already playing with the voices of different characters. And she was already writing for an audience, the best audience she would ever have: her own family, sitting around the fire, waiting to hear what she would come up with next.

As she grew into her later teens, the stories began to change. They grew a little longer, and a little more serious. There was a short novel in letters about a beautiful, clever, entirely unscrupulous widow, Lady Susan, who manipulates everyone around her. Jane probably wrote it when she was about eighteen or nineteen, though she never published it. It shows how well she already understood a certain kind of charm, and the damage it can do.

Imagine, for a moment, the upstairs room that the two sisters shared at Steventon, and the little dressing room beside it. Family memory said that this dressing room had a chocolate-coloured carpet, a piano, and shelves for their books. The window looked out over the garden and the fields. On a summer evening, the light would fall slanting and gold across the table, and a young woman would be bent over a sheet of paper, quill in hand, crossing out a word, trying another, smiling a little to herself.

She was learning her trade. Nobody was paying her, and nobody outside the family had read a word. But the habit was forming, quietly, of sitting down and making something out of nothing but people and talk.

And soon, as she grew up, a new world opened before her: the world of balls and dances, and partners and flirtations, and young men in fine coats. It was a world she would come to write about better than anyone. In the next part of our story, Jane Austen goes to the dance.
