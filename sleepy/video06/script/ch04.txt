## Chapter 4: Dancing Days, and a Young Irishman

In the world Jane Austen grew up in, dancing mattered a great deal. A ball was where young people met, where friendships began, and where, quite often, marriages were first imagined. There were private dances in the big houses of the neighbourhood, where the carpets were rolled back and someone played the piano forte. And there were public balls, called assemblies, held in the assembly rooms of the market town of Basingstoke, about seven miles from Steventon. Once a month in the winter season, the families of the district would climb into carriages, or walk if it was fine, and gather there to dance.

By her late teens, Jane loved these evenings. Her letters to Cassandra, the earliest of which date from her twentieth year, are full of them. She writes about how many dances she danced, and with whom, and who was too shy to ask her, and who stood about looking cross. She describes the gowns and the gossip and the candles. She is clearly having a wonderful time.

Dances in those days were long, lively, and sociable. People stood in two long lines, the gentlemen facing the ladies, and each couple worked its way down the line, turning and stepping and changing places with the others. A single dance might last half an hour, which gave a couple plenty of time to talk. Two dances together with the same partner was a sign of interest. More than that started people whispering. All of this would become part of the delicate machinery of her novels, where so much happens in a ballroom, in a glance across a crowded room, or a refusal to dance.

People described her in different ways. Her nephew, writing many years later from the family's memories, said she was tall and slender, with a light, firm step, a rich, healthy colour in her cheeks, hazel eyes, and brown hair that curled naturally around her face. Her family thought she was very pretty. One older neighbour, many years later, said rather unkindly that she had been the prettiest, silliest, most affected husband-hunting butterfly she ever remembered. But that remark came second hand and long after the fact, and it may tell us more about the neighbour than about Jane. What is certain is that she was lively, that she loved to laugh, and that she noticed everything.

And it was at these dances, in the winter of seventeen ninety-five, when she had just turned twenty, that she met a young man named Tom Lefroy.

He was Irish, the same age as Jane, and he had just finished his studies at Trinity College in Dublin. He was on his way to London to train as a lawyer, and he was spending the Christmas holidays with his uncle and aunt, the Lefroys, who lived in the next parish, at Ashe. Mrs Anne Lefroy, his aunt, was one of Jane's dearest older friends, a warm, cultured woman who loved poetry and encouraged the clever girl from Steventon.

Tom Lefroy and Jane Austen met at three or four dances over that Christmas season. And in her letters to Cassandra, who was away visiting at the time, Jane wrote about him with a teasing, flirtatious sort of delight. She described him as a very gentlemanlike, good-looking, pleasant young man. She joked that they had behaved so shockingly at the dances, sitting down together and dancing together, that she hardly dared tell her sister. She said he had only one fault, which time would entirely remove: his morning coat was a great deal too light. He was, she said, a great admirer of the novel Tom Jones, and she supposed that was why he wore the same coloured clothes as the hero did when he was wounded.

It is impossible to read these letters without smiling. A clever young woman is laughing at herself and at the young man at the same time, and enjoying every minute of it.

But the holiday came to an end. In January of seventeen ninety-six she wrote that the day was coming on which she was to flirt her last with Tom Lefroy, and that when Cassandra received the letter it would be over. She added that her tears were flowing as she wrote, at the melancholy idea. It is hard to tell how much of that is a joke. Perhaps she did not know herself.

Tom Lefroy went away to London, and he did not come back to Steventon. His family, it seems, may have quietly discouraged the friendship. He had no money of his own and a long career to build, and Jane had no fortune to bring to a marriage. These things mattered enormously in that world, as every reader of her novels knows. Tom Lefroy went on to have a long and distinguished life. He married an Irish heiress, had a large family, and eventually became Lord Chief Justice of Ireland. When he was a very old man, he is reported to have told a nephew that he had once been in love with Jane Austen, though he said it was a boyish love. That is the only word we have from his side.

Was it a great romance, or only a happy Christmas flirtation? Nobody can say for certain, and it is probably kinder not to make it larger than it was. But it is pleasant to remember that, for a few weeks, in the candlelight of a country ballroom, Jane Austen was twenty and laughing and very much admired.

Meanwhile, a deeper sorrow was gathering for her sister. Cassandra had become engaged to a young clergyman named Tom Fowle, who had once been one of George Austen's pupils at the rectory. They were devoted to each other, but he did not yet have a parish that paid enough for them to marry. So, in the hope of earning some money, he took a post as chaplain to a regiment and sailed to the West Indies with an expedition, at the end of seventeen ninety-five.

In early seventeen ninety-seven, he died of yellow fever, far away, off the island of Santo Domingo. The news did not reach England until months afterward. He had left Cassandra a legacy of one thousand pounds in his will. She never married. Family memory says that she bore her grief with great composure and quiet dignity, and that from that time on she seemed somehow older and graver than her years. The bond between the two sisters, already close, became the centre of both their lives.

So the dancing days were not without their shadows. But life at Steventon went on, with its seasons and its visitors, and its long evenings by the fire. And in those evenings, in the little dressing room upstairs, Jane had begun to write something much bigger than her teenage stories. In fact, she had begun to write the first versions of three of the most loved novels in the English language. We will look at them, gently, in the next part of our story.
