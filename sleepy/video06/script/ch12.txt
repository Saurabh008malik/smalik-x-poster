## Chapter 12: Sanditon, Winchester, and a Gentle Goodnight

In January of eighteen hundred and seventeen, in spite of her illness, Jane Austen began a new novel. It was set in a little fishing village on the south coast, which an enthusiastic local gentleman, Mr Parker, is trying to turn into a fashionable seaside resort, full of bathing machines and lodging houses and visitors who come for their health. Many of the characters are imaginary invalids, people who love talking about their ailments and their remedies, and the book is full of fresh, funny, sea-bright energy. It feels like the beginning of something new. Today we know it by the name of the village: Sanditon.

She wrote nearly twelve chapters. Then, on the eighteenth of March, eighteen seventeen, she stopped. On the manuscript, at the end of the last page she wrote, she put the date. She never went back to it.

Her illness was growing worse. She was often in pain, and very weak. She spent much of her time lying down, and because there was only one sofa in the sitting room, and she wanted her mother to have it, she lay instead on two or three chairs pushed together. A niece once asked her why she did not use the sofa. She said that if she used it, her mother would not be comfortable lying on it herself. That small story was remembered by the family for its gentleness.

What was the illness? Nobody at the time could say. In the years since, doctors and writers have suggested different possibilities, among them a disease of the adrenal glands called Addison's disease, or a kind of lymphoma, or something else again. The truth is that we do not know, and probably never will. What we do know is that she bore it with remarkable courage and humour.

In April she wrote her will, leaving almost everything to Cassandra. And still her letters were warm and full of jokes, and still she asked after everyone else's health and happiness, and still she wrote cheerful little verses.

In May of eighteen seventeen, the family decided she should go to the city of Winchester, about sixteen miles away, to be near a well-respected doctor there, Mr Lyford. On the twenty-fourth of May, she and Cassandra set off in her brother James's carriage. It rained all the way. Her brother Henry and her nephew William rode beside the carriage on horseback, in the rain, to keep her company. She wrote afterwards that she had been very much distressed to see them getting so wet.

In Winchester, they took lodgings in a house in College Street, a quiet, narrow street beside the walls of the Cathedral Close, and next to Winchester College, the old school. It was a neat house with a bow window, looking out onto the gardens of the college. From her window, Jane could see the trees and the sky.

For a few weeks she seemed a little better. She was able to sit up, and to move from her bed to a sofa, and to write a few letters. In one of them she wrote with love of her brothers and sister, and said that if she lived to be an old woman, she must expect to wish she had died now, blessed in the tenderness of such a family. Even then, she was thinking more about the people she loved than about herself.

On the fifteenth of July, which is Saint Swithin's day, the day on which it is said that if it rains, it will rain for forty days, she dictated a little comic poem about Saint Swithin and the races at Winchester. It was the last thing she composed. She was still finding something to smile about.

Three days later, in the early hours of the eighteenth of July, eighteen hundred and seventeen, Jane Austen died, very peacefully, with her head resting on a pillow in Cassandra's lap. She was forty-one years old.

Cassandra wrote to their niece Fanny a few days later. She said that she had lost a treasure, such a sister, such a friend, as never could have been surpassed. She said that Jane had been the sun of her life, the gilder of every pleasure, the soother of every sorrow. And she said that it was as if she had lost a part of herself.

On the twenty-fourth of July, early in the morning, before the services of the day began, Jane Austen was buried in Winchester Cathedral. In those days women did not usually go to funerals, and so Cassandra watched from the window of the house in College Street as the little procession went slowly along the street, and turned the corner, and passed out of sight. Her brothers Edward, Henry and Frank, and her nephew James Edward, followed the coffin into the great cathedral, and she was laid to rest under the stone floor of the north aisle.

Her gravestone praises the benevolence of her heart, the sweetness of her temper, and the extraordinary endowments of her mind. But it does not say that she wrote novels. Not a word.

That December, her brother Henry arranged for her last two finished novels to be published together. They were Northanger Abbey, the old story of Susan that had once lain in a drawer for thirteen years, and Persuasion. And at the beginning of the first volume, Henry wrote a short biographical notice of his sister. In it, for the first time, the world was told her name. He described her quiet life, her gentle, cheerful nature, her love of family, and her modesty. He said that she had never wished to be known as an author. And so, only after she was gone, the Lady of the title pages became Jane Austen.

Fame grew slowly at first, and then enormously. In eighteen seventy, her nephew James Edward Austen-Leigh published a memoir of his aunt, full of family memories, and her readership blossomed. Visitors began to come to Winchester asking to see the grave of Jane Austen, and the cathedral later added a brass plaque on the wall nearby, which does mention her writing. Today her six novels are read in dozens of languages, they are filmed and studied and loved all over the world, and the little cottage at Chawton is a museum, with her small table still standing by the window.

She once described her own work, in a letter to a nephew, as a little bit of ivory, two inches wide, on which she worked with so fine a brush as to produce little effect after much labour. It was a modest description. But it turned out that on that small piece of ivory she had painted the whole of human nature: pride and kindness, foolishness and wisdom, love and loss, and the long, patient hope of people who wait for life to come right.

And so, as our story comes to a close tonight, perhaps you might let the day settle quietly behind you, the way a page settles when the book is closed. Whatever was unfinished today can wait. There is no need to write another word tonight.

Somewhere, long ago, in a small brick cottage in Hampshire, the candles are being put out one by one. The fire is banked for the night. Upstairs, two sisters share a room, as they always have, talking softly in the dark until their voices slow, and stop. Outside, the road is empty now, and the coaches have gone. A light rain is falling on the garden and the orchard and the quiet fields. And everything is still.
