## Chapter 2: A Rectory Full of Boys, and a Fever Far from Home

The rectory at Steventon was never quiet for long. Besides his own children, George Austen took in pupils, boys from other families who came to live in the house and be taught Latin and Greek and mathematics, so that they could go on to the great schools or to the university. There might be three or four of them at a time. They slept in the rooms upstairs, ate at the family table, and ran about the garden and the fields with the Austen boys. So the house where Jane grew up was really a small boarding school as well as a home.

It meant a great deal of work for Mrs Austen. There were meals to plan for a dozen people, linen to wash, stockings to mend, and the dairy and the poultry and the vegetable garden to keep in order. It also meant that the household was always full of young voices, of lessons being repeated, of jokes and squabbles and games. A little girl growing up in such a house would learn very early how boys talk, how young men show off, how they tease, and how they can be kind. Years later, Jane Austen's novels would be full of brothers, and of young men who are sometimes foolish and sometimes good. She had a whole house of them to study.

George Austen also farmed a little land, as many country clergymen did. There were fields of wheat and barley, and pigs and cows and sheep. In the autumn the harvest was brought in, and in the winter the animals were kept snug in the barns. The children knew the farm workers and their families, and they knew the villagers who came to the church on Sundays. Jane's father visited the sick and the poor of his parish, and baptised the babies, and buried the old. It was a small world, but it was a whole one.

When Jane was about seven, her sister Cassandra was sent away to school. Cassandra was nearly three years older, and Jane could not bear to be parted from her. According to a remark that Mrs Austen made, and that the family remembered afterwards, if Cassandra's head were to be cut off, Jane would insist on sharing her fate. It was a joke, of course, a mother's teasing way of saying that the two girls were inseparable. And so, in the spring of seventeen eighty-three, both sisters went together.

They went first to Oxford, with their cousin Jane Cooper, to be taught by a Mrs Cawley. She was a relation by marriage, the widow of a head of one of the Oxford colleges, and she took a few young girls into her care. By all accounts she was a rather stiff and formal lady. Later that year, Mrs Cawley moved her little group of pupils down to the port of Southampton, on the south coast.

And there, in the late summer and autumn, something went badly wrong. A fever was going round the town. It was a kind of infection that people then called putrid fever, and which historians now think was probably a form of typhus, perhaps brought in by soldiers returning from overseas. The girls caught it. Jane, who was only seven, became very ill indeed.

Mrs Cawley, it seems, did not think it necessary to tell the families. But young Jane Cooper wrote to her mother anyway. Her mother, Mrs Cooper, who was Mrs Austen's sister, set off at once for Southampton with Mrs Austen, and together they brought the girls home. According to family memory, Jane came very close to dying. She recovered, slowly, in the safety of the rectory. But Mrs Cooper had caught the fever herself while nursing the children, and she died of it that October. It was the first great sorrow that the young Austen children knew, and it touched their cousins deeply too.

So the first adventure away from home ended in illness and loss. But the Austens did not give up on schooling for their daughters. In seventeen eighty-five, when Jane was nine, the two sisters went away again, this time to a school in the town of Reading, in Berkshire.

The school was called the Abbey School, because it stood in the old gatehouse of a great medieval abbey that had been mostly pulled down long before. The gatehouse was a tall, picturesque building of grey stone, with a large arch beneath it and rooms above. Around it were the ruins of the abbey and a pleasant garden with tall trees. The school was run by a Mrs La Tournelle, a kindly, elderly lady who, despite her French name, was English, and who was said to have had a cork leg, and to have loved talking about plays and actors much more than about lessons.

From what we know of it, the Abbey School was a relaxed sort of place. The girls had some lessons in the mornings, in writing and spelling and French and needlework, perhaps a little music and dancing. But much of the day was their own. They could wander in the garden and the ruins, and talk, and read. It sounds rather like the kind of school Jane would later describe in her novel Emma, a school where girls might be sent to be out of the way, and to scramble themselves into a little education, without any danger of coming back prodigies.

The sisters stayed at Reading for about a year and a half. Their brothers visited them, and once, it is said, took them out to dinner at an inn. Then, by the end of seventeen eighty-six, they came home to Steventon for good. Jane was not yet eleven years old, and that was the end of her formal schooling.

But in that house, it hardly mattered. Her real education was all around her. Her father's study was full of books, and she was allowed to read almost anything she liked. There were histories and sermons and travel books, and poetry by William Cowper, whom she came to love, and the novels of Samuel Richardson and Fanny Burney, which she read again and again. Her father helped her with her reading. Her brothers James and Henry, who were by then at Oxford, brought home their own ideas and books and jokes. The family read aloud to each other in the evenings, and talked about what they had read, and argued about it, and laughed.

Picture, if you like, a winter evening in the late seventeen eighties. The schoolboys have gone home for the holidays. The family are gathered in the parlour. Mr Austen is reading aloud from a novel, his voice slow and warm, while Mrs Austen mends a shirt by the light of two candles. Cassandra is drawing in a little sketchbook. And Jane, curled up in a chair, is listening very carefully, not only to the story, but to the way it is told, to which parts make everyone laugh, and which parts make them groan.

It was a good place to become a writer. And as we shall see in the next part of our story, that is exactly what she was already beginning to do.
