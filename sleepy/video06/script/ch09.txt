## Chapter 9: Home at Chawton, and a Book by a Lady

In the autumn of eighteen hundred and eight, Edward offered his mother a choice of houses. She could have a cottage near his great house in Kent, or a cottage on his other estate, in the village of Chawton, in Hampshire. Mrs Austen chose Chawton. It was close to the country she knew, and not far from her sons James and Henry. And in July of eighteen hundred and nine, Mrs Austen, Cassandra, Jane and Martha Lloyd arrived to make it their home.

Chawton was a small village about a mile from the market town of Alton, and about fifteen miles from Steventon. The cottage stood right at a road junction in the middle of the village, where the road from London divided, one way going to Winchester and the other to Gosport. It was a solid, red brick house, two storeys high, with a tiled roof and a little garden around it. It had once been an inn, and later the home of Edward's steward. Edward had it repaired and improved for them. A window that looked straight out onto the busy road was blocked up, and a new one was made facing the garden, so that the ladies could have a little more peace.

Coaches passed by the house often, carrying passengers and mail between London and the coast, and on a summer day the dust and noise of the road could come right up to the walls. But behind the house there was a garden with flowers and vegetables, an orchard, and a walk shaded by trees, with fields beyond, and across the road and up the lane stood the big old manor, Chawton House, and the little church of Saint Nicholas.

Jane was thirty-three when she came to Chawton. And very soon after she arrived, she wrote a little poem to her brother Frank, to celebrate the birth of his son. In it, she described their new home with delight. She wrote of how comfortable their Chawton home was, and how, when it was finished, with all its rooms in order, it would beat all other houses. For the first time in eight years, she had a settled home in the country. And she began, almost at once, to write again.

Life at Chawton had a gentle, regular shape. Jane got up early, and before breakfast she practised on the piano forte. A niece remembered that she played in the mornings, perhaps so as not to disturb anyone later in the day. Then she made the breakfast for the household. That was her particular job. She was also in charge of the stores of tea, sugar, and wine, which were kept locked up, because they were so expensive. Cassandra and Martha took on most of the other housekeeping, and Mrs Austen, now in her seventies, spent long hours in the garden, digging potatoes and tending her flowers in a green round frock like a labourer's smock.

This arrangement left Jane free to write. And she wrote in the sitting room, at a small, many-sided walnut table near the window, hardly bigger than a tea tray. According to her nephew's memoir, she had no study of her own, and she wrote on small sheets of paper which could easily be put away or covered with a piece of blotting paper if a visitor came in. There was a door between the front hall and the sitting room that creaked when it opened. The family offered to fix it, the story goes, but she asked them not to, because the creak gave her a warning that someone was coming. Whether every detail of that story is true, we cannot say. But the little table is still there in the house today, and you can stand beside it and look out of the same window.

At that small table, through the years eighteen hundred and nine and eighteen hundred and ten, she went back to her old novel, Sense and Sensibility, and revised it carefully once more. And this time, with the help of her brother Henry, she found a publisher.

Henry was now living in London. He had been a soldier, and then a banker, and he had a wide circle of friends and a great deal of energy. He was Jane's most enthusiastic supporter in the family, and he acted for her with the London booksellers. Through him, the manuscript went to a publisher named Thomas Egerton, of the Military Library in Whitehall.

The arrangement was what was called publishing on commission. Egerton would print the book and sell it, but Jane, through Henry, would bear the risk. If the book did not sell, she would have to pay the costs. It is said that she was so sure it would lose money that she put aside a sum from her own small savings to cover the loss.

In the spring of eighteen hundred and eleven, Jane went up to London to stay with Henry and his wife Eliza, and to correct the proofs. She wrote to Cassandra that she was never too busy to think of the book, and that she could no more forget it than a mother could forget her sucking child. It is a lovely image. After so many years, so many disappointments, the book was finally real.

At the end of October, eighteen hundred and eleven, Sense and Sensibility was published in three volumes. Her name was not on it. The title page said simply that it was By a Lady. In those days many writers published without their names, and for a clergyman's daughter it would have seemed proper and modest. Jane also seems to have wanted privacy. She did not want to be talked about.

The book was a quiet success. It was reviewed in a couple of magazines, kindly, as a pleasant and well written story with a good moral. People in fashionable society talked about it, and guessed at who the Lady could be. A young royal princess, Princess Charlotte, read it and said that she felt she was very like the impetuous Marianne. By the summer of eighteen thirteen, the whole first edition had sold out. Jane earned about one hundred and forty pounds from it. It was the first money she had ever really earned in her life, and she was proud of it. Not long afterwards, adding it to the money from her next book, she told her brother Frank that she had now written herself into two hundred and fifty pounds, which only made her long for more.

Picture, if you will, the little sitting room at Chawton on an autumn afternoon in eighteen eleven. The fire is lit. Mrs Austen is dozing in a chair. Cassandra is sewing. Through the window you can see the red leaves on the trees and the road beyond, where a coach is rattling by. And at the little round table, a woman of thirty-five sits with a new book in her hands, turning the pages slowly, the ink and paper smell still fresh. It does not have her name on it. But it is hers.

And, waiting in her drawer, there was another story, about five sisters and a proud gentleman. In the next part of our story, that story goes out into the world.
