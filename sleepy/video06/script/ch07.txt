## Chapter 7: A Proposal at Manydown, and a Winter of Loss

Not far from Steventon, in the Hampshire countryside, there was a large old house called Manydown Park. It belonged to the Bigg-Wither family, and the daughters of the house, Elizabeth, Catherine and Alethea Bigg, were among Jane and Cassandra's closest friends. The Austen sisters had often stayed there, especially when there was a ball in Basingstoke, because it was more comfortable to sleep at Manydown than to make the long, cold journey home in the dark.

In the late autumn of eighteen hundred and two, Jane and Cassandra went back to Hampshire on a long visit, and came to stay at Manydown. The family's only son, Harris Bigg-Wither, was at home. He was about twenty-one, nearly six years younger than Jane. He was the heir to the house and the estate, and would one day be a wealthy man. Family memory describes him as rather large and plain, awkward in company, and with a bit of a stammer, though a decent young man.

On the evening of the second of December, he asked Jane to marry him. And she said yes.

It is not hard to see why. A marriage to Harris Bigg-Wither would have given her a great deal. She would have become the mistress of a large house in the countryside she loved, only a few miles from Steventon. She would have had a secure home for the rest of her life, and the means to help her parents and her sister. Her friends, his sisters, would have become her family. In the world of her novels, many a sensible young woman accepts a sensible offer for exactly such reasons.

But during the night, she changed her mind. Perhaps she lay awake in her room at Manydown, listening to the wind in the trees, and thinking about the long years ahead. Perhaps she realised that she could not marry a man she did not love, however kind and however rich. In the morning, she went to him and withdrew her acceptance. Then she and Cassandra, deeply upset, left the house at once and asked their brother James to take them back to Bath.

It must have been a painful, embarrassing morning for everyone. And yet, in time, the friendship with the Bigg sisters recovered, and Jane went on visiting them for the rest of her life. Harris Bigg-Wither married someone else a few years later and had a large family.

Many years afterwards, Jane gave some advice to a niece who was unsure about a young man. She wrote that anything is to be preferred or endured rather than marrying without affection. It is a sentence that sounds very much like a lesson learned from her own life.

There is another story from these years, told by Cassandra to a niece much later. It is said that, during one of the seaside holidays in Devon, Jane met a young man, perhaps a clergyman, who seemed to be truly fond of her and whom she liked very much. He was expected to meet the family again. But before that could happen, news came that he had died. We do not know his name, or exactly when or where they met, and the story reaches us only at second or third hand. It may have been important to her, or it may have grown in the retelling. But it is a tender thing to hold in mind, gently, as one possible shadow in a quiet life.

In those Bath years, Jane also began another novel. It was about a young woman named Emma Watson, who returns to her own family after being raised by a wealthy aunt, and finds herself poor, among sisters who are desperate to marry. She wrote several chapters of it, perhaps in eighteen hundred and four. The writing is sharp and sad and very promising. But she put it aside, unfinished, and never went back to it. Today we know it as The Watsons.

Why did she stop? Nobody knows for sure. But some people have noticed that the story she was writing, about a family made poor by a father's death, came very close to what happened next.

By the winter of eighteen hundred and five, the family had moved to a house in Green Park Buildings, a row of houses near the river in the lower part of Bath. George Austen was now seventy-three. On the nineteenth of January, he fell ill with a sudden fever. He seemed at first to rally a little. But on the morning of Monday the twenty-first of January, he died peacefully, with his wife and daughters beside him.

Jane wrote to her brother Frank, who was away at sea, to tell him the news. In her letter she described her father's last illness, and how peacefully he had gone. And she wrote one short, simple line about him: His tenderness as a father, who can do justice to? These are among the most moving lines she ever wrote, and they are not in a novel at all, only in a letter to a brother on a ship.

He was buried in the church of Saint Swithin, at Walcot, in Bath, the same church where he and Mrs Austen had been married some forty years before.

George Austen had been the warm heart of the family. He had taught Jane to read and encouraged her to write. He had given her paper and, the family believed, a little portable writing desk, and he had tried to find a publisher for her first novel. Now he was gone. And his death did not only bring grief. It brought a very practical difficulty. A clergyman's income stopped when he died. Mrs Austen and her two unmarried daughters were left with very little money of their own.

Her sons came to the rescue. James, Edward, Henry, and Frank each agreed to give their mother a yearly sum, so that she, Cassandra and Jane would have enough to live on. Cassandra had her small legacy from Tom Fowle. Jane had nothing of her own at all. From now on, she was dependent on her brothers' kindness. It was, in her world, the usual fate of a single woman without a fortune, and it is something she understood with great clarity when, later, she wrote about the Dashwood sisters, and the Bennet sisters, and Miss Bates.

Picture, if you like, the little house in Bath on a grey January evening, a few days after the funeral. The fire is low. The candles are lit. Three women in black dresses are sitting together quietly in the parlour, writing letters, or sewing, or simply sitting. Outside, the river is running high and dark under the bridges. The town goes on with its card parties and concerts. But in this one room, everything has changed.

For a while, the three of them drifted, without a settled home. In the next part of our story, we will follow them through these wandering years, and on to a busy port town by the sea, where Jane's sailor brothers come and go.
