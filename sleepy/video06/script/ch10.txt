## Chapter 10: Her Own Darling Child

While Sense and Sensibility was being printed and read, Jane was already at work on her next book. She took out her old manuscript of First Impressions, the story she had written at Steventon when she was just twenty-one, and began to revise it. She cut it down, sharpened it, and changed its title, because another novel called First Impressions had appeared in the meantime. The new title was Pride and Prejudice.

This time, the publisher Thomas Egerton bought the copyright outright, for one hundred and ten pounds. Jane had hoped for a little more, but it was a firm sum, with no risk. And on the twenty-eighth of January, eighteen hundred and thirteen, Pride and Prejudice was published, once again without her name. The title page said it was by the author of Sense and Sensibility.

The next day, her copy arrived at Chawton, sent down from London by Henry. Jane wrote to Cassandra, who was away, in a mood of pure joy. She wrote that she had got her own darling child from London. That is how she thought of the book, as a beloved child.

That evening, a neighbour, Miss Benn, came to dine at the cottage. Jane and her mother did not tell her who had written the book. Instead, they said they had been sent a new novel from London, and Mrs Austen read the first volume aloud to the company that evening, while Miss Benn listened, quite unaware that the author was sitting across the table. Jane wrote that Miss Benn seemed to be really amused, and that she herself thought Elizabeth as delightful a creature as ever appeared in print. She added, with a touch of mischief, that she did not know how she would be able to tolerate those who did not like Elizabeth at least.

She did worry a little about how her mother read. She told Cassandra that Mrs Austen read too quickly, and that, although she perfectly understood the characters herself, she could not speak as they ought. Anyone who has ever heard their own words read aloud by someone else will know exactly how she felt.

She also wrote, in a mood of playful self criticism, that the book was rather too light, and bright, and sparkling. It wanted shade, she said. Perhaps a long chapter of sense, or a solemn piece of nonsense about something unconnected with the story, to give a contrast. She did not mean it, of course. She was laughing at the heavy, preachy novels of her day, and at herself.

Pride and Prejudice was a success from the start. The reviews were warm. People everywhere talked about Elizabeth Bennet and Mr Darcy. The first edition sold out within months, and a second edition followed that autumn. The playwright Richard Brinsley Sheridan is reported to have said it was one of the cleverest things he had ever read. And all the while, the author was making breakfast and practising the piano at Chawton, and almost nobody outside the family knew who she was.

That secret did not last long. Henry was so proud of his sister that he could not stop himself from telling people. In the autumn of eighteen thirteen, Jane wrote, half amused and half resigned, that the secret had spread so far that it was scarcely the shadow of a secret now. She had decided not to be too troubled by it.

Life went on, with its small sorrows and pleasures. Henry's wife, the lively cousin Eliza, had died in the spring of eighteen thirteen after a long illness, and Jane had helped to look after Henry in his grief. That autumn she stayed for a long while at Edward's house, Godmersham, in Kent, where she enjoyed the library, the quiet, the comforts, and the company of her niece Fanny, Edward's eldest daughter, who was clever and warm and became one of her favourites.

Meanwhile she was writing again. Her next novel was different from the others. It was quieter and more serious. It was about a shy, poor girl named Fanny Price, who is brought up in the grand house of her rich relations, the Bertrams, at Mansfield Park, and who is overlooked and undervalued by almost everyone. It is a book about conscience, and about the difference between charm and goodness. It also mentions, briefly, that Sir Thomas Bertram's wealth comes from plantations in Antigua, in the West Indies, and so it touches, quietly, on the great wrong of slavery, which was being fiercely debated in Britain at that time. Jane had read writers who opposed the slave trade, and her novel lets that shadow fall across the fine rooms of the great house.

Mansfield Park was published in May of eighteen hundred and fourteen. The reviewers mostly ignored it, but the public did not. The first edition sold out in about six months, and it earned her more money than any of her other books in her lifetime.

She liked to collect what people said about her novels. She kept a list of the opinions of her friends and family about Mansfield Park, and later about Emma, writing them down exactly as she heard them. Some liked Fanny best. Some preferred Elizabeth. Her mother thought Fanny insipid. It is a funny, honest little document, and it shows how much she loved to hear what real readers thought.

By now, she was an aunt many times over. Her nieces and nephews were growing up, and some of them had begun to write stories of their own. She wrote them letters full of good advice. To her niece Anna, who was writing a novel, she said that three or four families in a country village is the very thing to work on. It is perhaps the best description of her own art ever written. She told Anna to keep her characters in places she knew, and not to send them off to countries she had never seen. And she teased her gently, and encouraged her, and praised what was good.

Imagine her on a spring morning in the garden at Chawton, a letter to a niece in her pocket, walking slowly along the path under the trees. The birds are singing. Her mother is digging in the vegetable beds in her old green frock. A coach goes by on the road, and somewhere on that coach, perhaps, a stranger is reading Pride and Prejudice, and laughing out loud, with no idea that the author is standing in a cottage garden a few yards away, with earth on her shoes.

She was thirty-eight. She had published three novels, and she was already writing a fourth. In the next part of our story, she will be invited to a royal palace, and a very different kind of reader will take an interest in her.
