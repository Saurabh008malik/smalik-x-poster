## Chapter 5: First Impressions, Returned Unopened

In the middle of the seventeen nineties, in her early twenties, Jane Austen began to write the books that would make her name. She did not know that, of course. She was simply writing, as she always had, for herself and her family. But the pieces were growing longer, and deeper, and the characters in them were beginning to feel like real people.

The first of these longer works was a story told in letters, as so many novels were in those days. It was about two sisters, one calm and sensible, the other passionate and romantic, and about what happens to them when they fall in love and are disappointed. In this early version, the story was called Elinor and Marianne, after the two sisters. Family tradition says that Jane read it aloud to her family around the year seventeen ninety-five. Some years later she would take it apart and rebuild it completely, as an ordinary narrative rather than a set of letters, and give it a new title. But that is a story for later.

Then, in October of seventeen ninety-six, when she was twenty, she began something new. She worked on it through the winter and the following summer, and by August of seventeen ninety-seven it was finished. She called it First Impressions.

It was the story of a family of five daughters, the Bennets, in a country village, with a silly, anxious mother and a clever, ironic father, and of the second daughter, Elizabeth, who is lively and quick and sure of her own judgement, and of a proud, reserved, wealthy gentleman named Mr Darcy, whom she dislikes at first sight. It was, in other words, the first version of the novel that the world now knows as Pride and Prejudice.

We do not have that first version. It is lost, and we can only guess at how it differed from the book that was finally published many years later. But we know the family loved it. They read it again and again. Cassandra wanted to read it again and again. And their dear friend Martha Lloyd loved it so much that, a year or two later, Jane wrote teasingly that she would not let Martha read it again on any account, because she suspected Martha meant to publish it from memory.

George Austen believed in his daughter's gifts. On the first of November, seventeen ninety-seven, he wrote a letter to a well-known London publisher, Thomas Cadell. He said that he had in his possession a manuscript novel, in three volumes, about the length of Fanny Burney's novel Evelina. He asked what it would cost to publish it at the author's own risk, and how much the publisher might offer for it if it were approved.

He did not mention the author's name, or that she was a young woman. And the answer came back by return of post. It was a simple refusal. The publisher declined the offer without even asking to see the manuscript. Someone wrote on the letter, it seems, the words declined by return of post.

It is easy to imagine the disappointment in the rectory when that answer came. And yet, there is no sign that Jane stopped writing for a moment. Within a few months, she was at work on her old sisters' story again, turning Elinor and Marianne from a novel in letters into a straightforward narrative, and calling it by a new and balanced title: Sense and Sensibility.

And then, around seventeen ninety-eight and seventeen ninety-nine, she wrote a third novel. This one was perhaps the lightest and most playful of all. It was about a young girl named Catherine, a clergyman's daughter from a quiet country parish, who goes to the fashionable town of Bath and later stays at an old abbey. Catherine has read a great many thrilling Gothic novels, full of haunted castles and secret passages and terrible crimes, and she half expects real life to be just like them. Of course, it is not. The abbey is comfortable and modern, the mysterious chest contains nothing but linen, and the real villains turn out to be ordinary, greedy, selfish people. Jane called this book Susan, after the heroine's first name at that time. Many years later it would become Northanger Abbey.

In it she does something rather brave for a young writer. She stops the story to defend novels themselves, and the women who wrote them. In her day, many serious people looked down on novel reading as silly and even harmful, especially for young ladies. Jane stands up for the novel as a form in which the greatest powers of the mind are displayed, and the most thorough knowledge of human nature. She was, you might say, defending her own vocation before anyone had seen her work at all.

So by the time she was twenty-four, Jane Austen had written full drafts of three novels: Sense and Sensibility, First Impressions, and Susan. She had been turned down by a publisher without being read. And she was living a quiet country life in which nothing much seemed to happen.

Some years later, in the spring of eighteen hundred and three, when the family had moved away from Steventon, there was a small success. Her brother Henry, acting for her through a lawyer, sold the manuscript of Susan to a London publisher named Crosby, for ten pounds. The publisher even advertised it as forthcoming. Ten pounds was not a fortune, but it was real money for a story, and for a moment it must have seemed that her first book was about to appear in the world.

It did not. For reasons nobody quite knows, Crosby simply put the manuscript away, and never printed it. Months passed, and then years. The book stayed in a drawer in London, and Jane, far away, could do nothing about it. We will come back to that drawer later in our story.

There is a lesson in these years that is worth holding gently. Nobody outside her family thought she was special. The great publishers did not want her. Her best work was sitting unread in a stranger's office. And yet she kept going, quietly, writing and rewriting, for the simple reason that this was what she did, and what she loved.

Imagine her, if you like, on a still autumn afternoon at Steventon, walking along the hedgerow path to the church, her bonnet tied under her chin, her hands in a muff against the cold. The leaves are drifting down from the elms. Rooks are calling in the high branches. She is thinking about a character, about a line of conversation, about how to make a proud man change his mind. Nobody in the world knows her name. And she is perfectly content, for now, to let the story grow in its own time.

But a great change was coming to that quiet life. In the next part of our story, the Austens leave Steventon, and Jane goes to live in the town of Bath.
