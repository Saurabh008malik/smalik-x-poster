## Chapter 11: Emma, a Royal Library, and Persuasion

In January of eighteen hundred and fourteen, Jane began a new novel. She is said to have told her family that she was going to take a heroine whom no one but herself would much like. The heroine was Emma Woodhouse: handsome, clever, and rich, as the first line of the book says, the queen of her little village of Highbury, and so sure of her own good judgement that she spends most of the story getting everything wrong. She tries to arrange marriages for other people, misreads every sign, and hurts a poor, talkative spinster, Miss Bates, with one careless joke. And slowly, painfully, and with a great deal of comedy, she learns to see herself more clearly.

Emma is often thought of as Jane Austen's most perfect book. It is a novel in which almost nothing dramatic happens. There is a picnic, a ball, a strawberry party, a visit to a poor family, a great deal of gossip, and a few misunderstandings. And yet it holds a whole village in it, so alive and so funny and so true that readers have been returning to Highbury for two hundred years.

She worked on it through eighteen fourteen and into eighteen fifteen. And in the autumn of eighteen fifteen she went up to London to stay with Henry, who was now living at Hans Place, in Chelsea, to arrange its publication. This time she had a new and more important publisher, John Murray, who also published the poet Lord Byron.

While she was there, Henry fell seriously ill with a fever. For some days, the family feared he might die. Jane nursed him, and wrote anxious letters home, and sent for her brothers and for Cassandra. One of the doctors who came to see Henry happened also to attend the Prince Regent, the king's eldest son, who was then ruling the country in place of his father, King George the Third. And this doctor let it be known that the Prince was a great admirer of her novels, and kept a set of them in each of his houses.

Soon afterwards, Jane received an invitation. The Prince's librarian, a clergyman named James Stanier Clarke, invited her to visit Carlton House, the Prince's magnificent palace in London, and to see the library there. She went, in November of eighteen fifteen. We do not know much about the visit, except that Mr Clarke showed her round the splendid rooms, and that he told her she was permitted to dedicate her next novel to the Prince, if she wished.

Jane did not much admire the Prince Regent. His private life was famous for its extravagance and its scandals, and she had once written, about his unhappy wife, that she would support her as long as she could, because she was a woman, and because she hated her husband. But a permission from a prince was, in effect, a command. So Emma, when it was published, carried a formal dedication to His Royal Highness the Prince Regent.

The librarian, Mr Clarke, then began to write to her with suggestions. He thought she might like to write a novel about a clergyman, perhaps someone rather like himself, who loved literature and went to sea. Later, he suggested that she might write a historical romance about the royal house of Saxe-Coburg. Jane answered him with perfect politeness, and a very straight face. She said she could no more write a romance than an epic poem, and that she could not sit seriously down to write one under any other motive than to save her life. She must keep to her own style, she said, and go on in her own way. Then, privately, for her family's amusement, she wrote a wonderfully silly outline called Plan of a Novel, according to hints from various quarters, in which a perfect heroine and her clergyman father are chased all over Europe by villains. It is the young girl of the Steventon notebooks, laughing again.

Emma was published in December of eighteen hundred and fifteen. It was reviewed at length in an important journal, the Quarterly Review, by an anonymous writer who praised her truthful pictures of ordinary life. The writer was the most famous novelist in Britain, Walter Scott, though she probably did not know it. Some years later, Scott wrote in his private journal that she had a talent for describing the feelings and characters of ordinary life which was the most wonderful he had ever met with.

But eighteen sixteen brought troubles. In March, Henry's bank failed. It was a time of economic difficulty after the long wars with France had ended, and many banks collapsed. Henry lost everything, and several members of the family, including Edward and her uncle Mr Leigh-Perrot, lost large sums too. Jane herself lost a little of her savings. Henry, with typical resilience, took holy orders and became a clergyman, like his father.

That year, too, she was able at last to get back her old novel, Susan. Through Henry, she paid the ten pounds to the publisher Crosby and recovered the manuscript. According to a family story, once the deal was done, Henry had the pleasure of telling the publisher that the book he had let lie in a drawer for thirteen years was by the author of Pride and Prejudice. Jane began to revise it, and renamed the heroine Catherine. But she wrote a short note to go with it, apologising that some parts of the book might seem old-fashioned, because so many years had passed.

And she was writing something new. Since the summer of eighteen fifteen she had been working on a novel about a woman named Anne Elliot. Anne is twenty-seven, quiet, gentle, overlooked by her vain father and sisters. Eight years before the story begins, she was persuaded to break off her engagement to a young naval officer, Frederick Wentworth, because he had no money and no prospects. Now he returns, a successful captain, and she must meet him again. The book is called Persuasion. It is autumnal, tender, and full of the sea. It has naval officers who are generous and brave, and it has Lyme Regis, and the steps of the Cobb, and Bath again, seen through Anne's patient eyes.

She finished a draft in July of eighteen sixteen. But she was not happy with the ending. So she rewrote the last chapters, and in the new version Captain Wentworth writes Anne a letter, which is perhaps the most loved letter in all of English fiction. Those cancelled chapters, the only part of any of her published novels to survive in her own handwriting, are kept today in the British Library.

During that same year, her family began to notice that she was not well. She was often tired. She had pains in her back. Her face seemed to change colour. She rested more and walked less, and in time she would go out in a little carriage drawn by a donkey instead of on foot. She wrote about it lightly, as she wrote about almost everything. But the people who loved her were becoming anxious.

Imagine her in the late summer of eighteen sixteen, sitting at the little table in Chawton, with the window open and the sound of bees in the garden. A sheet of paper is in front of her. She is writing a letter from a captain to the woman he has loved for eight years. Her hand is a little slower than it used to be. But the words are coming.

In the last part of our story, we will follow her through her final months, and back to a quiet room in the old city of Winchester.
