## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Winter Birth at Steventon

Welcome. Let us settle in together. If you can, dim the lights a little, and let your shoulders soften. There is nothing you need to do tonight. You do not have to remember any names, and you do not have to keep track of any dates. You can close your eyes whenever you like, and the story will carry on quietly beside you, like a candle left burning on a table in the next room. We will go slowly. We will not hurry anywhere.

Tonight we will keep company with Jane Austen. She wrote six novels, and she wrote them in small rooms, on small sheets of paper, in the middle of an ordinary family life full of brothers and nieces and visitors and tea. She never married. She never travelled abroad. In her lifetime, not one of her books carried her name on its cover. And yet, more than two hundred years later, people all over the world still read her stories in bed at night, and laugh at her jokes, and feel that she somehow understood them.

So this is the story of a quiet life that turned out to hold a great deal. It is a gentle story, mostly, full of country lanes and parsonage gardens, of letters and dances and long walks in the rain. There are some sad parts, too, because her life was not a long one. We will try to tell it honestly. Much of what we know comes from her own letters, and from what her family wrote down after she was gone. Where a story comes only from family memory, we will say so.

Let us begin in the deep of winter, in the year seventeen hundred and seventy-five, in a small village in the county of Hampshire, in the south of England.

The village was called Steventon. It was not much of a village, even then. There were a few cottages along a lane, a handful of farms, and a little stone church of Saint Nicholas standing a short way off on a gentle rise, with an old yew tree beside it. The country all around was soft and rolling, with chalky fields, thick hedgerows, and narrow lanes that turned to deep mud in wet weather. In winter the lanes could be so bad that people walked along the raised paths at the edges of the fields instead.

At the bottom of the village stood the rectory, the house where the clergyman lived. It was a plain, comfortable house of brick, with a garden behind it, a sloping grass walk, and a row of elm trees. There was a barn, and a dairy, and fields where the family kept cows and pigs and grew some of their own food. This was the home of the Reverend George Austen, the rector of Steventon, and of his wife, whose name was Cassandra.

George Austen was a scholarly, gentle, good-humoured man. He had been an orphan as a boy, and had made his own way through school and on to the University of Oxford, where he was known as a handsome and able young man. He loved books, and he would one day keep several hundred of them in his study, which was a good many for a country parson. His wife Cassandra came from a family with some grand relations, the Leighs, but she herself was practical, quick, and funny. She wrote cheerful little verses, she managed a busy household, and she was not afraid of hard work in the garden or the dairy.

By the winter of seventeen seventy-five they had already been married for more than ten years, and the rectory was full of children. There was James, the eldest, a bookish boy who liked to write poems. There was George, who was a gentle child but did not develop as the others did, and who was cared for by a family in a nearby village for all his long life, as was sometimes the custom then. There was Edward, easy-going and kind. There was Henry, lively and charming and full of plans. There was a daughter, Cassandra, named after her mother. And there was Francis, a small, determined boy whom everyone called Frank.

That December, Mrs Austen was expecting another baby. By the family's own reckoning, the baby was late, and they had been waiting for weeks. And then, on the sixteenth of December, seventeen seventy-five, the baby arrived at last. It was a little girl.

George Austen wrote the news to a relation the next day. In his letter he said that they had now another girl, a present plaything for her sister Cassy, and a future companion. They were going to call her Jenny, he said. And it is a lovely thing, when you think about it, that the very first written words about Jane Austen describe her as a companion for her sister. Because that is exactly what she became.

The winter that followed was a hard one. There was deep snow and bitter frost, and the baby was not christened in the church until April, when the weather had turned. Her full name in the parish book was simply Jane. She had no middle name, and she would never need one.

The Austens had a custom with their babies that may seem strange to us now. When each child was a few months old, it was taken to live with a trusted woman in the village, who looked after it during the day, perhaps until it was walking and talking. The parents visited often, and the children were brought up to the rectory, but they spent much of their earliest life in a cottage nearby. It was a practical arrangement for a busy household, and it was not unusual among country families of that time. So little Jane, too, spent her first year or so in a cottage in Steventon, in the care of a village woman whose name has not come down to us.

Two and a half years after Jane, one more child was born, the last of the family. He was called Charles, and he was a sweet-natured little boy who would one day, like Frank, go to sea. So there were eight children in all, six boys and two girls, and Jane was the seventh.

Imagine, if you like, the rectory on a winter evening in those years. The candles are lit early, because the dark comes down by four o'clock. There is a fire in the parlour, and the smell of woodsmoke and baking bread. Somewhere upstairs, boys are reciting Latin. Somewhere downstairs, a kettle is beginning to sing. And in a corner, two little girls, one dark-eyed and serious, one small and bright, are sitting close together on a rug, sharing a picture book by the firelight.

That picture is imagined, of course. We cannot see into those rooms. But the facts of that house are well known: it was crowded, it was clever, it was full of books and talk and laughter, and it was the place where Jane Austen learned almost everything she would ever need to know about people.

And so, as the snow lies quietly over the Hampshire fields, and the rectory windows glow in the long December night, let us leave the baby sleeping for now. In the next part of our story, we will watch her grow up in that busy house, among the schoolboys and the brothers and the cows, and we will follow her on a journey away from home that very nearly ended her story before it had begun.
