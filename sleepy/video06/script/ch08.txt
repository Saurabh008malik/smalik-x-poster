## Chapter 8: Wandering Years, Sailor Brothers, and a Letter Signed M.A.D.

After George Austen died, the three Austen women stayed on in Bath for a while, moving from one set of rented rooms to another, each a little smaller and cheaper than the last. They lived for a time in Gay Street, and then in Trim Street, which was not a fashionable address at all. Their dear friend Martha Lloyd, whose own mother had just died, came to live with them. Martha was cheerful, practical, and kind, and she would stay with the Austens for many years, almost a third sister.

In the summer of eighteen hundred and six, they left Bath for good. Jane wrote later of the happy feelings of escape with which they had left it. They went visiting for a while: to Clifton, near Bristol, and then to relations in the Midlands, to Adlestrop in Gloucestershire, and to a great old house called Stoneleigh Abbey, in Warwickshire, which had just been inherited by a cousin of Mrs Austen. Stoneleigh was vast, with endless rooms and long corridors and a chapel, and Mrs Austen wrote a long, amused letter describing its many windows and its enormous meals. Some readers have thought that a little of Stoneleigh found its way, years later, into the grand house in Mansfield Park.

Then, in the autumn of eighteen hundred and six, they went to live in Southampton, on the south coast.

The reason was Frank. Jane's brother Francis had been in the navy since he was a boy of twelve. He was now a captain, serious, devout, brave, and very able, and he had just married a young woman named Mary Gibson. Because he was so often at sea, it made sense for his wife to live with his mother and sisters. So they all set up house together in Southampton, which was close to the great naval town of Portsmouth.

The navy was a large part of the Austens' lives in those years. Both Frank and the youngest brother, Charles, were naval officers, and Britain was at war with France almost without a break. Frank had missed the great battle of Trafalgar, in October of eighteen hundred and five, by a matter of days, because his ship had been sent away for supplies, and he was bitterly disappointed. But in the following year he fought in a victory in the West Indies, at the battle of San Domingo. Charles was serving far away, in the waters off North America and Bermuda, where he married the daughter of the attorney general of Bermuda.

Jane loved her sailor brothers. She wrote them long letters full of family news, and she followed the movements of their ships with anxious attention. She knew what it meant to wait for news of a battle. She knew the language of the navy, its ranks and its prize money and its long separations. And in two of her later novels, Mansfield Park and Persuasion, sailors play a warm and important part. In Persuasion especially, the naval officers are among the most generous, open-hearted people in the book, and it is hard not to think she was thinking of Frank and Charles.

In Southampton, after some months in lodgings, the family took a house in Castle Square. It was an old house with a large garden that ran down to the city walls, and from the top of the walls you could look out over the water. Jane loved that garden. She wrote to Cassandra about the gooseberry bushes and currants and raspberries they were planting, and the syringa and laburnum she hoped to have. She wrote about walks along the shore, and about the boats on the river, and about a little trip by water up to see the ruins of Netley Abbey, among the trees.

It was a busier and happier household than Bath had been. There were babies now, as Frank and Mary's children began to arrive, and visits from nieces and nephews. And there was a sorrow, too, in the autumn of eighteen hundred and eight, when news came from Kent that Edward's wife, Elizabeth, had died, very suddenly, soon after the birth of her eleventh child. Edward's two eldest boys, who were at school in Winchester, came to stay with their aunts in Southampton, and Jane looked after them tenderly. She played games with them, and took them out in a boat, and let them be as quiet or as noisy as they needed.

Edward himself is an interesting part of this story. When he was a boy, a wealthy childless couple who were relations of George Austen, Mr and Mrs Thomas Knight, had grown fond of him and adopted him as their heir. So Edward, the Austen boy, grew up to inherit large estates: a fine house called Godmersham Park, in Kent, and another estate at Chawton, in Hampshire. Eventually he took the Knight family name. He was a rich man now, but he never forgot his mother and sisters, and Jane and Cassandra often went to stay at Godmersham, with its park and its library and its quiet, beautiful rooms.

And through all these wandering years, something was nagging at Jane. Her novel Susan was still sitting in the London office of Crosby and Company, where it had been for six years, unpublished.

In April of eighteen hundred and nine, she decided to do something about it. She wrote to the publishers. She signed her letter with a made-up name: Mrs Ashton Dennis. And she wrote the initials of that name, M, A, D, so that her signature spelled the word MAD. It was a joke, and perhaps also a quiet little flash of real annoyance.

In her letter she reminded them politely that they had bought the manuscript in the spring of eighteen hundred and three and promised early publication. She suggested that it must have been lost, and offered to send another copy. If they did not want it, she said, she would look for another publisher.

The reply was cool. Mr Richard Crosby wrote back that there had never been any promise of publication at any fixed time. He said that if she tried to publish it elsewhere, he would take action to stop her. But she could have the manuscript back, he said, for the same ten pounds they had paid for it.

Ten pounds was a great deal of money to Jane at that time. She did not have it. And so, for now, the book stayed where it was.

Imagine her on a spring evening in Southampton, after that letter came, walking slowly in the garden by the old city walls. The tide is coming in. Ships are moving quietly down the water towards the sea. She is thirty-three years old. She has three novels finished and no way to get any of them printed. It would be easy to give up.

But something good was about to happen. In the next part of our story, Edward makes his mother and sisters an offer, and Jane comes home at last to the Hampshire countryside, to a small brick cottage in the village of Chawton.
