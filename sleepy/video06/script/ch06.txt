## Chapter 6: The White Glare of Bath

In December of the year eighteen hundred, Jane and Cassandra were away from home, staying with friends. When Jane came back to Steventon, she learned some startling news. Her father, who was now nearly seventy, had decided to retire. He would hand over the parish to his eldest son, James, who was also a clergyman. And the rest of the family, George and Mrs Austen and their two daughters, would leave the rectory and go to live in the town of Bath.

According to a story told in the family many years later, when Jane heard this news, she fainted. We cannot know whether that is exactly true, and family stories have a way of growing in the telling. But we do know that the decision was made quickly, and that it meant leaving the only home she had ever known: the house she was born in, the garden, the elms, the church on the rise, the lanes, the neighbours, the dances at Basingstoke, and the little dressing room where she had written three novels.

And it was not only the house. Things had to be sold. Her father's library of several hundred books was sold. Her own piano was sold. Furniture and pictures and the farm animals were sold. Her letters from those weeks are brisk and joking, as they so often were, but there are moments when you can feel the strain beneath the jokes. She writes about prices and arrangements, and about who is getting what, in a tone that is light, but perhaps a little too light.

In May of eighteen hundred and one, they arrived in Bath.

Bath was one of the most fashionable towns in England. It stood in a green valley in the west of the country, on the river Avon, and it was famous for its warm springs. For centuries people had come to bathe in the hot mineral waters and to drink them, in the hope of curing their aches and illnesses. By Jane's time, the town had been almost completely rebuilt in pale golden stone, with elegant terraces and crescents climbing up the hillsides, and wide streets and squares. There was the great Royal Crescent, a vast sweep of thirty houses curving around a lawn. There was the Circus, a ring of grand houses. There were the Pump Room, where people gathered in the mornings to take the waters and to see and be seen, and the Assembly Rooms, where balls and concerts were held in the evenings.

It was lovely to look at. But it was also busy, noisy, and rather fashionable in a way that Jane did not always enjoy. On the day they arrived, she wrote to Cassandra that the first view of Bath in fine weather did not answer her expectations, and that she thought she saw it more distinctly through rain. The pale stone glittered in the sun, and the hot pavements reflected the light. In one of her novels, a character speaks of her dislike of the white glare of Bath.

The family first stayed with Mrs Austen's brother, Mr Leigh-Perrot, and his wife, who had a house in Bath. Then they took a lease on a house at number four Sydney Place, looking out onto the pleasant Sydney Gardens, a kind of pleasure park with lawns, winding paths, a labyrinth, little bridges over the canal, and evening concerts with fireworks and illuminations in the summer. It was a good, solid, comfortable house, and they lived there for about three years.

Life in Bath had its own gentle routine. In the mornings there might be walks, or shopping, or visits. The Austens walked a great deal, up into the hills around the town, where there were fine views over the valley. In the evenings there were card parties, concerts, and sometimes a ball at the Upper Rooms. Jane went to some of them. But her letters from Bath tend to describe the people with a cool and amused eye, rather than with the delight of her Steventon dances. After one evening gathering, she called it another stupid party, and added that perhaps if it had been larger, it might have been less intolerable.

What did she write in Bath? Rather less, it seems, than in the years before. She went on revising, and it was during these years that her brother Henry sold the manuscript of Susan to Crosby. But some scholars think that the busy, unsettled life of a rented town house was not as good for writing as the quiet of a country rectory. It is often like that. A writer needs a corner of her own, and a certain kind of peace, and Bath did not always give her that.

But Bath gave her other things. It gave her a whole world of characters: invalids and fortune hunters, officers on leave, ladies with daughters to marry, gentlemen looking for wives with money, retired admirals, gossips and snobs. Two of her novels, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion, are partly set in Bath, and the town comes alive in them, with its pavements and its shops and its crowded Pump Room. She knew it well. She simply did not love it.

There were escapes, too. In the summers, the family went on holidays to the seaside, as many people did. They visited Sidmouth and Dawlish in Devon, and Teignmouth, and above all, in eighteen hundred and three and eighteen hundred and four, the little town of Lyme Regis on the Dorset coast.

Lyme Regis was a small, steep town tumbling down a hill to the sea. It had a curving stone harbour wall, called the Cobb, which reached out into the waves like a great arm, and the cliffs on either side were full of fossils. Jane loved it. She bathed in the sea there, and wrote to Cassandra that she enjoyed it so much she perhaps stayed in too long. She walked on the Cobb and along the shore. She went to a little ball in the assembly rooms and danced. Years later, she would set one of the most famous scenes in all her novels on the Cobb at Lyme, when a lively young woman jumps down the steep steps too quickly, and falls.

Imagine her standing on that harbour wall on a summer evening, with the wind blowing her shawl, and the sea a wide grey-blue under a pale sky. The gulls are crying. Fishing boats are rocking at their moorings. Far out, the light is fading on the water. For a few moments, the noise of Bath, and all the worries of a family without a settled home, seem very far away.

Those were good days. But a quieter, harder time was coming. In the next part of our story, Jane receives a surprising proposal of marriage, and the family suffers a great loss.
