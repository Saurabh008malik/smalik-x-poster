## Chapter 12: A Winter Afternoon at Osborne, and Goodnight

We have come to the last chapter of our story. Let us make ourselves comfortable, and let the pace grow slower still.

In the last years of her life, the Queen was very old, and she was growing tired. Her eyesight had become dim, with cataracts, so that she could no longer read easily, and her youngest daughter, Beatrice, who had stayed at her side all through the years of widowhood, read her papers and letters aloud to her. Her legs were weak, and she was pushed about in a wheeled chair, or drove out in a little carriage. But her mind was clear, and she still did her work every day, signing documents, reading despatches, and writing her opinions.

These years were not peaceful ones. In the autumn of eighteen ninety-nine, war began in South Africa, against the Boer republics. Once more, as in the Crimea, the Queen followed every report, and grieved over every loss. She knitted woollen scarves for soldiers, and she sent a gift of chocolate in decorated tins to the troops at the front for the new year. Many of those soldiers kept the tins, unopened, for the rest of their lives, as a treasure.

In the spring of nineteen hundred, at the age of eighty, she made a long visit to Ireland, her first in almost forty years, staying for three weeks in Dublin. She drove out almost every day, through the city and the parks, and met crowds of schoolchildren in Phoenix Park. It was a remarkable effort for a woman of her age.

The family suffered sorrows in that last year, too. In the summer, her second son, Alfred, died. One of her grandsons, Prince Christian Victor, died of fever in South Africa, where he was serving as a soldier. And her eldest daughter, Vicky, was very ill in Germany, too ill to travel to see her mother. The Queen felt each of these losses keenly.

At Christmas in nineteen hundred, she went as always to Osborne, the house by the sea that she and Albert had built together, more than fifty years before. But she was weak and very tired. She was sleeping more and more, and she was not eating well. On New Year's Day, she wrote in her journal that another year had begun, and that she was feeling so weak and unwell that she entered upon it sadly. The journal entries grew shorter. Her last entry was written, with Beatrice's help, in the middle of January.

Her family were sent for. Bertie came, and some of her children and grandchildren. Her grandson, the German Emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm, Vicky's eldest son, hurried across from Berlin, and in those last days he was gentle and kind, and he helped to hold her up in her bed.

On the evening of Tuesday, the twenty-second of January, nineteen hundred and one, with her family gathered quietly around her, Queen Victoria died at Osborne House. It was about half past six in the evening, and the winter darkness had already fallen over the island and the sea. She was eighty-one years old, and she had reigned for sixty-three years and seven months. Bertie, now King Edward the Seventh, was at her side.

Most people in Britain at that time had never known any other monarch. When the news came, it felt to many as if the world itself had changed. Shops closed. Church bells tolled. People wore black in the streets, and wept.

The Queen had left careful instructions for her funeral. She did not want the deep black of mourning. She asked for white. She was laid in her coffin in a white dress, with her wedding veil over her face. Beside her, it is said, were placed small treasures from her life, among them Albert's dressing gown and, hidden in her left hand, a photograph of John Brown and a lock of his hair, covered by flowers.

Her coffin crossed the Solent on a royal yacht, between long lines of warships firing salutes. Then it was carried by train to London, and through the streets on a gun carriage, past silent crowds, and on to Windsor for the funeral service in St George's Chapel, on the second of February. Two days later, on a cold, grey day, she was carried to the mausoleum at Frogmore, and laid at last beside her beloved Albert, under the marble figures she had chosen for them both, long before. It had been almost forty years since she had last seen him.

After her death, her daughter Beatrice carried out one more task. Over many years, Beatrice copied out her mother's journals, volume after volume, leaving out whatever she thought too private or too painful, and as she went, she destroyed most of the originals, as her mother had asked. Historians have sometimes wished she had not. But a great deal survived, in Beatrice's copies, in the letters, and in the early journals that the young Queen had written for herself, and through them we can still hear Victoria's voice, across the years.

What should we remember about her? She was not a perfect person, and she did not pretend to be. She could be stubborn, and impatient, and possessive of those she loved. She ruled an Empire that brought wealth to some and great hardship to others, and that history is still being weighed. But she was also honest, and loyal, and hard-working, and she cared deeply about duty. She loved music and dancing, the sea and the hills, her dogs and her children, and above all, her husband. She began her reign as a girl of eighteen in a dressing gown, promising to do her utmost. And for sixty-three years, in her own way, she tried to keep that promise.

And so, our story comes to its end.

Let us leave her now, one last time, at Osborne, on a winter evening. The light has gone from the windows. The sea is grey and calm. Down on the shore, the small waves turn over softly on the shingle, again and again, the way they always have. In the woods behind the house, the trees that Albert planted stand tall and quiet in the dark. And somewhere, in a room full of photographs and memories, a single lamp is turned down low.

Thank you for listening, and for keeping company with Queen Victoria tonight. Let your breathing slow. Let your hands rest. There is nothing more to do now, and nowhere else to be. The story is over, and the night is soft and long.

Goodnight. Sleep well.
