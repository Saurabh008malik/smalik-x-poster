## Chapter 1: Welcome, and a Baby at Kensington

Welcome. Let us settle in together. If you can, dim the lights a little, and let your shoulders sink down into the pillow. There is nothing you need to do tonight except listen. You can close your eyes whenever you like, and you do not have to remember a single thing. The story will wait for you, and we will go slowly.

Tonight we will keep company with Queen Victoria. She reigned over Britain for more than sixty-three years, longer than any British monarch before her. She was a small woman with a strong will, a keeper of diaries, a lover of music and dogs and Highland mists, a wife, a mother of nine, and for forty years a widow. Her name was given to a whole age. We will follow her from a quiet palace nursery in London, to a crowded abbey on the morning of her coronation, to a seaside house on the Isle of Wight, to a castle among the Scottish hills, and at last to a still room on a winter afternoon at the very beginning of a new century. It is a long life, and we will take our time with it.

Let us begin in the spring of eighteen hundred and nineteen, in London.

On the western edge of the city, beyond the busy streets, there was a large and rather old brick palace called Kensington Palace. It stood among wide gardens and avenues of trees, with a round pond and long lawns, and in those days it felt more like a country house than a royal court. Parts of it were shabby. Some rooms were shut up. Several members of the royal family lived there in separate apartments, a little like neighbours in a great old building, each with their own staff and their own small worries.

In one of those apartments, in the early hours of the twenty-fourth of May, eighteen hundred and nineteen, a baby girl was born. She was healthy and plump, and the people around her bed were pleased. Her father was Prince Edward, Duke of Kent, the fourth son of King George the Third. Her mother was a German princess, Victoria of Saxe-Coburg-Saalfeld, a widow who had married the Duke the year before.

When the baby was born, she was fifth in line to the throne. That sounds very far away from a crown, and it was. Ahead of her stood her father and three of his elder brothers. But the royal family at that time was in a strange position. King George the Third was old and unwell, and lived shut away at Windsor. His many sons had very few lawful children. Only a little while before, the one young princess of the next generation, Princess Charlotte, had died in childbirth, and the whole country had mourned her. So the question of who would one day wear the crown was suddenly an open one, and the Duke of Kent was quietly hopeful that his little daughter might matter a great deal.

The baby was christened a month later, in a private ceremony at Kensington Palace. Her parents had hoped for a grand list of names. But her uncle, the Prince Regent, who was ruling in place of the old King, was not fond of the Duke of Kent, and at the christening itself he would not allow most of the names they wanted. In the end she was named Alexandrina, after one of her godparents, the Emperor of Russia, and Victoria, after her mother. For her first years she was called Drina by the family. Later, she would choose to be known by her second name, and the world would know her as Victoria.

Let us imagine that nursery for a moment. A high window with soft curtains. A cradle near the fire. The sound of the gardens outside, the rooks in the elms, a carriage going by on the gravel. Her mother was devoted to her. Her father is said to have described the baby, in a letter, as plump as a partridge. He was proud of her, and the story is often told that he would show her to visitors and tell them to look at her well, because she would be Queen of England. We cannot be sure he said exactly those words, but it fits what we know of his hopes.

The family did not have much money. The Duke of Kent had large debts, and to save on expenses, at the end of that year the family travelled to the south coast of Devon, to the town of Sidmouth, where they took a house near the sea for the winter. The sea air was thought to be good for babies and for grown-ups alike.

But the winter was cold and wet. In January, the Duke went out walking in the rain, came home with wet feet and did not change his boots, and soon afterwards he caught a chill. The chill became a fever, and then an illness of the lungs. The doctors of the time did what they believed was right. They bled him, again and again, which we now know only made him weaker. On the twenty-third of January, eighteen hundred and twenty, the Duke of Kent died at Sidmouth. His daughter was eight months old.

Only six days later, the old King George the Third also died at Windsor, blind and confused, after sixty years on the throne. The Prince Regent became King George the Fourth. And in a small rented house by the sea, a young German widow sat with her baby, far from her own country, with little money and almost no friends at the English court.

Her own brother came to help. His name was Leopold, and he had been the husband of the lost Princess Charlotte. He was a careful, thoughtful man who still lived in England, and he gave his sister money and advice and a steady hand. For the little girl, Uncle Leopold would become one of the most important people in her life, a kind of second father who wrote her long letters full of guidance for years to come.

So the Duchess of Kent and her daughter returned to Kensington Palace, to the same old rooms, to begin a long, quiet and rather lonely childhood. The crown was still far off. But it was a little closer now than it had been.

Let us leave them there, in the gardens of Kensington, a young mother pushing a small carriage beneath the elms, while the rooks settle for the evening, and the lamps of London begin to glow far away across the park.
