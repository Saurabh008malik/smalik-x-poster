## Chapter 10: The Widow of Windsor

Let us walk on, slowly, into the long years after Albert, when the Queen lived mostly out of sight.

For a long time after his death, Victoria hid herself away. She stayed at Windsor, at Osborne and at Balmoral, and she hardly ever came to London. She would not open Parliament in person for several years. She did not want to be seen in public, or to ride in processions, or to hold great court ceremonies. She did her work. Every day the red despatch boxes came, full of state papers, and she read them and signed them and wrote her opinions in the margins. But she did it from her desk, far away from the people, and the people began to feel that they had lost their Queen.

People called her the Widow of Windsor. As the years went by, there was growing grumbling in the newspapers. Some asked what the country was paying for, if the monarch was never seen. A few public speakers even began to say that Britain might do better without a monarchy at all. For a time, in the early eighteen-seventies, the feeling against her was quite strong.

Then, in the winter of eighteen seventy-one, something happened that changed the mood of the country. Bertie, the Prince of Wales, fell gravely ill with typhoid fever, the same illness that had been named as the cause of his father's death, ten years before. For days, he lay close to death at his country house at Sandringham, in Norfolk. The Queen went to his bedside. The whole country waited for news. And then, on the fourteenth of December, the very anniversary of Albert's death, Bertie began to recover.

There was enormous relief. A few weeks later, at the end of February, eighteen seventy-two, the Queen and the Prince rode in an open carriage through the streets of London to a service of thanksgiving at St Paul's Cathedral. Huge, warm crowds came out to cheer them. The Queen was deeply moved. It was the beginning of a slow return of affection between Victoria and her people.

In these quiet years at Balmoral, one person came to matter a great deal to her. His name was John Brown. He was a Highland servant, a gillie, a big, bearded, plain-spoken man from the Balmoral estate, who had first worked for the family in Albert's time, leading the Queen's pony on her rides over the hills. In the years after Albert's death, her doctors and family hoped that riding out in the fresh air would do her good, and Brown was brought down to Osborne to lead her pony there too. He became her personal servant, always close by, at Balmoral and at Windsor and at Osborne.

Brown was devoted to her. He was honest and direct, and spoke to the Queen in a blunt way that no one else dared to, which she found comforting rather than rude. He looked after her on her drives and rides, and kept unwanted visitors away. Some of her family and household disliked him and resented the trust she placed in him, and there was gossip about him in the newspapers. But to Victoria he was a loyal friend, at a time when she felt very alone. When he died, in eighteen eighty-three, she grieved for him deeply, and had memorials to him set up at Balmoral.

Meanwhile, the great business of government went on, and two Prime Ministers stand out in these years, as different from each other as two men could be.

The first was William Ewart Gladstone, the leader of the Liberal Party. He was earnest, brilliant, deeply religious and tireless, and he served as Prime Minister four times. He was a great reformer. But he and the Queen never got on. He talked to her at great length, and very seriously, and she is reported to have complained that he addressed her as if she were a public meeting.

The second was Benjamin Disraeli, the leader of the Conservative Party, a novelist as well as a politician, witty, charming and clever. Disraeli understood the Queen, and he flattered her gently. He sent her long, lively letters full of gossip and news. He called her the Faery Queen, after a poem by Edmund Spenser. And he once said, so the story goes, that when it came to royalty, one should lay on the flattery with a trowel. Victoria was very fond of him. She sent him primroses from the woods at Osborne, which he loved.

It was Disraeli who arranged for her to receive a new title. In eighteen seventy-six, Parliament passed a law, the Royal Titles Act, and on the first of January, eighteen seventy-seven, at a vast ceremony at Delhi, Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India. She was delighted, and began to sign her letters Victoria R and I, for Regina et Imperatrix, Queen and Empress. She never travelled to India, but she was proud of the title for the rest of her life, and in her last years she even began to learn Hindustani.

Slowly, the Queen began to come out into the world again. She opened Parliament a few more times. She visited hospitals and opened new buildings. She travelled more widely, and in later years she spent part of each spring in the warm south of France, on the Riviera, where she would drive along the coast roads under the palms.

Let us leave her there, on a spring afternoon on the Riviera, an elderly lady in black in a small carriage drawn by a donkey, rolling gently along a sunlit lane between orange trees, with the blue of the Mediterranean shining far below.
