## Chapter 4: A Crown in the Abbey

Let us move forward a year, to the summer of eighteen hundred and thirty-eight, and to a morning of bells and banners in London.

The coronation of Queen Victoria took place on Thursday, the twenty-eighth of June, eighteen hundred and thirty-eight, in Westminster Abbey. London had been preparing for weeks. Wooden stands were built along the streets, and seats were sold at high prices. Visitors poured into the city from every part of the country, by coach and by the new railways, which were only just beginning to stretch out across the land. It was said that some four hundred thousand people came to watch the procession pass.

Victoria hardly slept the night before. She wrote in her journal that she was woken at four o'clock in the morning by the guns in the park, and could not get to sleep again because of the noise of the crowds and the bands and the people already gathering outside. When she looked out of the window, she saw the Green Park full of people, in a fine, bright morning.

At ten o'clock she set out from Buckingham Palace in the great golden State Coach, drawn by eight cream-coloured horses. The route went up Constitution Hill, along Piccadilly, down St James's Street and Pall Mall, through Trafalgar Square and down Whitehall to the Abbey. All along the way, the windows and balconies and rooftops were crowded with faces, and there was a great sound of cheering, rising and falling like the sea. Victoria wrote that she was deeply touched by the good humour and the loyalty of so many people.

Inside the Abbey, the old stone church had been filled with galleries of seats, hung with crimson and gold. Peers and peeresses sat in their velvet robes and coronets. There were ambassadors from foreign courts in glittering uniforms. Light came down through the high windows onto the ancient floor. And at the centre stood the Coronation Chair, the old oak throne made for King Edward the First more than five hundred years before.

The ceremony was very long, about five hours, and it had not been properly rehearsed. Some of the clergy were unsure of what came next. At one point a bishop turned over two pages of the service book at once, and the Queen was told the ceremony was finished, and sent away to a side chapel, only to be called back again to complete the parts that had been missed. The Archbishop of Canterbury placed the coronation ring on the wrong finger, the fourth instead of the fifth, and it had been made for the fifth, so he had to press it on, and it hurt her very much. Afterwards, she had to soak her hand in iced water before she could pull it off.

There was a gentler moment, too, which everyone remembered. Among the peers who came forward to pay homage was Lord Rolle, a very old man of nearly ninety. As he climbed the steps toward the throne, he stumbled and fell, and rolled back down to the bottom. He was not hurt, and he insisted on trying again. Seeing this, the young Queen rose from her throne and stepped down towards him, so that he would not have to climb so far. The people in the Abbey were moved by her kindness, and it was spoken of for a long time.

At the most solemn moment, the Archbishop lifted the crown and placed it on her head. At that instant, all the peers and peeresses put on their coronets together, and the trumpets sounded, and outside, the guns in the park and at the Tower boomed out, and the crowds in the streets cheered and cheered.

When at last it was all over, the Queen drove back to Buckingham Palace in the State Coach, wearing the crown, through the crowded streets, under the evening sky. And when she got home, tired out after so long a day, she went straight upstairs, and gave her little dog Dash his bath. That detail is in her own journal, and it is one of the most charming things in it.

That evening she dined with a few friends, and Lord Melbourne told her that she had done it all beautifully, every part of it. She watched the fireworks over the Green Park from her mother's balcony. Before going to bed, she wrote down everything that had happened, at great length, so that she would never forget it.

The first years of her reign were not all so happy.

In eighteen thirty-nine, there was a sad and ugly affair at court. One of her mother's ladies, Lady Flora Hastings, became unwell, and her body changed shape, and wicked gossip began to say that she must be expecting a child, though she was unmarried. Victoria, who disliked Lady Flora because she was a friend of Conroy's, believed the rumour, and did nothing to stop it. Lady Flora was examined by doctors, who said she was not with child. But the gossip did not stop. In fact, she was suffering from a serious illness, and a few months later, she died. The public was angry with the young Queen, and she was hissed at the races at Ascot. It was a hard lesson, and in later life, Victoria seems to have felt sorry for her part in it.

That same spring there came another crisis. Lord Melbourne's government lost its strength in Parliament, and he resigned. The leader of the other party, Sir Robert Peel, was asked to form a new government. He asked the Queen to change some of the ladies of her household, the Ladies of the Bedchamber, who were wives and daughters of Melbourne's party. Victoria refused. She did not want to lose her friends, and she did not want to lose Lord Melbourne. Peel would not take office without the change, and so Melbourne returned. People called it the Bedchamber Crisis. It showed that the Queen had a will of her own, but it also showed that she still had a great deal to learn about standing above the parties.

What she needed, people began to say quietly, was a husband.

Let us leave her there, on a warm evening after the coronation, in her room at Buckingham Palace, the crown set aside on its cushion, the fireworks fading over the Green Park, and a small dog asleep in his basket, still a little damp from his bath.
