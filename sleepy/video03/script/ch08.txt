## Chapter 8: War, Medals, and the Wider World

Let us move into the eighteen-fifties now, a decade that began in the glittering light of the Crystal Palace, and soon grew darker.

In eighteen fifty-four, Britain went to war. Together with France and the Ottoman Empire, it fought against Russia, mainly on the Crimean peninsula, on the northern shore of the Black Sea. It was the first war that Britain had fought against a great European power in forty years, since the days of Waterloo, and many people at home had cheered when it began. But the war went badly. The army was poorly supplied, and through the first terrible winter, the soldiers in their camps outside the city of Sevastopol had too little food, too little warm clothing, and too few doctors. Far more men died of disease than in battle.

For the first time, newspaper reporters were sending home accounts from the front, quickly, and people in Britain read about the suffering with shock and anger. A young nurse named Florence Nightingale took a party of nurses out to the army hospital at Scutari, across the water from Constantinople, and worked to bring cleanliness and order to its wards. In the long nights she walked the corridors with a lamp, and the soldiers came to call her the Lady with the Lamp.

The Queen followed the war very closely. She read every dispatch, and she worried for her soldiers. She and her daughters knitted mittens and scarves to send out to them. She visited wounded men in hospital at home, sitting by their beds and talking with them, and she was deeply moved by their courage. Later, she met Florence Nightingale at Balmoral and talked with her for a long time about how the care of soldiers might be made better, and she gave her a brooch that Albert had designed, with the words Blessed are the merciful.

Victoria felt that the courage of ordinary soldiers ought to be honoured, whatever their rank. Until then, the great medals and honours had mostly gone to officers. So, in eighteen fifty-six, with Albert's help, she created a new award, a simple bronze cross, to be given for the most outstanding bravery in the face of the enemy, to anyone, from the highest general to the youngest drummer boy. It was called the Victoria Cross. The words on it were simple: For Valour.

On a June morning in eighteen fifty-seven, in Hyde Park, the Queen herself pinned the first of these crosses onto the coats of sixty-two men, one by one, sitting on her horse. Today it is still the highest award for gallantry in the British forces.

The war ended in eighteen fifty-six. By then, the Queen and the Prince were already thinking about the marriage of their eldest child. Vicky was clever and lively, Albert's favourite, and in the autumn of eighteen fifty-five, when she was only fourteen, a young Prussian prince named Frederick, called Fritz, had come to Balmoral, and on a ride across the hills he had given her a sprig of white heather and spoken of his hopes. The two families agreed that they would marry, once she was old enough. They were married two years later, in January eighteen fifty-eight, when Vicky was seventeen, and she went to live in Berlin. Her parents missed her terribly. For the rest of her life, Victoria wrote to her eldest daughter several times a week, and thousands of their letters survive, full of family news and advice and opinions about everything under the sun.

In the following year, Vicky gave birth to a son, and so Victoria became a grandmother at the age of only thirty-nine. That baby grew up to become the last German Emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm the Second.

In these years too, the Queen's reign stretched further across the world. In eighteen fifty-seven, a great uprising broke out in India against the rule of the British East India Company, a trading company that had come to control much of the country. There was terrible violence on both sides, and the rising was put down with great harshness. Afterwards, in eighteen fifty-eight, the British government took over the rule of India directly from the Company, in the Queen's name. Victoria issued a proclamation to the people of India, promising, among other things, that they would be free to follow their own religions, and that people of every race and creed would be admitted fairly to offices of the government. She asked that the proclamation should speak with generosity and kindness, and that it should show respect for the religions of India. Not all those promises were kept by the officials who governed in her name.

The British Empire in these years was enormous, and growing, and it brought wealth and pride to many people in Britain. But it was also built on conquest, and it brought loss and hardship to many of the peoples who lived under it. Victoria herself, as far as we can tell, sincerely believed that the Empire was a force for good, and she cared about her subjects in distant lands, especially in India, a country she never saw. Historians today look at that Empire with a much more mixed and questioning eye.

Let us end this chapter quietly, at Windsor, at the close of the eighteen-fifties. The Queen and the Prince are forty now. Their eldest daughter is married and far away, and the younger children are growing fast. Albert is working harder than ever, at his desk before dawn every morning, with a lamp beside him and piles of papers waiting. He is tired, more tired than he admits. Victoria worries about him. But the evenings are still calm, the lamps lit, the children playing in the corridors, the castle settling for the night.
