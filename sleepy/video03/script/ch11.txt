## Chapter 11: Two Jubilees, and the Grandmother of Europe

Let us move now into the last years of the reign, when the Queen who had once been so far from her people became, at last, a beloved national grandmother.

In June, eighteen eighty-seven, Victoria celebrated fifty years on the throne, her Golden Jubilee. Kings and princes came to London from all over Europe, and rulers and representatives from across the Empire. On the twenty-first of June, she rode in an open carriage from Buckingham Palace to Westminster Abbey for a service of thanksgiving, through streets packed with cheering crowds. She would not wear a crown or the robes of state, as her ministers had hoped. Instead she wore a black dress and a bonnet, trimmed with white lace and diamonds. She was sixty-eight, a small, round, dignified figure, and the people loved her for it.

That evening, bonfires were lit on hilltops all across the country, one after another, until it seemed that the whole land was glowing. There were parties for children in the parks, with buns and mugs and games. The Queen, who had begun that morning thinking of Albert and feeling rather alone, ended it deeply touched by the warmth of so many people.

That same year, two young men came from India to serve at her table. One of them was named Abdul Karim. He was twenty-four, tall and serious, from the city of Agra. He soon caught the Queen's interest, and she asked him to teach her a little Hindustani, the language we would now call Urdu, and he did, every day, with her exercise book open on the desk. He told her about India, about its cities and its festivals and its food, and he is said to have introduced curry to the royal kitchens on a regular basis. Before long, she made him her Indian secretary, and gave him the title of Munshi, which means teacher.

Her household and family disliked him and his rise, and they tried hard to have him sent away. Their feelings were mixed up, it must be said, with the prejudices of their time. The Queen stood by him firmly. She was loyal to her friends, whatever others thought.

By now, the Queen had a very large family indeed. Her children had married into many of the royal houses of Europe. Vicky was the Empress of Germany, for a short and sad time, before her husband Fritz died of illness. Alice had married a German grand duke, and her daughter Alexandra would marry the last Tsar of Russia. Other grandchildren would become queens of Spain, of Norway, of Greece and of Romania. Victoria had forty-two grandchildren, and many great-grandchildren, and she was called the Grandmother of Europe. She wrote to them all, and gave them advice about everything, whether they wanted it or not.

The world was changing very fast around her. When she was born, people travelled by horse and by sailing ship, and letters took weeks to cross the sea. Now there were railways across every country, and great steamships, and the telegraph sent messages around the world in minutes. There were gas lamps and then electric lights. There were telephones, and the Queen heard one demonstrated at Osborne by Alexander Graham Bell himself, in eighteen seventy-eight. There were photographs everywhere, and in her last years there was even moving film, and she was filmed with her family at Balmoral.

Ten years after the Golden Jubilee came the greatest celebration of all, her Diamond Jubilee, in eighteen ninety-seven, sixty years on the throne. No British monarch had ever reigned so long. This time, it was made a festival of the whole Empire. Soldiers came from Canada and Australia, from India and Africa and the islands of the Caribbean and the Pacific, to march through the streets of London.

On the twenty-second of June, eighteen ninety-seven, before she set out, the Queen went to the telegraph room at Buckingham Palace and pressed an electric button. Her message flashed out along the wires to every corner of the Empire. It said: From my heart I thank my beloved people. May God bless them.

Then she rode through London in an open carriage, drawn by eight cream horses, along a six-mile route, past enormous crowds. She was seventy-eight now, and her legs were too weak for the steps of St Paul's Cathedral. So the service of thanksgiving was held outside, on the steps, with the Queen sitting in her carriage at the bottom. The sun shone all day. People said it was Queen's weather.

That night, she wrote that no one ever, she believed, had met with such an ovation as was given to her, passing through those six miles of streets. The cheering was deafening, she wrote, and every face seemed to be filled with real joy. She was much moved and gratified.

Let us leave her there, on a warm June night in eighteen ninety-seven, at Buckingham Palace. The streets outside are still full of people, singing and laughing. Lamps and lanterns hang in every window. And a very old lady in black sits quietly at her desk, her eyes tired but happy, writing it all down in her journal, as she has done on almost every night of her life, since she was a girl of thirteen.
