## Chapter 3: The Morning She Became Queen

Let us go back to that June night, in eighteen hundred and thirty-seven.

At Windsor Castle, King William the Fourth had been failing for weeks. He was seventy-one, and his heart was weak. On the eighteenth of June, the anniversary of the Battle of Waterloo, he asked to live to see the day, and he did. In the early hours of the twentieth of June, a little after two o'clock in the morning, he died quietly, with his wife, Queen Adelaide, beside him.

Almost at once, two men set out from Windsor in a carriage. One was the Archbishop of Canterbury. The other was Lord Conyngham, the Lord Chamberlain, the officer in charge of the royal household. They drove through the dark, along the roads toward London, and they arrived at Kensington Palace at about five o'clock, as the summer sky was growing pale. The palace was asleep. They knocked, and rang, and waited, and at last a porter let them in.

Victoria herself tells us what happened next, in her journal, in the words she wrote that same day. She was awoke at six o'clock by Mamma, who told her that the Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Conyngham were here and wished to see her. She got out of bed, and went into her sitting room, only in her dressing gown, and alone. There Lord Conyngham told her that her poor uncle the King was no more, and that consequently she was Queen.

Let us picture that room for a moment. The early light coming in at the windows. A small young woman, not quite five feet tall, in a dressing gown, her hair loose down her back. Two tired gentlemen in dark coats, who had driven all night, bowing low before her. And outside, the birds in the gardens of Kensington beginning their morning songs, as if nothing at all had happened.

Later that day, she wrote in her journal the lines that are perhaps the most famous she ever wrote. Since it has pleased Providence to place her in this station, she would do her utmost to fulfil her duty towards her country. She was very young, she added, and perhaps in many things inexperienced, though not in all. But she was sure that very few people had more real good will, and more real desire to do what was fit and right, than she had.

It was a long day. She ate breakfast. She wrote letters, one to her Uncle Leopold, who was now King of the Belgians, and one to the widowed Queen Adelaide, addressing it kindly to Her Majesty the Queen, so that the grieving widow would not be hurt by a change of title too soon. She received the Prime Minister, Lord Melbourne, who came in his full court dress to kiss her hand.

And at half past eleven that morning, she went downstairs, alone, to meet her Privy Council for the first time. The room was crowded with the great men of the kingdom, dukes and bishops and generals, old statesmen and young politicians, many of whom had never set eyes on her before. Among them was the Duke of Wellington, the victor of Waterloo. They had come, some of them, out of curiosity, to see what kind of a girl this was.

What they saw surprised them. She read her declaration in a clear, steady and musical voice, without any sign of nerves. She took the oaths. She received the homage of the councillors one by one, as each knelt and kissed her hand, and she treated them all with the same grave and simple courtesy, whether they were her own uncles or men she had never met. Those who were there later wrote with real admiration about how calm and dignified she had been.

There was one more small decision that day, and it said a great deal. For the first time in her life, that night, Victoria did not sleep in her mother's room. She asked for her bed to be moved into a room of her own. And over the following weeks, she made it plain that Sir John Conroy would have no place at her court. He was given a pension and a title, and he was kept at a distance. The long years of the Kensington System were over.

Within a few weeks, the young Queen moved out of Kensington Palace and into Buckingham Palace, a large new building at the end of St James's Park that had been rebuilt for the King but never really lived in. Victoria became the first monarch to make it her home. Her mother came with her, but she was given rooms some way off, and mother and daughter would remain at a polite distance for several years.

Now Victoria had a new and constant companion: the Prime Minister himself, William Lamb, Lord Melbourne. He was fifty-eight, a widower, handsome, witty, well read, and a little weary of the world. He came to see her nearly every day. He talked to her about politics and history, about books and people, about the ways of the court. He told her funny stories and gave her careful advice. He was patient with her, and kind. For the young Queen, who had grown up without a father, he became a trusted teacher and a dear friend, and her journal for those years is full of what Lord Melbourne had said.

She loved her new freedom. She rode in Windsor Great Park with a large party of ladies and gentlemen, cantering under the trees. She gave dinners and balls, and she danced until the early hours of the morning. She went to the opera as often as she liked. She laughed a great deal. She played with her beloved little dog, a King Charles spaniel called Dash, whom she would bathe and dress up in a little scarlet jacket.

She was eighteen, and for the first time she was free, and the whole of a long life lay before her.

Let us leave her there, on a summer evening at Buckingham Palace, the windows open to the park, music drifting from a room somewhere below, and a young Queen sitting at her desk, writing the day into her journal, line by careful line.
