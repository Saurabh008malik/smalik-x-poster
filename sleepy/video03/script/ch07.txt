## Chapter 7: The Highlands and the Crystal Palace

Let us travel north now, a long way north, to Scotland.

Victoria first visited Scotland in the autumn of eighteen forty-two, sailing up the east coast to Edinburgh, and she fell in love with it at once. The mountains, the lochs, the heather, the clear cold air, the sound of the bagpipes in the evening. Albert loved it too. The hills and forests reminded him of his own home in Germany, of Thuringia and the woods around the Rosenau. They came back again, and again, and in eighteen forty-eight they took the lease of a small old castle on the River Dee, in Aberdeenshire, called Balmoral.

They had never seen it before they took it. On their first visit, in September of that year, Victoria wrote that it was a pretty little castle in the old Scottish style, and that all seemed to breathe freedom and peace, and to make one forget the world and its sad turmoils. A few years later they bought the estate, and since the old castle was too small for so large a family, Albert worked with an architect from Aberdeen to build a new one, of pale grey granite, with a tall clock tower and turrets, just beside the old one. The new Balmoral was finished in eighteen fifty-five, and the old castle was taken down.

Let us picture the valley. The River Dee running fast and brown over its stones. Pine woods climbing the slopes. Behind them, the bare round shoulders of the mountains, purple with heather in late summer, and the high peak of Lochnagar, often wrapped in cloud. In the evening, the sky turns pale green and gold, and the air smells of pine and peat smoke.

At Balmoral, Victoria and Albert lived more simply than anywhere else. They took long walks and long rides over the hills, and picnics by the lochs. Albert went out stalking deer with the Highland gillies. Victoria sketched and painted in watercolour. They made expeditions in disguise, travelling with only a few servants, staying at small country inns under false names, and Victoria delighted in not being recognised. They went to Highland gatherings, where strong men threw heavy stones and great tree trunks, and the pipers played, and the dancers danced reels on the grass.

Many years later, Victoria published some of her Highland journal entries in a book, called Leaves from the Journal of Our Life in the Highlands. It sold in great numbers. People loved to read the Queen's own simple words about her walks and rides and picnics and the weather on the hills.

But Albert's greatest achievement in these years happened in London.

Albert believed in science and industry, in education, and in the idea that the nations of the world could learn from one another in peace. He had an idea for a vast exhibition, where every country could show its finest work, its machines and its crafts, its inventions and its arts. He worked on it for two years, through endless meetings and committees, and against a great deal of doubt and opposition.

The building chosen for it was extraordinary. It was designed by a gardener, Joseph Paxton, who had built great glasshouses for the Duke of Devonshire. His plan was for an enormous house of glass and iron, made of thousands of identical parts that could be put together quickly and taken apart again afterwards. It rose in Hyde Park in just a few months. It was so tall that it was built right over the top of several great old elm trees, which stood inside it, still growing. A magazine gave it the name that stuck: the Crystal Palace.

The Great Exhibition opened on the first of May, eighteen hundred and fifty-one. The Queen came with Albert and their two eldest children, Vicky and Bertie, and walked through the building in procession, under the shining glass roof, past fountains and palms and statues, to the sound of an organ and a great choir. She wrote that it was one of the greatest and most glorious days of their lives, and the most beautiful and imposing and touching spectacle ever seen. She was so proud of Albert. To her, it was Albert's triumph.

Inside, there were more than a hundred thousand objects, from all around the world. There were steam engines and printing presses and looms. There were carriages and ploughs, telescopes and pianos, silks and carpets and porcelain. There was the great Koh-i-Noor diamond, displayed in a gilded cage. There were stuffed animals, and a giant block of coal, and a knife with dozens of blades. There was a fountain of pink glass, nearly thirty feet high, at the very centre of the building.

And people came. On some days the railway companies ran cheap excursion trains, and working men and women came up to London from all over the country to see it, many of them for the first time in their lives. In about five months, more than six million visits were made to the Crystal Palace. Victoria herself went back again and again, dozens of times, walking the long aisles in the mornings before the crowds arrived.

The Exhibition made a large profit, and Albert used it to buy land in South Kensington, close to where Victoria had been born, for museums and colleges where people could go on learning. Out of that plan grew some of the great museums of London, including the one we know today as the Victoria and Albert Museum.

Let us leave them there, on a summer evening in Hyde Park, as the last visitors stream out through the doors of the Crystal Palace, and the low sun shines through all those thousands of panes of glass, so that the whole great building glows like a lantern among the trees.
