## Chapter 2: The Kensington Years

Let us return to Kensington Palace, and to a little girl growing up in its long, quiet rooms.

Victoria's childhood was comfortable, but it was not free. Her mother loved her deeply, and also worried about her constantly. The Duchess of Kent knew that her daughter might one day be Queen, and she was afraid of everything that could go wrong. She was afraid of illness. She was afraid of the King's court, with its gossip and its quarrels. And she was afraid, perhaps most of all, of losing her own place beside her daughter.

Beside the Duchess stood a man named Sir John Conroy. He had served the late Duke of Kent, and after the Duke's death he became the Duchess's chief adviser and the manager of her household. Conroy was clever, ambitious and very sure of himself. Together, he and the Duchess created a strict way of bringing up the young princess. Historians later gave it a name: the Kensington System.

Under this system, Victoria was almost never alone. She slept in her mother's bedroom, every night, until the day she became Queen. She was not allowed to walk down a staircase without someone holding her hand. She met very few children of her own age, and those she did meet were carefully chosen. Every visitor, every lesson and every outing was watched. The idea was to keep her safe, and to make her depend entirely on her mother and on Conroy. Many years later, Victoria would look back on these years without much warmth, and she would say that her childhood had not been a happy one.

And yet, inside those careful walls, there were gentle things too.

There was her governess, Louise Lehzen, a pastor's daughter from Hanover in Germany, who came into the household when Victoria was a small child. Lehzen was strict about lessons, but she was also loyal and affectionate, and she became the person Victoria trusted most. She was the one who sat with her, read with her, and quietly took her side.

There was a half-sister, Feodora, her mother's daughter from her first marriage. Feodora was twelve years older, and Victoria adored her. When Feodora married and moved away to Germany, the little princess missed her terribly, and they wrote to each other for the rest of their lives.

And there were dolls. Victoria and Lehzen together dressed a large collection of small wooden dolls, more than a hundred of them, many as characters from the theatre and the opera, with tiny silk gowns and lace collars. Some of those little dolls still survive today.

Let us picture her at her lessons. A table near the window. Copybooks and pens. A globe in the corner. She learned English, French and German, and later some Italian and Latin. She learned history and geography, a little arithmetic, and religion from a clergyman tutor. She learned to draw and paint in watercolour, which she would enjoy all her life. She learned to sing, and she had a sweet, clear voice. She loved riding, and she loved dancing, and she loved, above almost everything, going to the opera and the ballet, where she would watch with shining eyes and come home to draw the dancers in her sketchbook.

When she was thirteen, her mother gave her a journal. On the first of August, eighteen hundred and thirty-two, Victoria began to write in it, and she went on writing in a journal almost every day for the rest of her life, for nearly seventy years. At first her mother read the entries, so the young princess wrote carefully and politely, about the weather, her lessons, her lessons again, and the plays she had seen. But it was the beginning of a lifelong habit, and through those pages we can hear her voice across the years.

There is a well-known story from her childhood, told much later by Lehzen. It goes like this. When Victoria was about ten, a family tree was slipped into one of her history books, so that she could discover for herself how close she stood to the throne. She studied it, and understood, and after a moment she is said to have answered with the words, I will be good. It is a lovely story, and it may well be true, but we should hold it lightly. It comes from Lehzen's memory, written down many years after the event.

As she grew older, her mother and Conroy took her on long journeys around England and Wales. People came out in the thousands to see her. There were cheering crowds, church bells, bands and speeches. The idea was to show the country its future Queen. Her uncle, by then King William the Fourth, who had followed his brother to the throne, did not like these tours at all. He thought they looked like royal processions for a princess who was not yet anything of the kind.

In fact, King William and the Duchess of Kent disliked each other more and more. The King was a kindly but blunt old sailor, and he was fond of his niece. He wanted her at court. Her mother kept her away. At a large dinner on his birthday, in the summer of eighteen hundred and thirty-six, the King stood up and said, in front of everyone, that he hoped to live long enough for Victoria to come of age, so that her mother would never rule in her name. It was a painful scene, and Victoria, sitting at the table, was said to have burst into tears.

The King's wish was granted, if only just. On the twenty-fourth of May, eighteen hundred and thirty-seven, Victoria turned eighteen. She was now of age, and she could reign in her own right. In her journal she wrote, How old! And she added that she still felt very far from being what she should be.

Less than a month later, the old King lay dying at Windsor.

Let us leave her there, on a mild June evening at Kensington, at her window, looking out at the gardens where she had played as a little girl, not knowing that by the next morning, everything would change.
