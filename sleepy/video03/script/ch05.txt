## Chapter 5: Albert

Let us travel now, in our minds, to a small green corner of Germany, and to a country house among the hills.

Near the town of Coburg, in the duchy of Saxe-Coburg, stood a pretty house called the Rosenau, with yellow walls and pointed windows, set in a park beside a stream. Here, on the twenty-sixth of August, eighteen hundred and nineteen, just three months after Victoria's birth, a baby boy was born. His name was Albert. He was the second son of the Duke of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, and he was Victoria's first cousin, because his father was the brother of Victoria's mother. And by a small coincidence, the same midwife who had helped to deliver Victoria at Kensington had travelled back to Germany in time to deliver Albert too.

Albert's childhood was very different from Victoria's. He grew up in the countryside with his elder brother, Ernest, roaming the woods, collecting stones and shells and insects, and studying hard with a good tutor. But his family life was unhappy. His parents' marriage broke down, and when he was a small boy his mother left the court and never came back. He never saw her again. He grew into a serious, gentle, very hard-working young man, who loved music and science and art, and who was happiest in quiet places, among books and trees.

For years, their Uncle Leopold had hoped that Victoria and Albert might marry. In eighteen thirty-six, when they were both sixteen, Albert and his brother came to England for a visit. Victoria liked him. She wrote in her journal that he was extremely handsome, and kind and good and clever, and she wrote to her uncle to thank him for the prospect of great happiness. But nothing was settled, and after she became Queen, she was in no hurry at all. She was enjoying her freedom, and she told Lord Melbourne that she did not wish to marry for some years.

Then, in October, eighteen thirty-nine, Albert came to England again, to Windsor Castle. Victoria was waiting at the top of the staircase as he arrived, on the tenth of October, in the evening. And this time, everything was different. In her journal that night she wrote that it was with some emotion that she beheld Albert, who was beautiful.

Over the next few days, they rode together in the park, and danced together at the evening balls, and talked and laughed. Within five days, her mind was made up. Because she was the Queen, it was not proper for him to propose to her. She had to ask him. So on the fifteenth of October, eighteen thirty-nine, she sent for him to come to her private room at Windsor, and there, alone, she asked him to marry her. He said yes. She wrote that it was the happiest, brightest moment of her life.

Let us picture them, two young people of twenty, in a quiet room in a great old castle, holding hands by the window, with the autumn trees of the Great Park outside, turning red and gold in the October light.

The wedding took place four months later, on the tenth of February, eighteen hundred and forty, in the Chapel Royal at St James's Palace in London. The morning was wet and stormy. But by the time the bride set out, the rain had eased, and huge crowds stood along the streets to see her pass.

Victoria wore a dress of heavy white satin, trimmed with deep lace made by hand in the little Devon village of Beer, and a wreath of orange blossom in her hair instead of a crown. At that time, a royal bride would often have worn colours, or silver, or heavy robes of state. A white wedding dress was not unknown, but Victoria's choice made it fashionable, and brides across Britain and far beyond copied it, so that the white wedding gown became the custom that it is today.

The service was simple. Albert wore the uniform of a British field marshal. When the vows were made, the guns boomed in the park. Afterwards there was a wedding breakfast at Buckingham Palace, with an enormous cake, and that afternoon the couple drove out to Windsor for a very short honeymoon, of only a few days, because the Queen said she could not be away from London and her duties for longer.

That night, Victoria wrote that she had never, never spent such an evening, and that his great love and affection gave her feelings of heavenly love and happiness she had never hoped to feel before.

The early months of the marriage were not entirely easy. Albert was a foreign prince in a country that did not quite trust him. Parliament gave him a smaller allowance than he had expected, and refused to give him a British title. At first, the Queen did not want him to help with her work. He was not allowed to see state papers. He was only a husband, and he felt it. He wrote to a friend that he was the husband, but not the master in the house.

But slowly, very slowly, things changed. Albert was patient. He was clever and careful, and he was always working. In November of that first year, their first child was born, a daughter, also named Victoria, and known in the family as Vicky. During the months when the Queen was resting before and after the birth, Albert was given a key to the boxes of state papers, so that he could help her. He was good at it. He read everything. He made notes. He began to sit beside her when she met her ministers.

Little by little, he became not just her husband, but her private secretary, her adviser, and her partner in the work of the crown. And he brought order and calm to the household. He tidied up the palace accounts. He made changes to the way the royal houses were run, removing waste and muddle. And he gently moved Baroness Lehzen out of her position of influence, so that in eighteen forty-two she went home to Germany.

Let us leave them there, in a lamplit room at Buckingham Palace, two desks side by side, two heads bent over papers, and between them, in a cradle by the fire, a small baby asleep.
