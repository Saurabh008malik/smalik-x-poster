## Chapter 6: A Growing Family, and a House by the Sea

Let us look now at the busiest years of Victoria's life, the eighteen-forties, when her nursery was never empty for long.

After Vicky came a son, Albert Edward, in November, eighteen forty-one. He was the heir to the throne, and the family called him Bertie. Then came Alice in eighteen forty-three, Alfred in eighteen forty-four, Helena in eighteen forty-six, Louise in eighteen forty-eight, Arthur in eighteen fifty, Leopold in eighteen fifty-three, and last of all, little Beatrice, in eighteen fifty-seven. Nine children in seventeen years. Four sons and five daughters.

Victoria did not much enjoy being pregnant, and she was honest about it in her letters. She did not pretend that babies were always delightful, and she found tiny infants rather frightening. But she loved her children in her own way, and she and Albert gave a great deal of thought to how they should be raised. Albert, in particular, drew up careful plans for their education, with lessons and timetables and tutors. He hoped the children would grow up to be good, wise and useful, and above all he hoped that Bertie, the future King, would become a serious scholar. Bertie, a lively and affectionate boy who did not enjoy books at all, found this very hard, and it was a source of sorrow between father and son for many years.

For the birth of Prince Leopold, in April, eighteen fifty-three, the Queen did something new. She allowed a doctor named John Snow to give her a little chloroform, on a handkerchief, to ease the pain. She found it wonderful, and wrote of its soothing, quieting and delightful effect. Because the Queen had used it, many other women felt they could ask for it too, and it helped pain relief in childbirth to become accepted. She used it again for Beatrice four years later.

With so many children, the royal family needed somewhere to be a family. Buckingham Palace was grand, but it was in the middle of London, and crowded with officials and visitors. Windsor Castle was ancient and magnificent, but it was also cold and full of courtiers. Victoria and Albert wanted a home of their own, a private place, away from the business of state.

They found it on the Isle of Wight, a green island just off the south coast of England, across a narrow stretch of sea called the Solent. In eighteen forty-five, they bought an estate there called Osborne, with their own money. The old house was too small, so Albert pulled it down and designed a new one himself, working with a London builder named Thomas Cubitt. He built it in the style of an Italian villa, with two tall towers, wide terraces and gardens stepping down toward the sea. The view across the water reminded him of the Bay of Naples.

Let us walk there for a while. Pale stone walls, warm in the sun. Terraces with fountains and statues, and beds of bright flowers. Below them, lawns and woods running down to a private beach, where the waves turn over softly on the shingle. Albert planted trees there, thousands of them, and he loved to walk in the woods and listen to the nightingales in the evening. Victoria felt she could hardly imagine a prettier place.

The children were happy at Osborne. They had their own little bathing machine, a small wooden hut on wheels that was rolled down into the sea, so they could step straight into the water. Victoria herself first bathed in the sea there, and wrote in her journal that it was delightful, until she put her head under the water, when she thought she should be stifled.

In the gardens, Albert built a real wooden chalet for the children, in the Swiss style, called the Swiss Cottage. Inside it there was a small kitchen, where the princesses learned to cook and make cakes, which they served to their parents. Outside, each child had a small garden of their own, where they grew vegetables and fruit, and Albert paid them for their crops at the market price, so that they would learn the value of work.

Let us picture an evening there, in high summer. The children have washed their hands after their gardening, and the light is going gold over the lawns. Somewhere in the house, Albert is playing the organ, and Victoria is at her desk by the window, writing in her journal, as the boats on the Solent turn slowly at their moorings, and the first stars come out above the sea.

These were the years when the royal family began to look, to the people of Britain, like a family of the middle classes. Pictures and engravings showed them at home together, the Queen and the Prince surrounded by their children. Families across the country copied them. And at Christmas in eighteen forty-eight, a famous picture appeared in a London newspaper, showing the royal family gathered around a Christmas tree at Windsor, hung with candles and sweets and small presents.

Christmas trees were an old German custom, and Albert loved them from his own boyhood. They were not quite new in England, because Queen Charlotte, long before, had set one up at court. But when ordinary families saw that picture, they wanted one too, and within a few years the Christmas tree had become part of an English Christmas.

But the eighteen-forties were not peaceful years for everyone. These were the years of the Great Famine in Ireland, which was then part of the United Kingdom. From eighteen forty-five, a disease destroyed the potato crop, which so many poor families depended on, again and again, year after year. About a million people died, and more than a million left their homes for ever, sailing to America and elsewhere. The Queen gave money from her own purse to relief funds, and she wrote a letter asking churches to collect for the starving. But the government's response was slow and far from enough, and the famine left a deep and lasting grief in Ireland. It is part of the story of those years, and we should remember it gently.

Let us leave the royal family now, at Osborne, on a quiet summer night, the windows of the villa glowing above the dark lawns, the sea washing softly on the shore below, and in the woods, a single nightingale, singing.
