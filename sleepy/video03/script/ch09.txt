## Chapter 9: The Winter of Eighteen Sixty-One

This chapter holds the saddest part of Victoria's story, and we will go through it slowly and gently, the way one walks through a quiet house at night.

The year eighteen sixty-one began with a loss. In March, the Queen's mother, the Duchess of Kent, died at Frogmore House, near Windsor, with her daughter holding her hand. The long years of coldness between them had softened, after Victoria's marriage, into real affection. Now, going through her mother's papers, Victoria found old notebooks and letters, and she learned for the first time how very much her mother had loved her, all through those difficult years at Kensington. She was overcome with grief, and with regret for the misunderstandings of the past. For weeks she could hardly bear to see anyone.

Albert took on more and more of her work, so that she could rest. He was always working. He rose before dawn, and sat at his desk for hours before breakfast, writing memoranda and letters. He served on endless committees. He was worried about the Prince of Wales, Bertie, who was now nineteen, and who was not living the steady, studious life his father had hoped for. And he was not well. For some time he had been suffering from bouts of pain and sickness and sleeplessness, and he looked older than his forty-two years.

In late November, he travelled to Cambridge, where Bertie was studying, to talk with his son about his conduct. It was a cold, wet day, and the two of them walked for a long time in the rain. Albert came back to Windsor exhausted and chilled.

At about the same time, a serious quarrel was brewing between Britain and the United States, where the Civil War had begun earlier that year. An American warship had stopped a British mail ship, the Trent, at sea and taken off two Confederate envoys. People in Britain were furious, and there was talk of war. The government drafted a stern message to Washington. Albert, though he was already ill, read the draft at his desk early one morning, and suggested gentler wording, which left room for the Americans to settle the matter without losing face. The message was softened, and in the end the crisis passed peacefully. It was the last piece of public work he ever did.

Through the first two weeks of December, Albert grew steadily weaker. He could not eat or sleep. He wandered from room to room at Windsor Castle, unable to rest. The doctors at first said there was nothing to worry about. Then they said it was a fever. They told the Queen it was typhoid fever, an infection that was common then, often caught from bad water and drains. Today, some historians and doctors have wondered whether Albert had in fact been suffering from a longer illness as well, which had weakened him over many months. We cannot know for certain.

He was moved into a large room at Windsor called the Blue Room. His daughter Alice, who was eighteen, sat with him day after day, reading to him and nursing him with great calm and tenderness. Victoria came in and out, hoping and fearing by turns. On one of those days, Albert asked for music, and Alice played some of his favourite hymns and chorales for him on the piano in the next room, and he listened quietly.

On the evening of the fourteenth of December, eighteen sixty-one, it was clear that the end was near. The children who were at Windsor came in to say goodbye. Bertie had been sent for, and had arrived in time. The Queen knelt by the bed and held her husband's hand. A little before eleven o'clock that night, Albert's breathing grew softer and slower, and then it stopped. He was forty-two years old.

Victoria was forty-two too. They had been married for nearly twenty-two years.

Her grief was overwhelming. For a long time, she could not believe it. She felt that her life had ended with his. She wrote to her Uncle Leopold that the poor fatherless baby of eight months was now the utterly broken-hearted and crushed widow of forty-two, and that her life as a happy one was ended. She wrote that the world was gone for her.

Albert was buried first at St George's Chapel at Windsor, while the Queen, unable to face the funeral, stayed at Osborne. Then, near her mother's resting place at Frogmore, she had a great mausoleum built for him, of granite and marble, with a domed roof and walls rich with paintings, and with a marble figure of Albert lying at rest on top of the tomb. She chose the place at once, within days of his death, and she meant to lie there beside him one day.

For years, she kept Albert's rooms exactly as they had been. Every evening, his clothes were laid out, and hot water was brought for him to shave in the morning, just as before. His pen and blotting paper were kept on his desk. She slept with his nightshirt beside her, and with a photograph of him, taken as he lay at rest, above her pillow. Wherever she went, she took small portraits of him with her.

She wore black for the rest of her life, for nearly forty years. Black silk dresses. A small white widow's cap. Jewellery of black jet, from the cliffs near Whitby in Yorkshire, which became a great fashion because of her. Even her writing paper had a thick black border.

Let us leave her now, very gently, in the stillness of Windsor in that dark December. Let us imagine snow falling softly over the castle and the Great Park, and in a small room, a lamp burning low. A woman in black sits at a desk with a pen in her hand, writing her husband's name, again and again, so that she will never forget a single day of the life they shared.
