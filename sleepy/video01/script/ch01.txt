## Chapter 1: Welcome, and the Boy from Milk Street

Welcome, and thank you for being here tonight.

Let us begin by settling in. If you are lying down, let your shoulders sink a little into the pillow. If there is a light on, you may want to dim it. There is nothing you need to do for the next hour and a half, except to listen, and to let a long life unfold, slowly and gently, from its beginning to its end.

Tonight's story belongs to a man named Benjamin Franklin. He was a printer, a writer, a scientist, an inventor, a diplomat, and a quiet builder of libraries, hospitals and schools. He was born in a small, busy port town in the year seventeen hundred and six, he lived for eighty-four years, and when he died, tens of thousands of people walked behind his coffin.

But we will not hurry to the end. We will begin where he began, as a child in a house that smelled of tallow and wood smoke, in a town beside the sea.

Take a slow breath in, and let it go.

The town was Boston, in the colony of Massachusetts Bay, which at that time belonged to the English crown. It was not yet a city of brick and stone as we might picture it now. It was a crowded, narrow, lively harbor town, with wharves reaching out into the water, church steeples rising above the roofs, and streets that bent and wandered where the cows had once wandered before them. You might picture ships coming and going, gulls turning over the water, and the sound of rope and timber creaking in the wind.

On a street called Milk Street, close to the old South Church, stood the house where Benjamin Franklin was born. By the calendar they used at the time, the date was the seventeenth of January, seventeen hundred and six. The calendar has changed since then, and so the date is a little different today, but Franklin himself always kept the seventeenth of January as his birthday, and so shall we.

His father was named Josiah Franklin. Josiah had grown up in a village called Ecton, in the English county of Northamptonshire, where the Franklin family had lived for generations, working as blacksmiths and farmers. Josiah learned a different trade. He became a dyer for a time, and then, after he crossed the Atlantic with his first wife and their small children in the year sixteen eighty-three, he turned to making soap and candles. These were humble goods, made from animal fat, boiled in great kettles, but every household needed them, and a careful man could make an honest living from them.

Josiah's first wife died, and he married again. His second wife was Abiah Folger, a woman from the island of Nantucket, whose father had been a schoolteacher and an interpreter. Between them, Josiah and his two wives had seventeen children. Benjamin was the fifteenth of them, and the youngest of the sons. You might imagine what such a house was like: full of voices, of shoes by the door, of one pot always on the fire, and of a great many chairs squeezed close around the table.

Franklin would later write about this table. In the story of his own life, which he wrote down as a letter to his son, he remembered that his father liked to invite one or two sensible neighbors to dinner, and liked to steer the talk toward something useful or thoughtful. He said that the family paid so little attention to what was on their plates that he grew up almost careless about food, and hardly noticed what he ate. That is a small thing, but it tells us something. In that house the conversation mattered more than the meal.

Josiah was a serious man, with a strong voice and a good ear. He sang hymns in the evening, and he had a gift for judging a problem fairly. People in the town came to him for advice, even about matters of the church and the town council, and Franklin remembered that his father was often asked to settle quarrels between neighbors.

His mother, Abiah, is described as a wise and kind woman, who raised her children well, and who lived to a great age. She nursed all of her children herself, and the family, as Franklin put it, was healthy. He remembered his parents as people of good sense and good character, and he gave them credit for much of what he became.

The family was religious in the Puritan way. They went to church on Sundays, and they were expected to work hard, to be honest, and to be careful with money. Josiah had hoped that his youngest son might become a minister. It was a respectable path for a clever boy, and in those days it was one of the few ways for the son of a tradesman to rise in the world.

But there was one problem with this plan, and the problem was money. A boy who goes to the university needs years of schooling, and years of schooling cost more than a candlemaker with so many children could easily afford. We will come back to that in a moment.

First, let us stay for a little while with the child himself.

Even as a small boy, Benjamin was quick, curious, and restless. He loved the water. Boston was full of it, with the harbor on one side and the tidal flats on the other, and he learned early to swim and to handle a boat. He wrote that he was a good swimmer, and that he often led the other boys in their games on the water. In a letter written many years later, he remembered how, as a boy, he once let a kite pull him across a pond while he floated on his back. If you think about it, that was a gentle foreshadowing. A kite, a boy, and the wind, long before any storm.

He also loved to read. He read whatever came into the house, which at first was not much. There were some old books about religion that his father owned, and he read those. Then he found a copy of Pilgrim's Progress, and a few histories, and a book called Plutarch's Lives, which tells the stories of famous Greeks and Romans. He read these over and over. Whenever he found a book he liked, he spent his small coins to buy more, and he later said that he spent nearly all of his pocket money on books.

Let us pause here, at this early picture. A small boy, in a crowded house, in a town by the sea. He is not yet famous. He is not yet anything at all. He has a quick mind, a love of books, and a family that works hard and eats simply. He has no way of knowing that one day he will sail across the ocean several times, that he will be welcomed in the palaces of Europe, or that his face will one day be printed on a banknote.

All he knows tonight is the room around him, the sounds of his brothers and sisters, and the warm smell of the candle shop below.

And so, let us leave him there for the moment, safe in the house on Milk Street, and let us turn the page, to the next part of his story: the boy who went to school for only a very short time.
