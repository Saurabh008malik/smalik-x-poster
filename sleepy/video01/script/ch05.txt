## Chapter 5: London

In November of seventeen hundred and twenty-four, Benjamin Franklin, who was eighteen years old, sailed for England.

He went with a good friend named James Ralph, a clever young man who liked poetry, and with a quiet confidence. In his luggage there were no letters of credit. The governor of Pennsylvania had promised to send them to the ship, and Franklin waited, and the ship waited, and the governor's secretary told him that the letters would be coming aboard at any moment. But they never came. When they reached London, a kind merchant named Thomas Denham, who had been traveling on the same vessel, gently told him the truth. Governor Keith, he said, was a man who made promises of every kind, and kept almost none of them. There had never been any letters, and there never would be.

Franklin was young, and far from home, and with very little money. But he did not despair. He landed in London on Christmas Eve, and he did what he had been trained to do. He looked for work as a printer.

It did not take long. He found a place at a large printing house, and he worked there for about a year, setting type in a room full of strong, jolly men who drank a great deal of beer. Franklin drank water. He was so strong, in spite of it, that they called him "the Water-American," and he said, with some pride, that he could carry a heavy forme of type up the stairs, in each hand, when the others needed both hands for one. Later he moved to a bigger printing house, where the work was finer. He gained skill with every month.

London in those years was the largest city he had ever seen. He walked through the streets, and visited the bookshops, and the coffee houses, and the theaters. He went to see the great church of St. Paul, and the Tower, and the Thames, where the barges and the wherries moved up and down the river. He met interesting people. One of them was Sir Hans Sloane, a famous physician and collector, whose collection would later become the beginning of the British Museum. Franklin had a purse made of a strange fibre which does not burn, and he showed it to Sir Hans, who bought it, and invited him to visit his house to see his many curiosities.

He was also a good swimmer. On one occasion, in the summer of seventeen hundred and twenty-six, he swam several miles down the river Thames, from Chelsea to Blackfriars, and he performed some tricks in the water for his friends. Word of it spread. A nobleman, Sir William Wyndham, asked him to teach his sons to swim. Franklin thought, for a moment, of staying in England and opening a swimming school.

But he was a young man, and he also made mistakes there. He and his friend Ralph spent a good deal of money on theaters and amusements. Ralph borrowed money from him, and never paid it back. Franklin wrote a small pamphlet about philosophy, which he later considered a youthful error, and which he said he wished he had never printed. And, worst of all, he had written to the young woman in Philadelphia, Deborah Read, only once, and told her that he was in no hurry to return. In the end, she married someone else. Franklin would later call this one of the errata of his life, using the printer's word for the mistakes which are noted at the end of a book.

But by this time he was also tired of London, and he was a little homesick. And then a good opportunity came, in the shape of the kind merchant, Mr. Denham, who had told him the truth about the governor's letters. Denham was going back to Philadelphia to open a store, and he said that he would give Franklin a position as a clerk, and teach him the business. He promised that, if all went well, he would later send him to the West Indies with a cargo.

Franklin accepted. He wrote that he found that he was glad to be going home, and that he was only a little sorry to leave the city he had grown to love. In the summer of seventeen hundred and twenty-six, he boarded a ship, and began the long voyage west.

It was a slow crossing, of about eighty days. He spent much of it keeping a journal. He wrote about the sea and the weather, and about the fish that were caught along the way, and he made some resolutions about how he would live. He intended to be frugal, to be truthful, to be industrious, and to speak ill of no one. He called them rules for the conduct of his future life, and he was only twenty, but he seems to have meant every word of them.

He arrived in Philadelphia in October of that year, and he began to work in Denham's store. For a while he was happy. He was learning the business of selling goods, and he enjoyed it, and he made friends again.

But a year later, both he and his employer fell sick. Franklin nearly died. He recovered slowly. Mr. Denham was not so fortunate. He died in the summer of seventeen hundred and twenty-eight, and left Franklin a small sum of money in his will, and with that, the young man's path through the world of shopkeeping came to an end.

And so he returned, quietly, to the trade he knew best. In the next chapter, we will see how a young printer, in a growing town, with a little money and a great many ideas, began to build a life.
