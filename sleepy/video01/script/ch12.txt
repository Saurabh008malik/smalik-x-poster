## Chapter 12: Home, and Goodnight

On the fourteenth of September, in seventeen hundred and eighty-five, Benjamin Franklin returned to Philadelphia. A crowd gathered at the river, bells rang, and cannons were fired. He was seventy-nine years old, and he had been away from home, with only brief returns, for almost twenty-five years.

He had hoped for a quiet retirement. But his country was not ready to let him rest. Within weeks, he was chosen to be president of the state of Pennsylvania, a post which he held for three years. And in the summer of seventeen hundred and eighty-seven, he was one of the delegates sent to Philadelphia to write a new constitution for the United States.

He was eighty-one, the oldest man in the room, and he was often in pain. He was carried to the meetings in a sedan chair, and his speeches were so short that he usually asked a colleague to read them aloud. But his presence was a comfort. When the debates became bitter, and the small states and the large states could not agree, he urged the delegates to find a compromise. When the work was done, and some of the men still doubted it, he spoke for the whole: he said that there were parts of the Constitution he did not approve, but that he agreed to it, with all its faults, because he doubted that any other convention could make a better one.

James Madison recorded one last moment. As the delegates were signing, Franklin looked at the sun painted on the back of the president's chair, and said that during the whole summer he had wondered whether it was a rising sun or a setting sun. Now, he said, he had the happiness to know that it was a rising sun.

There is one more thing that we should say about these years, and say it honestly. Like many prosperous men of his time, Franklin had owned enslaved people, and for a long time he had accepted slavery as part of the world. Over the years his view changed. In the last years of his life he became the president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, and in February of seventeen hundred and ninety, only a few weeks before he died, he signed a petition to Congress urging an end to slavery. The petition was not granted. It is a fact of his life, with its lights and its shadows, and it is right to remember it.

In these last years, he also returned to his book. Long ago, he had begun to write the story of his life, as a letter to his son, and he now took it up again, working on it in his room, with his books around him. It was never finished. It reaches only to the year seventeen hundred and fifty-seven. But it became one of the most widely read books of its kind, and it was the story that we have been telling through this night.

He spent the last two years of his life mostly in his house, in a room with books and papers, surrounded by his daughter Sally and her children. He sat in his garden under the mulberry tree, and in the evenings, his friends came to talk. He was cheerful, as he had always been, though the pain was often great.

In the spring of seventeen hundred and ninety, he fell ill with a sickness of the chest. On the seventeenth of April, in the evening, he died peacefully, in his bed. He was eighty-four years old.

At his funeral, four days later, the bells of Philadelphia rang, and thousands of people walked in the procession. It is said that about twenty thousand came. He was buried beside Deborah, in the churchyard of Christ Church, under a plain stone.

He had written his own epitaph, as a young man, a playful little verse. It said that the body of B. Franklin, printer, like the cover of an old book, with its contents torn out, and stripped of its lettering and gilding, lay there, food for worms. But the work, it said, would not be lost. It would appear once more, in a new and more elegant edition, corrected and improved by the author.

And that, perhaps, is the best way to remember him. A boy from Milk Street, who loved to read, and who made himself into a printer, a scientist, a builder, a diplomat, and a friend. He left money in his will to be lent to young tradesmen in Boston and Philadelphia, to help them begin their working lives, for two hundred years.

The fire is low now, and the house is quiet. The press is still, and the candle has burned down. Somewhere out in the dark, a kite string hums softly in the wind, and then it is still.

Thank you for spending this night with Benjamin Franklin. If you are still awake, rest easy. And if you are drifting off, sleep well. Goodnight.
