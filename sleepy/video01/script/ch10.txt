## Chapter 10: The Agent in London

In the summer of seventeen hundred and fifty-seven, Benjamin Franklin, now fifty-one years old, boarded a ship once more.

The Assembly of Pennsylvania had a quarrel with the family of William Penn, the proprietors of the colony. The Penn family owned great stretches of land, and they did not wish to pay taxes on them, while the people of the colony were being asked to pay for their defense. The Assembly chose Franklin to travel to London, and to plead its case, as its agent. He took with him his son William, who was by then about twenty-six, and who was going to London to study law.

It was a long crossing. But when it was over, and he came ashore in England, he found himself in a city he knew, though he was a very different man from the young printer who had walked its streets thirty years before.

He took rooms in a quiet, narrow street near the river, called Craven Street, in the house of a widow named Mrs. Margaret Stevenson. The house still stands today, and it is the only one of his homes that does. Mrs. Stevenson looked after him very kindly, and her daughter Mary, whom everyone called Polly, became a dear friend. He wrote her long, affectionate letters about science and about life for the rest of his days. It was, in a way, a second family.

Now, the work of an agent is slow. There were meetings with the Penn family, and with officials, and there were letters to write, and a great deal of waiting. Franklin did not mind, as much as one might think. He was a famous man in London, thanks to his book on electricity. In seventeen hundred and fifty-nine, the University of St. Andrews in Scotland gave him an honorary doctorate. In the spring of seventeen hundred and sixty-two, Oxford did the same, and he went to the ceremony in a scarlet gown. And his days were filled with the company of scientists and writers, and with meetings of the Royal Society.

In seventeen hundred and sixty-two, he sailed home, and was glad to see Deborah and Sally and his friends. But the quiet did not last. Two years later, the Assembly asked him to go back to London, and he left Philadelphia once again, in the autumn of seventeen hundred and sixty-four. He told Deborah that he expected to be away a short time. In fact, he would be gone for more than ten years.

While he was away, the quarrel between Britain and her colonies grew serious. In seventeen hundred and sixty-five, the British Parliament passed the Stamp Act, which put a tax on printed paper of every kind, on newspapers, and legal documents, and even on playing cards. The colonists were angry, because they had no representatives in Parliament, and they believed that they should not be taxed without their consent. At first, Franklin did not oppose the act as strongly as some. Some of his friends in Philadelphia were furious with him for it. But when he saw how deeply the colonies felt, he changed his position.

In February of seventeen hundred and sixty-six, he was called to speak before the House of Commons, and for several hours, he answered question after question about America. He was calm, and clear, and well informed. Soon afterward, Parliament repealed the Stamp Act. It was one of the proudest moments of his time in London, though it did not solve the larger problem.

The years that followed were harder. More taxes were laid on the colonies, and the tension in Boston grew. Franklin was acting for several colonies by now, and he tried again and again to find a peaceful way. He still hoped, for a long time, that the empire could be held together.

His hopes were wounded in the winter of seventeen hundred and seventy-four. Some private letters, written years earlier by the governor of Massachusetts, had come into Franklin's hands, and he had sent them to friends in Boston, where they were made public. The British government was angry. In January, Franklin was summoned to a hearing in a room called the Cockpit, and while he stood in silence, in his velvet coat, a government lawyer spoke bitterly against him for the better part of an hour. The following day, he was dismissed from his post as deputy postmaster general.

Not long afterward, sad news came from home. Deborah, who had stayed in Philadelphia, always afraid of the sea, suffered a stroke and died in December of seventeen hundred and seventy-four. Franklin had been away for most of the past ten years, and he was not there. He did not get to say goodbye.

His son William had taken a different road. William was governor of New Jersey, and he remained loyal to the king. The two men, who had once been so close, would never again see eye to eye. It is a quiet sorrow in this story, and it was never fully mended.

In March of seventeen hundred and seventy-five, Franklin left London for good. He was sixty-nine years old. During the voyage, he spent his time taking the temperature of the sea, to learn more about the Gulf Stream. And while he was out on the Atlantic, in the spring of that year, the first shots of a war were fired in the colonies.

In the next chapter, we will follow him home, and then out again across the ocean, into a story that would change the world.
