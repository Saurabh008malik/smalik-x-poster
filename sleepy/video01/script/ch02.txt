## Chapter 2: School, Candles, and the Love of Books

When we left Benjamin, he was a small boy in a busy house, with a quick mind and a great many brothers and sisters.

His father, Josiah, had hoped to give him a church career, and so, when Benjamin was eight years old, he was sent to the Boston Latin School. This was the school where boys of promise learned Latin and the old languages, which was the first step toward the university and the ministry. By all accounts the boy did well. He rose quickly, and was moved up a class before the year was out, and everything seemed to be going to plan.

But there was the matter of the cost. A boy who stays at school into his teens, and then goes on to college, needs many years of fees and books and patience from his family, and Josiah looked at his many children and his modest income, and he came to a quiet decision. Before the year had ended, he took Benjamin out of the Latin School. He sent him instead to a smaller school, run by a man named George Brownell, where boys learned to write a good hand, and to do arithmetic, the practical skills of a working life.

At the writing, Benjamin did well. At arithmetic, he did not. He later admitted, with a smile, that he failed at it, though he said that he learned the subject properly on his own, some years afterward.

And that was the end of his schooling. By the time he was ten years old, Benjamin Franklin had finished with school altogether. All of his formal education had lasted perhaps two years. It is worth letting that settle for a moment, because the man who would one day be honored by universities in Europe and America, who would help to found a university of his own, and who would become one of the best-known writers of his age, had left the classroom before he was a teenager.

So, at ten years old, he went to work in his father's shop.

Let us imagine it. The candle shop was a room of warm smells, some pleasant, some not. Tallow, which is animal fat, was melted in large pots, and the wicks were hung on rods and dipped, again and again, until a candle grew thick enough to sell. Soap was boiled in great kettles, with ashes and lye. Benjamin's work was to cut the wicks, to fill the molds, to mind the shop, and to run errands through the streets of the town. It was honest work, and it was steady, and he did it, but he did not like it.

What he liked was the sea. The harbor was a few minutes away, and ships were arriving and leaving all the time. He told his father that he wanted to become a sailor. This troubled Josiah deeply, for one of his older sons, also named Josiah, had already gone to sea, and had never come home. The family did not want to lose another.

So Josiah did something wise. He took his restless young son on walks around the town. They visited the workshops of the joiners, the bricklayers, the turners, the braziers, and the cutlers, and they stood and watched the craftsmen at their work. He wanted Benjamin to see the trades, so that he could choose one which he might enjoy. Franklin later remembered that these walks taught him a lasting habit of mind. He learned to watch how things are made, and he said that it was useful to know how workmen did their tasks, and sometimes he would use that knowledge himself, to mend things at home and to try small experiments of his own.

In the end, it was Benjamin's love of books that decided the matter. His father noticed that he loved to read, and he knew that his older son James had recently come back from England with a printing press and a set of type. James was setting up a print shop in Boston. A printer, said Josiah, works with books and words all day long, and it seemed a good fit for his youngest son.

Benjamin was twelve when he signed the papers. They were called indentures, and they were a kind of contract. He would work as an apprentice to his brother, and in return he would be taught the trade of printing, and be given his food and a roof over his head. But the agreement was long. It bound him to serve until he was twenty-one years old, and for the last year he would be paid the wages of a skilled workman, but not before.

And so, in the year seventeen hundred and eighteen, Benjamin Franklin went to work in a printing house.

Now we come to the part that was most important to the shaping of his mind. In the print shop, he was surrounded by books, and he was able to meet the people who sold them. He made friends with the apprentices of the local booksellers, and they would lend him books to read. He would borrow a book in the evening, read it through the night, and return it in the morning so that nobody would miss it. He wrote of reading late into the night, by candlelight of course, sometimes for most of the night, in his small room.

He read about history. He read a book by Daniel Defoe, the author of Robinson Crusoe, which was about how to improve one's community. He read a book by the Boston minister Cotton Mather, called Essays to Do Good, and he said later that it gave him a turn of thinking that influenced some of the main events of his life. A great deal of what he did when he was older, the libraries and the fire companies and the hospitals, grew from the idea that an ordinary person can do good things for the people around him.

He also wanted to write well. Like most young people, he thought he could write poetry, and he composed a couple of ballads, which his brother printed, and he was sent out to sell them in the streets. One was about a shipwreck, and the other was about the capture of the pirate known as Blackbeard. They sold well. But when his father read the verses, he told him, gently but plainly, that poets are generally poor, and Franklin was glad, in time, to put the idea aside.

But the wish to write well did not leave him. And so, he looked for a better way to learn.

Let us stop here for a moment, on this picture of a boy in a candlelit room, with a borrowed book open on the table, and the whole house asleep around him. In the next chapter, we will see what he did with that wish to write, and how it led, quite by accident, to his first small adventure in the newspapers.
