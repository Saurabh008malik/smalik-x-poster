## Chapter 8: The Scientist

In the middle of the seventeen forties, when Franklin was in his late thirties, he went to see a demonstration.

A visiting lecturer from Scotland, Dr. Archibald Spencer, was passing through Boston, and he showed his audience a number of curious tricks with electricity. He rubbed a glass tube, and it drew bits of paper to it. He made sparks jump from one object to another. He gave a gentle shock to a volunteer. It was entertainment, more than science, but Franklin was fascinated, and he thought that there must be something behind it.

Not long afterward, a gift arrived in Philadelphia. A friend of the Library Company, a London merchant named Peter Collinson, sent over a glass tube, with a short note explaining how it could be used for experiments with electricity. Franklin took the tube home, and he and his friends began to work with it. He said that he had never been so engaged in any study as in this one, and he spent almost all of his spare time on it for the next few years.

Let us picture the room. It is evening, and a small group of friends has gathered. There are glass tubes and glass globes, wires and chains, and small jars lined with metal foil. There are bits of cork hung from silk threads. There is a quiet hum of talk, and now and then a small crackle, and a soft blue spark in the dim light, and laughter.

This was a time when nobody quite knew what electricity was. Franklin did not claim to be certain, either. But he was a careful watcher, and he did simple experiments, and he made notes. He discovered that electricity seemed to behave like a kind of fluid, which could be present in more or in less than the usual amount in a body. He gave the names positive and negative to those two states, names that are still used today. He is also remembered for words such as battery, charge, and conductor, which came into use in connection with his work.

He noticed something else, which was to be very important. He found that a pointed metal rod could draw off a spark silently, from a distance, while a blunt one could not. And he asked himself whether lightning, which also makes a spark, might be the same thing on a vast scale. He wrote down his suggestion for an experiment, and he sent his letters to his friend Collinson in London, who passed them on, and had them printed in a small book. It was translated into French and into other languages, and it made Franklin famous in Europe.

And now we come to the story of the kite. It is one of the best-known stories in the history of science, and like many such stories, it has been told so often that it has grown a little tangled. So let us tell it plainly, as the careful historians tell it.

In the spring of seventeen hundred and fifty-two, a French experimenter put Franklin's idea to the test, with a tall iron rod, and found that Franklin was right: the clouds were electrified. The news had not yet reached America. And so, in June of that year, on an afternoon when a thunderstorm was gathering over Philadelphia, Franklin made his own experiment in a different way. He built a kite from a silk handkerchief, because silk does not tear easily in the wind, and he fixed a pointed piece of wire to the top. He tied a length of hemp string to the kite, and at the lower end he tied a metal key, and he attached a silk ribbon to hold, because silk stays dry, and is a poor conductor. According to the best-known account, he took his son William with him to help launch the kite, and they stood under a shed, to keep the ribbon dry.

For a while, nothing happened. Then Franklin noticed that the loose fibers of the hemp string were standing up, as if they were reaching for something. He touched his knuckle to the key, and he felt a spark. As the rain wet the string, it carried more of the charge, and the sparks were stronger. He collected some of the electricity in a Leyden jar, a small glass bottle for storing charge, and later he used it to do the ordinary experiments that he had done with the charge from a glass tube. And so he had shown that the clouds, and the glass tube, held the same thing.

It is worth saying that the kite was almost certainly not struck by lightning, which would have been deadly. The experiment worked because the storm charged the string from a distance. A year later, in seventeen hundred and fifty-three, a scientist in St. Petersburg named Georg Richmann tried a similar experiment, and was killed. Franklin was a lucky man, and the story reminds us that his experiment was far more dangerous than it sounds.

What came out of the experiment was practical. Franklin had suggested that a pointed iron rod, fixed to the top of a building and connected to the ground, could safely carry the lightning down. He called it a lightning rod. Soon the buildings of Philadelphia began to have them, and then the buildings of other cities. A great many people were saved from fire. And for a century afterward, many people in Europe regarded the lightning rod as one of the most useful inventions of the age.

Franklin never took a patent on it. He wrote that he was glad if his discoveries were of use to others.

Honors followed. The Royal Society in London, the oldest scientific society in the world, gave him its Copley Medal in seventeen hundred and fifty-three, and later made him a fellow. Harvard and Yale gave him honorary degrees, though he had never been to a university. People began to call him Doctor Franklin, and he was called by that name for the rest of his life.

And his curiosity did not stop with electricity. He drew a chart of the Gulf Stream, the great warm current of the Atlantic, with the help of his cousin Timothy Folger, a whaling captain, who knew where the whales and the current ran. He invented a musical instrument, the glass armonica, which makes a sweet, ringing sound from spinning glass bowls, and which was played by people in the courts of Europe. And, in his later years, he devised a pair of spectacles with two kinds of lens in one frame, one for seeing far, and one for seeing near, which we now call bifocals.

Let us pause here with the image of a quiet man, at a window, with a glass bowl turning under his fingers, and a clear, long note rising from it, like a voice in the evening, and slowly fading.
