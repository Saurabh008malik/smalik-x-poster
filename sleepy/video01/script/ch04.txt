## Chapter 4: The Runaway

In the autumn of seventeen hundred and twenty-three, a young man of seventeen quietly left the town where he had been born.

It was not easy for him to go. Boston was home, his family lived there, and everyone he knew was there. But he could not stay in the print shop, and he could not find another master, for his brother James had made sure that no printer in Boston would hire him. If you worked against a man's family, you might lose the favor of his friends. And the ministers of the town, who did not like his reputation for cheeky writing, had begun to whisper that Benjamin was a doubter and a freethinker.

So he made up his mind. He sold some of his books, to have a little money for the journey, and a friend arranged for him to board a sloop that was sailing to New York. It was a quiet departure. His family was not told, and his father was not told. A few days later, after a gentle passage with the wind, he arrived in a city where he knew no one, with almost no money in his pocket.

New York was small then, and he looked for work at the only printer in town, a man named William Bradford. But Bradford had no place for him. He did, however, give him a suggestion. His son Andrew was a printer in Philadelphia, and perhaps his shop could use an extra hand.

And so Benjamin set out again. He took a boat across the bay toward Perth Amboy, and he met bad weather. A storm tore the sail, and the little boat was driven toward Long Island, and one passenger, a drunken Dutchman, fell into the sea. Benjamin caught him by the hair and pulled him back into the boat. The man dried off, took a book out of his pocket, and asked Benjamin to dry it for him. Franklin looked at it, and it was a copy of Pilgrim's Progress, in Dutch, the very book he had loved as a boy.

He landed, and walked, in the rain, across New Jersey, for many miles. He took another boat down the river, and, at last, on a quiet morning, he came to Philadelphia.

This is one of the most famous pictures in the story of his life, and it comes from his own words. He had been traveling for days, and he was dirty, and tired, and his pockets were full of stockings and shirts, so that they bulged. He walked up Market Street, from the river. He was hungry, and he had little money, and he had not eaten properly for a long time. He stopped at a bakery, and asked for three pennyworth of bread. The baker handed him three large rolls, much bigger than he had expected. He had no room to carry them, so he put one roll under each arm, and walked up the street, eating the third.

And as he walked, a young woman stood at the door of a house and watched him go by. Her name was Deborah Read. Franklin himself wrote that he made, as he certainly did, a most awkward and ridiculous appearance. He could not have known that the girl who smiled at him from a doorway would one day become his wife.

He walked on, until he reached a Quaker meeting house. He was so tired that he went inside, and sat down, and fell asleep. Nobody disturbed him. It was a quiet room, full of silent people, and when the meeting ended, someone gently woke him up. And that was how Benjamin Franklin, who would become the most famous citizen of Philadelphia, first entered the city. Asleep, in a Quaker meeting, with a loaf of bread, with no friends and few shillings, in the autumn of his seventeenth year.

Let us pause on that picture, and let it be still for a moment. The young man is sleeping, in the soft light of a quiet room, with the sounds of the city outside, and nobody there to know who he will be.

Now, the next days were practical ones. He found a place to stay, in a boarding house. He met a printer, an older man named Samuel Keimer, who had just opened a small shop with an old press and very little skill, and who gave Benjamin a job. He also found a lodging with Deborah's family, the Reads, and so he began to see more of the young woman who had watched him pass.

But there was another kind of surprise for him. Some weeks later, Benjamin received a visit from a very important man indeed. The governor of Pennsylvania, Sir William Keith, had heard about the clever young printer from a letter, and he came to the shop, and he spoke warmly to him. Keith was impressed. He praised him, and encouraged him, and said that he should set up as a printer on his own. He suggested that Benjamin should go to England to buy a press and type, and promised to write letters of introduction and credit, to help him with the money.

Franklin was a young man of eighteen, and he had left his family in anger, and he was flattered, and he believed the governor. He went home to Boston first, on a visit, to ask his father for help with the plan. His father, a careful man, thought that his son was too young to start a business, and he said no, but he was glad to see him alive and well, and he sent him back to Philadelphia with his blessing and a good deal of advice.

And so, a few months later, Benjamin Franklin prepared to sail for London.

But you might wonder what became of the governor's letters. We shall hear about those in the next chapter. For now, let us leave the young man where he is: in a small room above a printing shop, in the evening, with the river outside the window, and the whole of his life still in front of him.
