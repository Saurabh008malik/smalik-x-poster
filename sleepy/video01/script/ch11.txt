## Chapter 11: Revolution, and Paris

On the fifth of May, in seventeen hundred and seventy-five, Franklin landed in Philadelphia. The war had already begun. Fighting had broken out at Lexington and Concord, in Massachusetts, a few weeks before.

The very next day, he was chosen as a delegate to the Second Continental Congress. He was sixty-nine years old, which made him one of the oldest men in the room, and one of the best known. He had spent a lifetime in the service of his town and his colony, and now he was asked to serve a country that did not yet exist.

He went to work. He served on committees, and helped to organize the postal service of the new states. He was asked to draw up a plan of union for the colonies, much as he had proposed at Albany twenty years before.

In June of seventeen hundred and seventy-six, Congress appointed a committee of five to prepare a Declaration of Independence. Franklin was one of the five, along with Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Roger Sherman, and Robert Livingston. Jefferson wrote the first draft. Franklin was suffering from gout and from painful boils at the time, and he was not often at the meetings, but he read the draft, and he is traditionally credited with a small change, in which Jefferson's words about truths being sacred and undeniable became the words we know, that these truths are self-evident.

The Declaration was adopted on the fourth of July, and in the months that followed, the war went badly for the new country. It needed money, and weapons, and friends. And so, at the age of seventy, Franklin was asked to cross the ocean again, this time to France.

He sailed in October of seventeen hundred and seventy-six, on a small, fast warship, the Reprisal. It was cold, and the voyage was rough, and he was not well when he landed on the coast of Brittany, in December. He took with him two of his grandsons, William Temple Franklin, who was sixteen, and Benjamin Franklin Bache, who was seven. They traveled together to Paris.

And here something remarkable happened. The French people took Franklin to their hearts. They had read about his experiments, and they thought of him as a philosopher of nature, and a plain, wise American from the forest. He did not wear a powdered wig, as the fashionable men of Paris did. He wore his own gray hair, and a simple brown coat, and a cap made of fur. His face appeared on medals, and on snuffboxes, and on rings. Poets wrote about him. In the salons of Paris, he was welcomed by clever and charming people, and he enjoyed it a great deal.

He lived in a pleasant house in the village of Passy, just outside the city, which had a garden, and a view over the river Seine. He set up a small printing press there, and he wrote, and he received visitors, and he worked for years, quietly, to win France to the American cause.

It was not easy. France was a kingdom, and its king had little reason to love a revolution. At first, the French helped in secret, by sending supplies. They were waiting to see whether the Americans could win. Then, in December of seventeen hundred and seventy-seven, news arrived of a great American victory at Saratoga, in New York. And the mood in Paris changed. On the sixth of February, in seventeen hundred and seventy-eight, France signed a treaty of alliance with the United States, and a treaty of friendship and trade. Franklin signed with the other American commissioners.

The alliance brought ships, and soldiers, and money, and it was a turning point of the war. Without it, historians agree, the story might have ended very differently.

Even then, the work was not finished. The war went on for years. Franklin stayed in France, working through the long negotiations, and he was joined in the talks by John Adams and John Jay. At last, on the third of September in seventeen hundred and eighty-three, the Treaty of Paris was signed, and Great Britain recognized the independence of the United States.

In those same years, he found time to be curious. In the autumn of seventeen hundred and eighty-three, he watched, with wonder, as the first people rose into the sky in a balloon. Someone near him is said to have asked what use such a thing could be. Franklin is said to have answered by asking what use a newborn baby is. Whether or not he said it, it sounds like him.

By now, he was nearly eighty. His health was poor, he was in pain from a stone in the bladder, and he longed for home. He asked Congress to let him come back. In the summer of seventeen hundred and eighty-five, the permission came. He left Passy in July, and he was carried to the coast in a litter, borne by mules, because the jolting of a carriage was too painful.

He longed, in those days of waiting, to see his family and his own country once more.

In the last chapter, we will follow him home.
