## Chapter 3: The Apprentice and Silence Dogood

In the print shop, Benjamin had found a new teacher, though it was not a person. It was a book called The Spectator.

The Spectator was a collection of short essays, written in London by two men named Joseph Addison and Richard Steele. The essays were light, clear, and graceful, and they were famous for the elegant way they spoke about ordinary life. Benjamin thought that the writing was excellent, and he wanted to write like that himself. But how could a young apprentice, who had left school at ten, learn to do it?

He invented a method. He took one of the essays, and read it carefully, and wrote down short notes about what each sentence said. Then he put the book away for several days. When some time had passed, he tried to write the essay again, in his own words, from his notes alone. And then he took out the original, and compared the two, and found his faults, and corrected them.

Sometimes he jumbled the notes, to see whether he could put the ideas in the best order. Sometimes he turned an essay into verse, and then back into prose, to learn how to find the right words. It was a patient, quiet way of teaching himself, and he did it in the early mornings before work, and in the evenings, and on Sundays, when he should have been at church.

There was another small change in his life around this time. At the age of sixteen, he happened to read a book which argued that people should not eat meat. He decided to try it, and he became a vegetarian. His brother James had to pay for his meals at a boarding house, and Benjamin offered to take half of the money and to feed himself, and the arrangement worked well. He lived on bread, on potatoes, on rice, and on a handful of raisins, and he found that this simple food was cheap, and that he could spend the savings on books. He also found, he wrote, that his mind seemed clearer. He would eat his small meal quickly, and have the remaining part of the lunch hour to read in the empty print shop.

Meanwhile, in the year seventeen hundred and twenty-one, his brother James began a newspaper. It was called the New-England Courant, and it was only the fourth newspaper in the American colonies. It was a daring paper, which poked fun at some of the important people of Boston, and the most important people did not always enjoy this. Benjamin worked on it too. He set the type, and he printed the pages, and then he went out into the streets, with the papers in his arms, to sell them to the people of Boston.

Some of the writers who wrote for the Courant were clever friends of his brother, and they met at the print shop and talked and laughed. Benjamin listened, and wished he could be one of them. But he was only a boy, an apprentice, and he was sure that James would never print anything that he wrote, if he knew it was his.

So he decided on a gentle trick.

He invented a person. Her name was Silence Dogood, and she was a widow, middle-aged, living in the countryside, with a good heart, a lively wit, and strong opinions. He wrote a letter in her name, and he disguised his handwriting, and late one night, he slid the letter under the door of the printing house. In the morning, the friends of his brother found it, and read it, and they liked it. They discussed who might have written it. They guessed at the names of some of the wise men of the town. And without knowing that the writer was the quiet boy at the next table, they printed it.

That was in the spring of seventeen hundred and twenty-two, and Benjamin was sixteen years old. Over the next months, Silence Dogood wrote fourteen letters in all. She wrote about the problems of the town, about the manners of fashionable people, about the way young men were educated, and about the wish that a woman might have a voice. She wrote with humor and with kindness, and the readers of Boston enjoyed her very much. Some of them even wrote to the newspaper to say that they would like to marry her.

When the fourteenth letter was done, Benjamin revealed himself to his brother and to his friends. Some of them were delighted. His brother James was not.

You see, James was an older brother, who was also the master of the shop, and he was easily annoyed. Benjamin later wrote honestly that he thought James treated him harshly, and that James sometimes beat him. He also admitted that he himself was probably a little proud and a little saucy, and that he might have provoked some of it. When the praise began to fall on the apprentice, James was jealous, and the relations between them grew worse.

Then something happened which changed the whole situation. The Courant printed something that offended the colonial government, and James was put in jail for a month. And some months later, he was forbidden to print the paper at all. How could the paper go on?

The brothers made a plan. The Courant would be printed in the name of Benjamin Franklin. To make this work, James had to give Benjamin his indenture papers, and release him from the apprenticeship, at least officially. But they signed a new secret agreement in private, which kept him bound for the rest of the term.

For a few months, then, a seventeen-year-old boy was the publisher of a newspaper. And that gave him something valuable. He had a sense of what it meant to stand up, in public, and to be responsible for words that other people would read.

But the bond between the brothers did not mend. They quarreled again, and James beat him again, and eventually Benjamin did something which, in those days, was a very serious thing. He decided that he would not stay. If the secret agreement came out, James would have to admit that he had cheated the government, and so the boy felt that James could not easily take him to court. He thought that he could simply leave.

In the next chapter, we will follow him as he does. For now, let us rest for a while with a quieter picture: a boy, at a table, in the early morning, rewriting an old essay in his own words, one small sentence at a time.
