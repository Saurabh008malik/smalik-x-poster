## Chapter 7: A Family, a Fire, and a Loss

As the years passed, the printer on Market Street was becoming a man of some standing in his town. And as his business grew, so did his household.

A son named William was born around the year seventeen hundred and thirty, or shortly after. The records of his birth do not tell us who his mother was, and Franklin never said, but he brought the boy up in his own house, and Deborah, who may have been wounded by the story, accepted him and raised him as her own. William grew into a clever, handsome young man, and his father was proud of him, and took him along on many of his adventures. We will meet him again, in a later chapter.

In the autumn of seventeen hundred and thirty-two, a second son was born, whom they named Francis Folger Franklin, after his father and after his grandmother's family. He was a bright and lively little boy, and he was very much loved by his parents.

But in the year seventeen hundred and thirty-six, there was an outbreak of smallpox in Philadelphia. It was a terrible disease, and it killed many children in those times. Little Francis, who was only four years old, caught it, and in November of that year he died.

Franklin was heartbroken. Many years later, in the story of his life, he wrote about it with great honesty. He said that he had long regretted bitterly that he had not given the boy the inoculation, which was a way of protecting a person from the disease. He had been afraid of the risk. He wrote that he mentioned it for the sake of parents who held back from inoculation, fearing that they could never forgive themselves if a child died under it. His own example, he said, showed that the regret may be the same either way, and that the safer course should therefore be chosen.

When you are quiet, and the house is dim, it may be a comfort to know that even the greatest of men were also tender, and that they also grieved.

Some years later, in seventeen hundred and forty-three, Franklin and Deborah had a daughter, Sarah, who was called Sally, and she grew up to be a spirited young woman, devoted to her parents, and very much the pride of her father.

But let us go back to the years in between, when Franklin's mind was busy with the care of his town.

Philadelphia, like all towns of wooden houses and open fires, was in danger of burning. A fire could start in a kitchen, and in the course of a night, could take a whole street. There was no regular fire service, and the neighbors had to help one another, with buckets and ladders. Franklin had written about the matter in his newspaper. And in seventeen hundred and thirty-six, he organized the Union Fire Company, a group of volunteers who pledged to come to each other's aid, and to keep leather buckets and bags and baskets ready. It was so successful that other companies followed. He also helped to organize a night watch for the town, and he encouraged the paving of the streets.

In the following year, he was appointed postmaster of Philadelphia. It was a modest job, with a small salary, but it gave him a chance to see letters and newspapers from all over the colonies, and to improve the way they were carried. And it taught him a great deal about the ways in which the thirteen colonies were connected, and how they were not.

He was also interested in warmth. In the winters of Pennsylvania, the open fireplaces of the day were very inefficient. They sent most of their heat up the chimney, and left people cold, with a smoky room. Franklin designed a new kind of iron stove, which stood out in the room, and which made the fire burn more efficiently, so that a room could be kept warm with far less wood. It was called the Pennsylvania fireplace, and then, in time, the Franklin stove.

The governor, Mr. Thomas, was so pleased with it that he offered to give Franklin a patent, which would have given him the sole right to sell it for years. Franklin declined. He wrote that, as we enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours, and that this we should do freely and generously. He did not want to make money from it. He printed a pamphlet which explained how the stove worked, and let anyone build one.

This was something quite typical of him. And it brings us to a quiet, important moment in his life. By the year seventeen hundred and forty-eight, Franklin was forty-two years old, and his printing house was doing so well that he did not need to work in it every day. He took a partner, a hard-working young Scotsman named David Hall, who would run the business and give him a share of the profits for many years. And so, he decided to retire from daily trade, as he put it, to have time for study, for experiments, and for the service of the public.

At forty-two, he had become a man of leisure. He had a good house, a loyal wife, two living children, and many friends. He had read a great deal, and he had begun to wonder about the way the world worked.

In the next chapter, we will follow his curiosity into a new field of study, one that was strange, and new, and a little dangerous, and which came to be known as electricity.
