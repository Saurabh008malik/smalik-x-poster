## Chapter 6: Building a Life in Philadelphia

Now we come to a part of the story which is, in its way, quite peaceful. It is the story of a young man making a place for himself in a small, growing town.

Philadelphia, in those years, was a city of perhaps ten thousand people, laid out in tidy squares by the Quaker founder, William Penn. It was a place of brick houses and wide streets, of shops and wharves and meeting houses. It was a place where a person with a skill and a good name could do well.

Franklin went back to work for his old master, Samuel Keimer, for a short time, and then he left to start his own printing business, with a partner named Hugh Meredith, whose father put up some of the money. It was a small shop, with a press and a case of type, and two hard-working young men. They worked very late at night. Franklin said that people in the neighborhood heard the sound of his press at ten o'clock, when the club of merchants met nearby, and again early in the morning, and they took it as a sign that the new printer was a man of industry. He understood how to build a reputation. He dressed plainly. He never went fishing or shooting for pleasure. He sometimes wheeled a roll of paper through the street in a barrow, so that everyone could see that he was working.

In the autumn of seventeen hundred and twenty-seven, he had also started something very different. He gathered a group of his friends, mostly young tradesmen like himself, a surveyor, a shoemaker, a cabinetmaker, a clerk, and they began to meet on Friday evenings. They called their club the Junto, which is a word meaning a small group joined for a common purpose. They read to each other, and they asked questions, about morals, about politics, about the natural world, and each member was supposed to bring forward some thoughts of his own. They were also asked a list of questions, such as whether they had heard of any good that could be done for the town.

It was a simple club, but it had a lasting effect. A great many of the good works of Philadelphia grew from the Junto, and Franklin was at the center of it.

The first of these good works was a library. In those days, books were expensive and hard to get, and most people in the colonies had few. The members of the Junto each had a few books of their own, and they thought that they might share them. And then Franklin thought of something larger. In seventeen hundred and thirty-one, he proposed that a group of people should each pay a small sum to join, and a smaller sum each year, and that the money should be used to buy books for all of them to read. This was the Library Company of Philadelphia. It began with about fifty subscribers, and it still exists today. Franklin, many years later, called it the mother of all the subscription libraries in North America.

In the meantime, his business was growing. He wrote a pamphlet in favor of printing paper money, which the colony needed, and when the government agreed, he was given the work of printing it. He also bought a newspaper, the Pennsylvania Gazette, in seventeen hundred and twenty-nine, which he made into the best-written paper in the colonies, with clear, lively prose, and a good deal of humor. It soon became very successful.

Now we should say something about his private life, for it was in these years that he built a home. Deborah Read had married a man named John Rogers, but the marriage had not been happy. Rogers had left her, and had gone away, and it was said that he had died in the West Indies, though nobody was quite sure. Because of this, Deborah and Benjamin could not easily marry in the church. But in September of seventeen hundred and thirty, they did something that was common in those days. They simply agreed to live together as husband and wife, and they did so for the next forty-four years.

It was not a romantic match, as we might think of it, but it was a loyal and a practical one. Deborah worked in the shop, kept the accounts, sold paper and ink and soap, and managed the household. She was not a great reader, and she did not like to travel, but Franklin called her his "dear Debby," and wrote to her fondly all the days he was away.

It is in these years, too, that he did something rather unusual. He decided to try to improve himself, in an orderly way, as one might improve a piece of work. He made a list of thirteen virtues, and gave a name to each: temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility. He kept a small book, with a page for each, and a chart, and every evening he made a mark where he had failed. He would work on one virtue for a week, and then move on to the next.

He never reached perfection, and he knew he would not. Of order, in particular, he said that he was never able to make himself tidy, and that he had come to think that a streaky, spotted axe, which keeps its edge, is as good as a bright one. And of humility, he said, with a wry smile, that even if he thought he had overcome pride, he would probably be proud of his humility. But he said that the effort made him a better and happier man than he would otherwise have been.

And then, in December of seventeen hundred and thirty-two, he began something which would make him famous. He wrote an almanac. An almanac was a yearly book with a calendar, the phases of the moon, the weather forecasts, and some amusing stories and sayings, and nearly every household owned one. Franklin's was published under the name of a made-up astronomer, Richard Saunders, and it was called Poor Richard's Almanack.

It was filled with short, clever sayings, about money and work and good sense. "Early to bed, and early to rise," it said, "makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise." Many of the sayings were not new, and he did not pretend that they were. He gathered them from older proverbs, and polished them, and made them sharper. The almanac sold in the thousands every year, and he published it for twenty-five years.

Let us rest here for a moment. The press is quiet in the evening, the shop is closed, and a young man sits at a table with a small book, and writes a mark beside the word order. Outside, the town is settling to sleep.
