## Chapter 9: The Builder of Common Things

While Franklin was exploring the secrets of lightning, he had not forgotten his town. In fact, the years between seventeen hundred and forty-three and seventeen hundred and fifty-four are, in a way, the years in which he did some of his quietest and most lasting work.

Think back to the Junto, the club of friends who met on Friday evenings to talk about how to improve themselves and their city. Out of that small circle, ideas kept coming, and Franklin had a gift for turning an idea into a plan, and a plan into a gathering of willing people.

In seventeen hundred and forty-three, he proposed the founding of a society for the study of science, the American Philosophical Society, which would bring together curious men from the different colonies, to share their discoveries by letter and in meetings. It is still in existence today, in Philadelphia, and it is the oldest learned society in the United States.

A few years later, he turned his attention to education. In his own life, he had learned most things for himself, and he wished that more young people could have a good education, which was useful as well as ornamental. He wrote a short pamphlet, which he called Proposals Relating to the Education of Youth, in which he suggested a school that would teach not only the old languages but also English, writing, history, mathematics, and the practical knowledge of the trades. The school, called the Academy, opened in seventeen hundred and fifty-one, and it grew, over the years, into the University of Pennsylvania.

His next project was a hospital. The poor of Philadelphia, when they were ill, had no place to be cared for, and one of Franklin's friends, a physician named Dr. Thomas Bond, had been trying to raise money for one, without much luck. Franklin took up the cause. He wrote about it in his newspaper, and he went from house to house asking for donations. When the response was slow, he came up with a clever idea. He asked the government of the colony to promise a gift of two thousand pounds, but only if the private citizens of Philadelphia had first raised the same amount. The people who opposed the hospital thought that they had nothing to lose, because they thought the sum would never be raised. But the idea of a matching gift encouraged the generous, and the money was found. The Pennsylvania Hospital was founded in seventeen hundred and fifty-one, it received its first patients soon afterward, and it is still caring for patients today.

He also paid attention to the less glamorous side of city life. He helped to organize the paving and cleaning of the streets. When he found that the city's street lamps gave a poor light, because the globes grew sooty so quickly, he designed a better lamp, with four flat panes of glass and a chimney at the top to draw out the smoke. It burned with a clearer flame, and it was easier to clean. Nobody remembers the name of the man who made the first clean street lamp. But the Philadelphians had a little more light on their evenings, and a little less soot on their shoes.

He took on a new public job, too. In seventeen hundred and fifty-three, the crown appointed him, together with another man, to be the deputy postmaster general for all the northern colonies. He traveled widely to inspect the routes, and he reorganized the service, so that letters traveled faster, and the post office, which had been losing money, began to make a profit. It also meant that he had seen, with his own eyes, how the colonies were scattered along the coast, and how little the people of one colony knew of another.

That thought stayed with him. In the spring of seventeen hundred and fifty-four, a war was beginning, with the French and their allies, over the land beyond the mountains. The colonies were being asked to defend themselves, and they were not good at working together. Franklin printed in his newspaper a small drawing of a snake cut into pieces, each labeled with the name of a colony, and beneath it three words: Join, or die. It is one of the first political cartoons in American history.

And in June of that year, he went to Albany, in the colony of New York, where delegates from several colonies were meeting with the leaders of the Iroquois nations. Franklin brought a plan for a union of the colonies, under a grand council and a president appointed by the crown, with the power to raise soldiers and build forts, and to look after the frontier. The delegates approved it. But when it was sent back home, the colonial assemblies thought that it gave too much power to the central government, and the crown in London thought that it gave too much to the colonies. It was turned down on both sides of the ocean.

Franklin later said that if his plan had been accepted, the troubles which followed might have been avoided. He may have been right, and he may not. But the seed had been sown. The idea of a union of the colonies had been spoken aloud, and the man who spoke it would help, twenty years later, to turn it into reality.

Now, he was nearly fifty years old. He had made a fortune in business, a reputation in science, and many friends in his town. He might have chosen to stay at home, with Deborah, in the house on Market Street, and to do quiet experiments, and enjoy his garden. But the colony of Pennsylvania had a problem, and the Assembly was looking for a man to send across the ocean to solve it.

And in the next chapter, we will follow him, once again, to the great city on the Thames, though this time, he will not be a runaway printer with no letters in his pocket. He will be a famous man.
