## Chapter 12: Cloux, and Goodnight

The last winter at Cloux passed quietly. Leonardo was now in his late sixties, which was a great age in the sixteenth century. His hand was weak, his steps were slow, and he spent many hours in his chair. But he was not idle. He wrote and sorted his papers. He made drawings of the water and the sky. He talked with the visitors who came to the house, and with the young men who looked after him. He had not stopped looking.

By the spring of fifteen hundred and nineteen, he knew that the end was near. On the twenty-third of April, he made his will, in the presence of a notary and several witnesses, at Cloux. It was a calm and careful document, written in the spirit of a man who has put his house in order.

He asked that his body be buried in the church of Saint-Florentin, in the castle of Amboise, and that sixty poor men carry candles at his funeral. He asked for masses to be said in several churches. He gave generously to the people he loved. To Salaì, who had been with him since he was a boy, he left half of his vineyard outside the walls of Milan, together with the house which Salaì had built on it. To his servant, a woman named Maturina, he left a cloak of fine black cloth, and a small sum of money. And to Francesco Melzi, the faithful companion of his last years, he left everything that mattered most to him: his books, his instruments, his drawings, his manuscripts, and the remaining sum of his pension. Melzi was named the executor of the will.

It was an act of trust, and Melzi kept it. Without him, we might know very little about what was in Leonardo's mind.

On the second day of May, in fifteen hundred and nineteen, in the manor of Cloux, Leonardo da Vinci died. He was sixty-seven years old. He died, it appears, at home in his bed, in the company of friends.

A famous story, told by Vasari, says that the king himself was present, and held the old man's head in his arms as he died. It is a moving picture, and it has been painted many times, most famously by the French artist Ingres. But it probably did not happen like this. The records suggest that the king was elsewhere on that day, at another castle. Still, the story shows what people wanted to believe, that the great king and the great artist were close, and that the old master was not alone.

In a letter that Melzi wrote to Leonardo's half-brothers in Florence, he told them of the death, with a heavy heart. He wrote that Leonardo had been to him like the best of fathers, that the loss was a grief too great for words, and that for as long as his own body held together he would carry an endless sorrow, because Leonardo had shown him such warm and daily love. It is a rare and touching letter, and it tells us that the man we have followed through this night was loved.

He was buried, as he had asked, in the church of Saint-Florentin. The church stood within the castle grounds, and it was torn down in the years after the French Revolution, and its stones were carried away. Today no one knows exactly where his grave lies. What stands now is a small chapel in the castle grounds, where a tomb is marked with his name. Whether his remains truly rest there, the experts cannot say. It seems fitting, somehow, that a man who left so little behind of the ordinary kind should have left so much of another kind.

What did he leave? First, a handful of paintings, perhaps fewer than twenty that we can be fairly sure are his. They are among the most admired things in the world. The Mona Lisa passed into the collection of the kings of France, and now hangs in the Louvre, in Paris. The Last Supper is still on its wall in Milan, faded and carefully protected. The Lady with an Ermine is in Kraków, and the Virgin of the Rocks is in Paris and in London.

Second, he left his notebooks, thousands of pages that carry his drawings, his questions and his dreams. They were scattered after Melzi died, in fifteen hundred and seventy, and were found again, page by page, over centuries. Some of them are in Milan, some in Paris, in Windsor, in Madrid. Each time one is opened, we hear him think.

And third, he left an example. Not of an answer, but of a way of looking. He showed that a person can ask a question about a bird or a river or a bone, and give it all of their attention, and that this attention is itself a form of love for the world. He was not perfect. He left many things unfinished. He was restless, and sometimes he disappointed those who depended on him. But he never stopped being curious, and he never stopped trying to see more clearly. That is a gift to all of us, whether we paint, or build, or only watch the clouds go by.

The fire is low now, and the house is quiet. Outside, the river Loire runs on in the dark, as it did on the night he died, past the willows and the sandbanks and the sleeping town. The candle on the table has burned down. A last page lies open beside it, written in a small hand, from right to left, and the ink is dry. Somewhere over the water a bird is calling, once, softly, and then it is still.

Thank you for spending this night with Leonardo da Vinci. If you are still awake, rest easy. And if you are drifting off, sleep well. Goodnight.
