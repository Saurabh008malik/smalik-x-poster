## Chapter 2: Looking at the World

A boy who grows up in the hills has a great deal to look at. The seasons turn slowly, and every one has its own colors. In winter, the vineyards are bare and brown, and mist lies in the valley below. In spring, the fields are bright with new wheat. In summer the air hums with insects, and the stones of the walls are warm to the touch. In autumn, the grapes are gathered, and the olives, and the smell of pressed oil drifts out of the farm doors.

We do not have many facts about Leonardo's childhood. Nobody wrote his story down while he was a boy, and the records that remain are mostly tax returns, contracts and family notes. But later, when he was a grown man with notebooks of his own, he wrote and drew about things which he must have first noticed when he was young. He drew the way water curls around a stone. He drew the flowers of the Tuscan fields, and the folds of hills, and the shapes of clouds. He wrote about the flight of birds, and about shells and fossils found high on the slopes, far from any sea. It seems very likely that these were questions which began in his childhood, when he walked out with his uncle and looked at the land around him.

Of his schooling, we know less than we would like. It seems that he learned to read and write in his own Italian, and to do the arithmetic that a merchant's or a notary's household needed. He did not receive the long classical education in Latin which was given to the sons of the great families, and which was the doorway to the universities. Later in life, he would sometimes call himself a man without letters, and in his notebooks you can find him teaching himself Latin words, copying lists of them carefully, as an adult. He learned many things this way, slowly, by his own effort, because he wanted to know them.

What he did seem to do, from very early, was draw.

There is a story about this, told many years later by a writer named Giorgio Vasari, who collected the lives of the Italian artists in a famous book. It is a charming story, and we should tell it, but with a little care, because Vasari wrote more than thirty years after Leonardo's death, and he liked a good tale. In his account, a local peasant asked Ser Piero to have a round wooden shield painted, and Ser Piero gave the job to his son. Leonardo, it is said, filled the shield with a fearsome creature, a monster of his own invention, which he made from lizards and bats and snakes and other things he had gathered. When his father came to see it, he was so startled that he drew back. Leonardo smiled, because he had wanted that exact effect.

Whether it happened exactly like this, nobody can say. But the story tells us what people believed about him: that he had a gift for making things look real, and a delight in the strange.

Another tradition says that Ser Piero, noticing his son's talent, took some of the boy's drawings to a friend in Florence, a sculptor and painter named Andrea del Verrocchio. The friend looked at them and was very impressed. This also comes from Vasari, and it may be partly a story. But the outline of it is almost certainly right. Sometime in the fourteen sixties, when Leonardo was in his teens, he left the hills and went to Florence, to learn a trade, in the workshop of one of the best teachers in the city.

By the year fourteen hundred and sixty-nine, we find the family records placing him in Florence. His father had by then moved into a house there, and Leonardo seems to have lived at first with him. The young man was about seventeen. For the first time in his life, he was in a great city.

Let us imagine, for a moment, what that might have been like.

Florence in those years was one of the richest and most beautiful cities in Europe. It stood in a bowl of green hills, on the banks of the river Arno, with a high wall around it and many towers inside. Its streets were crowded with merchants, bankers and weavers, because wool and silk made the city wealthy. On the skyline rose the great red dome of the cathedral, which had been finished only a few decades before, and which was the largest dome since ancient Rome. Sculptors worked on statues for the churches. Painters decorated the chapels. In the narrow lanes, the shops of goldsmiths and cloth dyers stood side by side, and from morning to evening there was the sound of hammers.

For a boy from the hills, it must have seemed like a great, loud, glittering machine. There was a new kind of beauty in it, a human kind, made of stone and paint and metal, and we may suppose that he looked at it with the same long, patient attention which he had given to the birds and the streams.

He would have walked through the Piazza della Signoria, with the old city hall rising like a tower of golden stone. He would have crossed the Ponte Vecchio, where the butchers' stalls leaned out over the river. He would have gone into the churches, and looked up at the frescoes painted by the great masters of the previous generation, with their calm, solid figures and their clear light.

And somewhere near the center of this city stood the workshop he was going to join.

Before we go in, it is worth noticing one thing. All his life, Leonardo kept the hills with him. In the background of his paintings, you will often find a landscape of winding rivers and blue distant mountains, with rocks rising from the water and mist lying in the hollows. It is not the real landscape of Vinci, exactly. But it carries its air. A man can leave the place where he was born, and travel across a continent, and still, when he closes his eyes, there are hills in them.

For now, though, the evening is falling over Florence. The lamps are being lit in the windows. The bells of the churches speak to one another across the rooftops. A young man from Vinci is walking home along the river, in the blue dusk, with a drawing under his arm.
