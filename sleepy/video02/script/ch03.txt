## Chapter 3: The Workshop of Verrocchio

In the Florence of Leonardo's youth, an artist was not a lonely person in an attic. He was a craftsman, and he worked in a bottega, which is the Italian word for a workshop. A bottega was a busy place. It had a master at its head, and assistants, and apprentices, and sometimes a dog sleeping by the door. It took in orders from churches, from merchants, from the great banking family of the Medici, and from anyone who could pay, and it produced everything from altarpieces to banners, from marble tombs to a silver cup for a wedding.

The master of the bottega that Leonardo entered was Andrea del Verrocchio. Verrocchio was a goldsmith by training, and a sculptor in bronze and marble, and a painter as well. He was a skilled and thoughtful teacher, and his workshop became one of the best schools of art in Italy. Several of the painters who would later become famous passed through it, or through its neighborhood, and the same young men learned from one another every day.

An apprentice began at the bottom. In the first years, he did not paint. He swept the floors, he ground the colors from minerals and plants, he prepared the wooden panels with layers of glue and chalk, and he learned to make the brushes. He learned to draw, and he drew all the time, copying the master's designs, copying plaster casts, copying draped cloth hung on a frame, and copying one another. Drawing was the foundation of everything. A young man who could not draw well could not be trusted with a commission.

Leonardo seems to have taken to this life at once. He learned how to work in metal and in clay, and how to mix paint, and how to build up a figure from within, understanding the bones and muscles beneath the skin. He learned perspective, the art of making a flat surface seem deep. And he began to try ideas of his own. In the years he spent in the workshop, he would also become interested in machines, such as the cranes and pulleys used to raise the great copper ball that was placed on top of the cathedral dome in the fourteen seventies, a job in which Verrocchio's shop was involved.

There is a famous painting which came out of this workshop, called the Baptism of Christ. It shows Saint John pouring water over the head of Jesus, in a landscape beside a river, while two angels kneel on the bank. It was painted mostly by Verrocchio, but he did not do it alone, and historians believe that the young Leonardo painted part of it, especially the angel on the left, and some of the soft, hazy landscape in the background, and perhaps the water near the feet of Christ.

You can see the difference, if you look closely. The master's figures are firm and clear, almost like carved sculpture. The angel which is thought to be Leonardo's has a gentler look, a lighter touch, and the face seems to be listening, or just about to turn. The paint was laid on in thin, glowing layers, a technique with oil which was fairly new in Italy. The landscape in the distance dissolves into mist.

Vasari tells a story about this painting. He says that when Verrocchio saw how much better the young man's angel was than his own work, he was so humbled that he put down his brushes, and never painted again. Modern writers think this is almost certainly a legend. Verrocchio did not stop all at once. But it is true that he painted very little after this period, and he turned more and more to sculpture. Perhaps it is better to think of this as a story that people liked to tell, because it showed that the pupil had begun to surpass the teacher, which is the best thing a teacher can hope for.

In the year fourteen hundred and seventy-two, when Leonardo was twenty years old, his name was entered in the book of the painters' company of Florence, a guild named for Saint Luke, the patron saint of painters. This meant that he was recognized as a master painter, able to take commissions of his own. He did not leave Verrocchio's workshop, however. For some years more, he seems to have continued to work there, and to live in the master's circle.

Of his earliest paintings, a few remain. One of them, an Annunciation, shows the angel Gabriel kneeling before the Virgin Mary in a garden, with a low wall, a row of cypress trees, and a quiet landscape behind. The painting is polished and careful, a little stiff here and there, and it shows Verrocchio's influence. But in the grass, in the folds of the dress and in the delicate wings, you can see that someone is looking very closely at the world.

There is also a small drawing, dated by Leonardo himself to the fifth of August, fourteen hundred and seventy-three. It is a view of a green valley with a river, a castle on a hill, and a cliff in the foreground. It is a pen drawing, done quickly, probably on a walk. It is one of the earliest landscapes in European art that seems to have been drawn simply to record a place, without any religious or story purpose. Perhaps he was visiting his family in the hills when he made it. In any case, it shows us that the boy who loved the valley had become a man who could draw it.

Another early painting is a portrait of a young woman named Ginevra de' Benci, the daughter of a wealthy Florentine banker. She looks out of the picture, calm and a little distant, in front of a juniper bush, with her pale face and curled hair. The juniper is a play on her name, as the word for it in Italian is ginepro. It is a portrait of a person, but also a portrait of a mood, and it is among the first of the pictures in which Leonardo shows what would become his special gift, which was to paint not just a face, but a mind behind it.

The workshop was not always quiet. There were deadlines, and arguments over payment, and spoiled panels that had to be redone. There were apprentices who played jokes on one another, as apprentices always do. But there was also a feeling of being part of something. Everyone in that room was trying to see better, and to make what they saw more beautiful or more true.

By the end of this period, Leonardo had learned most of what Verrocchio could teach him. He was in his middle twenties. He was handsome, it is said, and strong, and good-humored, and he played the lyre, though these details come from later writers. The next step was to stand on his own.

The shop is closing now. The apprentices are putting away their brushes, and the last light is turning the white dust on the floor to gold. The sound of hammers outside is fading. One young man stays a little longer than the others, bent over a drawing of a curl of water, until the light is gone.

In the next chapter, we will see him leave the workshop, and take his first steps as a master of his own.
