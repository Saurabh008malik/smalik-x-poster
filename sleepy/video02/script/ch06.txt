## Chapter 6: The Notebooks

Wherever Leonardo went, he carried a notebook. Sometimes it was a small book, hung from his belt, and sometimes it was a loose sheet of paper, tucked into his sleeve. He wrote and drew in them constantly: things he saw in the street, thoughts that came to him in the night, lists of books and of debts, designs for machines, notes about people he wanted to remember. He sketched faces in the crowd, a horse's leg, the way a swirl of water turned a leaf. If a thought struck him, he wrote it down at once.

These pages are one of the great treasures of human history. When he died, he left behind thousands of them. Today, many of them survive, scattered in libraries and collections across Europe, and in the United States. One of the largest, the Codex Atlanticus, kept in Milan, holds over eleven hundred leaves of drawings and notes, from engineering to optics to the flight of birds. Another, the Codex Leicester, is about water, rocks and the heavens. No one else of his age left such a record of a mind at work.

You may have heard that he wrote his notes backwards, in mirror writing, so that they could only be read with a looking glass. It is true that he wrote from right to left, with the letters reversed. But the reason was probably simple, and not secret at all. Leonardo was left-handed. When a left-handed person writes from left to right with ink, the hand drags across the wet words and smears them. Writing from right to left kept the sleeve clear of the ink. To anyone who held a page up to a mirror, or who simply learned the trick, it was perfectly easy to read.

What did he write about? Almost everything. He wrote about how to paint a face, and how to paint a distant mountain, which, he noticed, looks blue because of the air between it and the eye. He wrote about the way light falls on a sphere. He wrote about water, which fascinated him above all, in rivers, in whirlpools, in waves, in the little curls it makes when it flows around a post. He drew plants and flowers with exact care. He studied the shapes of clouds. He asked questions, in endless lists, like a child. What is sleep? Why is the sky blue? How does a bird stay up? What is the tongue of a woodpecker like?

He believed in looking first. In his notes, he says again and again that experience, that is, careful observation of the real world, is the true teacher, and that arguments which do not rest upon it are likely to be wrong. This was an unusual idea in an age when many scholars preferred to learn from old books. Leonardo respected old books, and he read them, but he did not stop there. He wanted to see for himself.

Around the year fourteen hundred and ninety, in these Milan years, he made a drawing which has become one of the most reproduced images in the world. It shows a man standing with his arms and legs stretched out, inside both a circle and a square. We call it the Vitruvian Man, after an ancient Roman architect named Vitruvius, who had written that the human body fits perfectly into those shapes. Leonardo set out to test the idea with measurement, and he adjusted the figure to be more true to nature. It was an attempt to show that the human body is built with harmony and proportion, and that we are connected to the order of the universe. He added notes below, in his mirror writing, explaining the measurements.

He was also fascinated by flight. He spent years watching birds, especially kites, hawks and swifts, and he made notes about how their wings change shape in the air, how they glide, how they use the wind. From these studies he drew many designs for flying machines, some with flapping wings and some like a spiral screw. We do not know that he ever built one that left the ground. Most of these ideas could not have worked with the materials and the knowledge of his time. But they show a mind which asked how things could be done, and kept asking, long after the answer seemed out of reach. His drawings were not predictions. They were questions put on paper.

Many of his other inventions, such as a giant crossbow, an armored vehicle, and a diving suit, were also studies, and only a few of them were ever made. What survives is a kind of invitation to the future. Hundreds of years later, engineers still look at these pages with wonder.

In these years, Leonardo's household also grew. In July of fourteen hundred and ninety, a boy of about ten years old came to live with him. His name was Gian Giacomo Caprotti, and Leonardo called him Salaì, which means something like "little devil." It was a good nickname, as Salaì turned out to be a trial. Leonardo's notes, written a few days after the boy arrived, describe him as a thief, a liar, stubborn and greedy, and record a number of small thefts, from money to a silverpoint pencil. But the master was fond of him all the same. He bought him clothes, shoes and a fine cap, and Salaì stayed with him for the rest of his life, as an assistant, a painter of some small skill, a companion, and a kind of difficult family. It says something good about Leonardo that he kept the boy near him.

In the summer of fourteen hundred and ninety-three, there was a quieter arrival. In a short note in one of his books, dated the sixteenth of July, Leonardo wrote that a woman named Caterina had come to him. Scholars think she was his mother. She would have been an older woman now, and it seems that she lived with him for less than a year. Her funeral expenses are recorded in his notes, with the small items listed with care: the cross, the pall, the candles, the bearers, the priests, and the grave digging. It is a short list. But the fact that he wrote it down, in the same neat hand which recorded his grandest ideas, tells us that she had mattered, and that his love for her had been steady, through all the years between the hills of Vinci and the city of Milan.

The lamp on the table has burned low. The pages of the notebook rest, closed, beside an open inkwell. Outside, a cart rolls over the cobbles and the sound fades away into the night. Somewhere in the house, a boy turns over in his sleep.
