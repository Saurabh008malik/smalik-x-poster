## Chapter 1: Welcome, and the Boy from Vinci

Welcome. Let us settle in together. If you can, lower the lights a little, and let your shoulders rest. There is nothing you need to do tonight except listen. You can close your eyes whenever you like, and you do not have to remember anything. The story will be here, and we will go slowly.

Tonight, we will keep company with Leonardo da Vinci. He was a painter, an engineer, a draughtsman, a student of water and birds and the human heart, and one of the most curious people who ever lived. We will follow him from a farmhouse in the hills of Tuscany, to the workshops of Florence, to the court of Milan, and at last to a quiet house beside a river in France. It is a long life, and it is also a gentle one, and we will take our time.

Let us begin in the spring of fourteen hundred and fifty-two, in the hills of Tuscany, in a part of Italy that belonged then to the Republic of Florence.

Here, among the olive trees and the vines, stood a small hill town called Vinci. It was a place of stone houses and narrow lanes, with a castle at its top, and below it the fields went down in soft terraces toward a valley. In April the grass was green, and the almond blossom was over, and the evenings smelled of woodsmoke and warm earth.

On Saturday, the fifteenth of April, at about three o'clock in the night, a baby boy was born. We know the hour because his grandfather wrote it down. The grandfather's name was Antonio, and he kept a careful notebook, as many men of his family did. In it he wrote that his grandson had been born to his son, Ser Piero, on that night, and that the child was baptized by a priest of the town, with ten people standing as godparents. The boy was given the name Lionardo, the form of the name which was used in Tuscany at the time. We say Leonardo.

Historians still disagree a little about exactly where he was born. Tradition says it was in a small hamlet called Anchiano, a short walk from Vinci. Others think it may have been in the town itself. It does not matter very much tonight. He was born in or near Vinci, in the hills, and the hills were his first home.

His father, Ser Piero, was a notary. That was a respected profession in Italy, rather like a lawyer who writes contracts, wills and agreements, and the men of the family had been notaries for generations. Ser Piero was about twenty-five years old, and he worked mainly in the city of Florence, a day's walk away.

Leonardo's mother was a young woman named Caterina. Very little is certainly known about her. She appears in a few records, and we know that she was of modest background, and that she and Ser Piero were not married. Scholars have suggested different stories about who she was. One recent theory, based on tax records, proposes that she was a young orphan from the countryside nearby. Another, older idea imagined that she had come from far away. The truth is that nobody can say for sure, and so we will not pretend to. What we do know is that she is the mother of Leonardo, and that she mattered to him, and we will meet her again later in the story.

Because his parents were not married, Leonardo was what people at that time called illegitimate. In those days, this was not so shameful in everyday life, but it had real consequences in law. He could not follow his father into the notaries' guild, nor into some other professions, and he could not easily claim the family property. Some historians have wondered whether this closed door was a kind of gift. If he had been allowed to become a notary, he might have spent his life writing contracts. Because he was not, he was free to find another path.

Soon after he was born, Caterina married a local man, a farmer and kiln worker, and she went on to have other children with him. Ser Piero, too, married a woman of good family within a few years. Leonardo seems to have stayed, for most of his boyhood, with his father's family, in the house of his grandfather Antonio.

A tax record from the year fourteen hundred and fifty-seven shows us the household. It lists Antonio, and his wife Lucia, and Ser Piero's brother Francesco, and the family's land, and a little boy of five years old, written down plainly as the illegitimate son of Ser Piero. That small line in an old record is the first time we see the child as a member of a family.

It was an ordinary house, in an ordinary Tuscan town, but there was one person in it who mattered a great deal to Leonardo. His uncle Francesco, Ser Piero's younger brother, was a man who loved the countryside. He had no taste for the city or for law. He liked to walk in the fields, and look after the olives and the vines, and he seems to have been fond of his small nephew. In later years, he would name Leonardo in his will. It is a good thing to imagine, a young boy at the side of a patient uncle, walking out through the grass.

Let us walk with them for a while.

The path leaves the town and goes down between low stone walls. The air is warm, and there are bees in the rosemary. Ahead, the land falls away, and far off you can see a long blue valley, with a river shining in the middle of it and hills on every side, each one a little paler than the one before. A line of cypress trees stands against the sky. A kite, a gliding bird of prey, turns slowly above the fields, hardly moving its wings.

The boy stops to watch it. He watches for a long time. We cannot say, of course, exactly what he was thinking, and this scene is only our imagining. But we do know that he became a man who could look at a bird, or a stream, or a stone, for hours, and see something that other people missed. It is easy to believe that the habit began here, among these hills.

For tonight, that is enough. A boy in a hill town, with a notary for a father, and a grandfather's notebook, and an uncle who loved the land. The sun is going down behind the olive trees. The bees have gone home. Somewhere a bell is ringing, very softly, and the lane is quiet.

In the next chapter, we will follow the boy a little further, and see how a child who loved to look found the work he was meant to do.
