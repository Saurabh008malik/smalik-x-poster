## Chapter 8: The Traveler

In the autumn of fourteen hundred and ninety-nine, the world that Leonardo had known in Milan came to an end. The French king, Louis the Twelfth, who had a claim to the duchy of Milan through his grandmother, sent an army across the Alps. The Milanese had little heart to resist. Ludovico Sforza, the duke whose court Leonardo had served for seventeen years, fled northward to seek help from the emperor. In September the French marched into the city.

Leonardo watched all this from his rooms, with a sense, we may suppose, of how quickly a world can turn. His patron was gone. His great clay horse, as we have heard, was damaged by the invaders. His supper on the wall at the convent had survived, and it was admired by the French king himself, who is said to have wondered whether the whole wall could be cut out and carried home to France. It could not be done, of course, and the painting stayed.

For a man in his late forties, with no home of his own and no steady employer, this was a moment of uncertainty. Leonardo packed his drawings and his notebooks, and set out on the road. He had sent some of his savings ahead to a bank in Florence. He took with him his old friend, the mathematician Luca Pacioli, who had been teaching him geometry. And together, in the last days of that year, they traveled eastward.

The first stop was the city of Mantua, the home of Isabella d'Este, a young marchioness who was one of the greatest patrons of her age. She was clever, curious and determined, with a famous collection of art and antiquities. She had already, years before, asked Leonardo to paint a portrait of her. Now, during his visit, he made a large preparatory drawing, a profile view in black chalk, with a touch of red, and it is an elegant study of a poised young woman. But the portrait in paint was never made. Isabella wrote to him again and again over the following years, in letters that grew more and more polite and more and more impatient, asking him to paint her, or even just to paint any small picture of the Virgin, and he did not. This was Leonardo as we know him. Great ideas always came with a delay.

From Mantua he went to Venice, the proud city on the water, where he was asked for advice on defense against a possible Turkish attack by sea. He proposed a system of flooding the river valley of the Isonzo, which could be done by opening the sluices of a dam. We do not know whether his advice was used. But it shows how readily people turned to him when a problem demanded a clear mind.

In the spring of fifteen hundred, he came home to Florence. He was welcomed, and the friars of the church of Santissima Annunziata gave him workspace, and he began to work on a cartoon, a full-size drawing, of the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne. Vasari tells us that people crowded in to see it, as they might to a festival, men and women, young and old, coming to look at the drawing as at a marvel.

But in the summer of fifteen hundred and two, his life changed course again. He entered the service of Cesare Borgia.

Cesare Borgia was the son of Pope Alexander the Sixth. He was clever, handsome, and ruthless, a soldier-prince who was carving out a state for himself in the region of Romagna, east of Florence. Later writers would remember him as a figure of cold ambition, and the political thinker Niccolò Machiavelli, who knew him, would study him closely. It might seem strange that the gentle student of birds and water would work for such a man. But Leonardo needed employment, and Borgia needed an engineer. In his documents of that year, the duke named him as "architect and general engineer."

For the better part of a year, Leonardo traveled through the towns of Romagna and the Marche on Borgia's behalf, inspecting castles and fortresses, measuring walls, and planning improvements. He went to Urbino and Pesaro and Rimini, to Cesena and to Cesenatico on the coast, where he drew a design for a harbor canal. His notebooks from this period are full of tiny sketches of walls and ditches, and notes about distances, written as he walked, with the small wheel he used to measure the length of a road.

One result of this journey was something unusual and rather wonderful. He made a plan of the town of Imola, which Borgia had captured. At that time, towns were usually drawn in a bird's-eye view, as if from a hill or a tower, showing the buildings in perspective. Leonardo instead drew the town as if seen from straight above, with the streets and walls and houses laid out in a precise circle, measured by compass and distance. It looks very much like a modern map. He colored it with soft washes of green and brown and the pale blue of the river. It was a map for a general, but it is also beautiful, like a lace pattern seen from the sky.

Leonardo did not stay long in the service of Cesare Borgia. By early fifteen hundred and three, the duke's fortunes were failing, and Leonardo had gone home to Florence. He did not look back. In later years, Borgia would be brought down by the death of his father, and would die in battle in Spain, but Leonardo's brief service in his army left no mark on his reputation except as a curious episode.

So in these years the artist became a kind of wanderer. He had the freedom of a man with no master, and the unease of a man with no home. Yet this was also a time of discovery, because every town he visited brought something new to look at, a different kind of stone, a different sort of river, a church with a strange arch, a bird that he had never seen before. He wrote down what he saw. And all the while the notebooks grew.

The road is dusty, and evening is near. A traveler walks beside his mule, with a wheel for measuring distances tied to his pack. Ahead lies a town of red brick walls, with a tower catching the last of the sun. The swallows are wheeling above it. Somewhere a shepherd is calling in the fields, and the day cools into blue.

In the next chapter, we will follow him back to Florence, where he will meet a rival, and begin the portrait of a woman who would become the most famous face in the world.
