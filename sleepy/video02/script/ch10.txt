## Chapter 10: The Anatomist

Of all the subjects which Leonardo studied, none drew him more deeply than the human body. He had begun it as a painter, as a young man in Verrocchio's workshop, when he learned that to paint a figure well, you must know the bones and muscles under the skin. But over time, it became more than a craft. It became a question. How does a person work? What is inside us? What makes the heart beat, and the hand close, and the eye see?

In his time, the study of anatomy was done in a few universities, usually by a professor who read aloud from an ancient book while an assistant cut the body. The old books, written by Greek and Roman physicians, were trusted a great deal. Leonardo did not think that was enough. He wanted to see for himself.

He began in Milan, around fourteen hundred and eighty-nine, with a study of the human skull, drawn in beautiful pen-and-ink pictures that show the bone from the front, from the side, and cut open in section. He used the same method he used for everything: he looked patiently, he measured, and he drew. Later, in Florence, he gained permission to work in the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, where bodies of patients who had died could be examined. It was a large and busy hospital, one of the oldest in the city, and he was able to spend long hours in its rooms.

He tells us about one of these occasions in his notebook. In the winter, probably of fifteen hundred and eight, he spent time with an old man in the hospital, who said he was a hundred years old. The old man told Leonardo that he had no illness, except weakness. Not long after, he died quietly in his bed, as if falling asleep. Leonardo asked to examine the body, to find out the cause of such a gentle death. He wrote that he found the arteries around the heart and the liver were hard and narrowed, like dried and wrinkled things. He understood that the old man had died of a slow stiffening of the vessels which carry the blood. It was an astonishing observation for the time. His drawings of the veins of the arm, made in connection with this study, can be seen today, together with his notes on the death of the old man.

It was a quiet, careful, humble kind of research. Over the years, he studied perhaps thirty bodies in all. He drew the muscles of the arm and the shoulder, and the way the tendons of the hand pull the fingers. He drew the bones of the foot, the curve of the spine, the lungs and the windpipe. He drew a child in the womb with extraordinary tenderness. He cut his subjects in layers, and drew them from different sides, as if turning them in his hands, and he invented new methods of showing the inside of the body, such as drawing a structure as if it were transparent, or exploding it into separate parts, or using cross-sections like those used today.

Some of his best discoveries concerned the heart. In the winter of fifteen hundred and ten, in the city of Pavia, he worked with a young professor of anatomy named Marcantonio della Torre, who shared his interest. Leonardo studied the heart with great care, and in the years that followed he used a glass model of the aortic valve, filled with water and with grass seeds, to see how the water swirled in little eddies behind the valve and helped it to close. He understood that the heart is a muscle, a kind of pump, and that the valves only let the blood go one way. He came very close to understanding that the blood circulates around the body, an idea which was not proved until more than a century later.

He also tried a clever experiment on the brain. Around fifteen hundred and nine, he injected hot wax into the cavities in the head of an ox, and when it cooled, he cut away the surrounding tissue, leaving a cast of the inner spaces. Through this, he saw that the cavities had a very different shape from the one which the old books described. It was an early example of the method of making a cast to study a hollow structure, a method that is still used.

Why, then, is Leonardo not remembered as one of the great founders of modern anatomy? The sad answer is that his work remained hidden. He planned a great book on the human body, with hundreds of drawings and full explanations. He never finished it. His notes stayed in his private papers. After his death, they passed to his friend Francesco Melzi, and later were scattered. It was only in the nineteenth century, when his notebooks were published, that people saw what he had done. If they had been printed in his lifetime, the history of medicine might have taken a faster road.

And there is a tenderness in these pages which is not only scientific. In his notes, he wrote that he was a little afraid of spending the night in the company of the dead, and that the work was disagreeable. He wrote that one who studies the body must have a strong stomach, and a love of the work. Yet he went back to it, night after night, because of a conviction that to know the body was to understand something of life.

In between these studies, he moved again. In fifteen hundred and six, the French governor of Milan, Charles d'Amboise, asked the Florentines to lend him their famous painter, and, after some negotiation, Leonardo traveled north for a first visit. Two years later, in fifteen hundred and eight, he moved back to Milan to stay. He entered the service of the French, and designed a villa for Charles d'Amboise, and made plans for water and gardens. Another young man joined him in his household, a nobleman's son from a village near Milan, named Francesco Melzi, who was still in his teens. He became Leonardo's pupil, companion and in time his closest friend and heir. He remained with him for the rest of his life.

In those Milan years, Leonardo continued his studies of the body and the earth. He wrote about fossils on the mountains. He wrote about rivers and canals. He drew the Alps. He made dozens of small drawings of storms and floods, which seem to anticipate a different kind of anatomy, the anatomy of a world in motion.

The room is very quiet, and the winter night is deep. A single lamp stands on a table, and its light falls on a sheet of paper covered in tiny, careful drawings of an arm, a hand, a heart. The ink is almost dry. The man at the table puts down his pen and rubs his eyes. Outside, the snow is falling softly on the roofs of Milan, and in the room, there is only the sound of breathing.
