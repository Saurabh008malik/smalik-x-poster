## Chapter 9: Florence Again

Florence in fifteen hundred and three was a republic once more. The Medici had been driven out some years before, and the city was governed by elected councils. A new chief magistrate, Piero Soderini, held office for life, and he wanted the city to look as grand as its history. A large hall had been built in the Palazzo della Signoria, the town hall, for the meetings of the Great Council, and its walls needed to be decorated with scenes of Florentine glory.

In October of fifteen hundred and three, Leonardo was given the commission for one of the walls. The subject was the Battle of Anghiari, a victory of the Florentines over the army of Milan in the year fourteen hundred and forty. He made many drawings and studies. He studied horses again, and men in fury, with open mouths and twisted faces, and swords and banners and dust. He wanted to show not the glory of war but its violence, and a group of horsemen struggling over a standard, a flag. It was meant to be extraordinary.

Soon after, a second great artist was given the wall opposite. He was a young sculptor, not yet thirty, whose giant marble statue of David, then being finished, was already the talk of the city. His name was Michelangelo Buonarroti.

The two men were quite different. Leonardo was past fifty, courteous, charming, elegantly dressed, a man of the court and of the studio. Michelangelo was much younger, intense and solitary, with a fierce temper and a gift for stone. There are stories of friction between them, including one in which Leonardo was passing a group of men who were discussing a passage from Dante, and asked Michelangelo to explain it, and Michelangelo answered with a sharp remark about Leonardo's unfinished horse. We cannot be sure that this really happened. But it is plain that Florence enjoyed watching two giants in the same city, and that the rivalry made both of them work harder. In fact, when the Florentines gathered a committee of artists in the winter of fifteen hundred and four to decide where to place Michelangelo's David, Leonardo was among the members who gave an opinion. They moved the statue to a spot near the entrance of the town hall.

Meanwhile, Leonardo's own wall was beginning to rise. He was working on a large cartoon, and then began to paint on the wall itself. Once again, he wished to try a new method. According to the accounts that came later, he used a technique in which oil paint was used on the plaster, with fires in braziers set below it to help the paint dry. It did not work as he had hoped. The colors ran and sagged in the lower part of the wall. He abandoned the work.

The painting was never finished, and, in time, it was lost. Some decades later, the hall was remodeled, and the wall was covered over. What remains are Leonardo's drawings, a few copies made by other artists, and a famous drawing made a century later by the Flemish painter Peter Paul Rubens, who copied the central group of horsemen from a copy he saw. The furious knot of men and animals in that drawing still gives a hint of what the painting must have been.

In these same years, there was also a shadow over Leonardo's private life. In July of fifteen hundred and four, his father, Ser Piero, died in Florence, at the age of seventy-seven. Leonardo wrote about it in his notebook with a calm, terse note, giving the date and the hour. His father had been married four times, and by then had a large family of legitimate children, who inherited his property. Leonardo, as an illegitimate son, was not given a share. Not long after, his uncle Francesco, the same patient countryman who had been so kind to him in childhood, also died, and named Leonardo as his heir, which led to a long quarrel with his half-brothers over the estate. It is a sad little chapter in a great life.

And it is in this time that he began to paint a portrait.

We think he began it around fifteen hundred and three. The woman in it is traditionally identified as Lisa Gherardini, who was about twenty-four years old. She was the wife of a Florentine silk and cloth merchant named Francesco del Giocondo. This is why the picture has been called, in Italian, La Gioconda, and in French, La Joconde. The English name, Mona Lisa, comes from "Monna," a short form of "madonna," meaning "my lady." The Louvre Museum, which owns the painting, identifies the sitter in this way.

It is a small picture, painted on a panel of poplar wood, only about seventy-nine by fifty-three centimeters. A woman sits in a chair with her hands folded, looking out at us. Behind her, a long balcony rail and a vast, strange landscape of winding roads, rivers and rocky peaks, dissolving into mist. Her dress is simple. She wears no jewelry. And on her face is the smile which has become the most discussed expression in the history of art.

Why does it hold us? Part of the answer is the way it was made. Leonardo built up the face with many fine layers of transparent paint, so thin and soft that you cannot see the brushstrokes. The shadows at the corners of the mouth and the eyes are so gentle and blended that the expression seems to change as you look. The smile is not fixed. It appears and fades. It seems to be thinking.

He worked on it for years, on and off. He did not hand it over. Perhaps he was dissatisfied, as usual, or perhaps he had grown attached to it. He kept it with him for the rest of his life, and he carried it with him to Milan, to Rome, and finally to France. Whatever the reason, the portrait was never delivered to the merchant who had paid for it.

Evening comes to the city of the lilies. The river Arno slides under the bridges, gold and quiet. In a workshop near the cathedral, a woman has gone home, and a painter sits alone in the lamplight, with a small panel before him. He lifts a brush, so fine it holds almost nothing, and touches the corner of a mouth, and pauses, and looks for a long time.

In the next chapter, we will follow Leonardo's eye inward, to the mysteries of the human body.
