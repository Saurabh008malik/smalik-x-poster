## Chapter 4: A Young Master in Florence

By the late fourteen seventies, Leonardo was a painter in his own right, and the city of Florence was full of opportunities for him. It was the age of the Medici, the banking family who ruled the city without ever quite being its kings. Lorenzo de' Medici, whom people called the Magnificent, loved poetry, philosophy, music and art. He kept a circle of scholars and artists around him, and Florence was alive with talk about ancient Greece and Rome, and with new ideas about what a human being could do.

In the middle of this lively city, Leonardo set up as a master, and began to take on work. In the winter of fourteen hundred and seventy-eight, he received a commission to paint an altarpiece for a chapel in the old city hall, the Palazzo della Signoria. It was a mark of trust, his first public commission. But he did not finish it. Years later, the work was given to another painter.

This was to become a pattern, and it is only fair to say so plainly. Leonardo was a perfectionist. He had so many ideas, and so much curiosity, that the work in front of him often seemed to him not good enough, or not yet understood well enough, and he would stop. He would turn to a new problem, or a new experiment, and the painting would be left, still waiting. Patrons were sometimes patient, and sometimes they were not.

Of the pictures he did finish in these years, one of the most lovely is a small painting of the Virgin Mary with the Christ child, who reaches for a carnation, which is a flower that was often a symbol of motherhood. Mary sits by a window with a view of far-off mountains, and her face is soft and thoughtful. The painting is called the Madonna of the Carnation. It shows that Leonardo was still learning the possibilities of oil paint, and some parts are a little less secure than others. But the mood is warm and quiet, and one can see how he lingers over the glass of water on the table, and the curls of the hair, and the light on the child's skin.

Then, in the year fourteen hundred and eighty-one, came a large and important commission. The monks of a monastery called San Donato a Scopeto, just outside the walls of Florence, asked him to paint an altarpiece for their church. The subject was the Adoration of the Magi, the moment in the story when the three wise men from the East come to kneel before the newborn child.

Leonardo began with great energy. He made many drawings. He planned a crowded, moving scene, with the Virgin and child at the center, and all around them a crowd of figures, people pressing forward, gesturing, wondering, with horses and ruined buildings in the background and a great staircase. It was unlike any Adoration that had been painted before. Instead of a calm, tidy procession, it was full of surprise and feeling, as if the crowd had just been struck by something wonderful.

He did a great deal of preparation, and he laid in the painting in brown tones, which is the first stage of a picture, a kind of underpainting that shows its shape and its shadows. And then he stopped. The Adoration of the Magi was never finished. It still exists, in the brown stage, and it can be seen today in a museum in Florence. The figures seem to emerge out of a warm mist, half formed, and there is something very moving in that. It is rather like looking at a thought at the moment before it is put into words.

Why did he leave it? We cannot know for sure. There may have been money disputes with the monks. Leonardo himself may have been restless. And, around this time, a new opportunity came his way. At about the same time he worked on another unfinished painting, a Saint Jerome, which shows an old, thin hermit kneeling in a wilderness, beating his chest, with a lion beside him. It too stayed in brown, as if the painter's mind had moved on.

What had moved his mind was a plan. Florence was a wonderful place, but it was crowded with talented artists, and its great patron, Lorenzo, did not seem to be offering Leonardo what he wanted. Other painters, such as Botticelli, Ghirlandaio and Perugino, were sent to Rome to decorate a new chapel for the Pope. Leonardo was not among them. It may have hurt him a little, although he never said so.

So he began to look elsewhere. He looked north, to the rich and powerful city of Milan.

Milan was ruled by the Sforza family, who were dukes. They were a military dynasty, and the real power in the city was in the hands of a clever and ambitious man called Ludovico Sforza, the uncle of the young duke. Ludovico was called "il Moro," which means "the Moor," most likely because of his dark complexion, or perhaps a play on the Latin word for the mulberry tree. He had risen to power in a quiet but ruthless way. He wanted to make Milan the greatest court in Italy, and for that he needed engineers, soldiers, musicians and artists.

It was a different world from Florence. Where Florence was a city of merchants and republican ideals, Milan was a city of armies and princes. The land around it was wide and flat, with canals and rice fields, and the great cathedral was still under construction. If you wanted to be an engineer, to design fortresses and weapons and waterworks, this was the place to go.

Leonardo, now about thirty years old, decided to go. Some accounts say he traveled as part of a diplomatic gift, carrying a silver lyre shaped like a horse's head, which Lorenzo de' Medici sent to Ludovico. It is a pleasing detail, but it comes from later sources, and we should hold it lightly. What we do know is that around the year fourteen hundred and eighty-two, he packed his drawings and his few belongings, and left Florence for Milan.

He was leaving the city where he had learned everything he knew. He would not return to live there for almost twenty years. He took with him a head full of ideas, a talent that few people had yet recognized, and a letter, which he had written to the duke, offering his services.

It is a famous letter, and we will hear about it in the next chapter.

For now, the road north is long. The hills of Tuscany roll away behind him, blue and gentle, and the poplars along the river bend in the wind. The evening bells of a village are ringing. He walks on, or rides, with a bundle on his back and a lyre at his side. And ahead, beyond the mountains, the great plain of Lombardy is opening, wide and green, under a pale sky.
