## Chapter 5: The Court of Milan

Milan, in the year fourteen hundred and eighty-two, was a city of towers, canals and marketplaces, where the sound of church bells mixed with the clatter of armorers at work. It stood in the middle of the great plain of Lombardy, and on a clear day, if you climbed high enough, you could see the white line of the Alps far away to the north. The streets were busy with merchants and mule trains. In the center rose the unfinished cathedral, its marble pale against the sky, and beside it the heavy brick castle of the Sforza, with its tall towers and wide courtyards.

Into this city came a Florentine painter of thirty, who was, as yet, hardly known outside his home town. He had to make an impression on the ruler, Ludovico Sforza. And so he did something unusual. He wrote a letter.

The letter has survived, in a large collection of Leonardo's papers called the Codex Atlanticus, kept in the Ambrosian Library in Milan. What survives is a copy of the text, and it may not be exactly the letter that was sent. It is one of the most famous job applications in history.

It is a long list of what he could do. And the surprising thing about it, to a modern reader, is that almost all of it is about war. Of ten numbered paragraphs, nine offer the services of a military engineer. He wrote that he could build light and strong bridges which could be carried easily and set up quickly. He wrote that he knew how to drain the water from a moat when a fortress was besieged, and how to make ladders and siege machines. He described a kind of covered armored carriage, which could carry guns into the enemy's ranks. He promised cannon, mortars and other engines of beautiful and useful shape, and ways to dig tunnels and secret passages. And at sea, he said, he had designs for ships that could stand up to heavy fire.

Only at the end, in the tenth paragraph, did he mention what he really was. In peacetime, he wrote, he believed he could give satisfaction equal to any other, in architecture, in the leading of water from one place to another, and in sculpture of marble, bronze and clay. And in painting, he added, he could do whatever could be done, as well as any man, whoever he might be. He also said that he would be happy to make a bronze horse, to honor the memory of the duke's father and the glorious house of Sforza.

We should keep two thoughts in mind about this letter. The first is that Leonardo was a practical man, who knew what a ruler like Ludovico wanted. A duke in those days, in an age of constant small wars, was far more interested in a bridge or a cannon than in a pretty picture. The second is that we do not know how many of those engines he had actually built, and he may have been promising more than he had done. Some of his designs were real ideas. Some were more like hopeful sketches. But the letter shows us how he saw himself. He did not see himself as only a painter. He saw himself as a person who could solve problems of any kind.

It worked. In the years that followed, he became a part of Ludovico's household, and he stayed in Milan, with some interruptions, for about seventeen years.

His duties were many and varied. He was an engineer, as he had promised, and he gave advice on fortifications and on waterways. Milan lay in a land of canals, and the lord of the city needed people to think about locks, channels and irrigation. Leonardo studied them. He was also an architect, and entered a wooden model in the competition to design the great lantern above the cathedral crossing. Above all, he was a master of ceremonies. Ludovico loved festivals, and the court needed people who could design costumes, build stage machinery, and create spectacles for weddings and visiting princes. Leonardo planned pageants with moving scenery, and a mechanical sky of stars and planets, and masques and riddles and tableaux. He loved this work, and he was good at it.

But he was also a painter. In the spring of fourteen hundred and eighty-three, Leonardo and two brothers, who were local painters, the de Predis brothers, signed a contract to produce an altarpiece for a chapel in the church of San Francesco Grande. It was to show the Virgin Mary with the infant Christ, the little Saint John the Baptist and an angel, in a rocky grotto. The painting is now known as the Virgin of the Rocks.

It is a strange, beautiful picture. The figures sit in a dim cave, with spiky stones and ferns and flowers around them, and a view through an opening to distant water and cliffs. The faces are soft and rounded, lit from one side, and shadows melt into one another without any sharp outlines. It was a new way of painting, which he used all his life. The Italians called it sfumato, from the word for smoke, because the edges between light and dark dissolve like a haze.

The contract led to a long dispute about payment, which dragged on for many years, and was not settled until fifteen hundred and eight. There are two versions of the painting. One is in the Louvre, in Paris, and the other is in the National Gallery in London. Experts continue to study which parts were painted by whom, and in what order.

In these same years, Leonardo painted a number of portraits for the court. One of the best known is the portrait of a young woman holding a stoat, the small white animal which, in winter, is called an ermine. It is thought to be Cecilia Gallerani, a young woman of the court who was close to the duke. She turns her head as if she has heard a voice behind her, and the animal in her arms is sleek and alert. The hand that holds it is long and delicate. There is a sense of life in the picture, as though it had paused between two movements.

Court life had its pleasures. There were banquets, and music, and poets, and a library to visit. There was also a lot of waiting for payment. Leonardo's relations with Ludovico were not always easy, and in the middle of this period he wrote a short note, reminding the duke of money which was owed to him.

Still, he had found a home. He had a workshop, and apprentices, and a circle of friends. He was free to think. And in this great plain, with its canals and mills and wide skies, his curiosity found new worlds to explore.

As the evening falls over Milan, lamps are lit along the canal. A barge drifts under a bridge, and the water carries the golden reflections of the windows. In a quiet room in the castle, a candle burns beside a table covered with papers, and someone is writing, in a small, careful hand, from right to left.

In the next chapter, we will open those papers, and see what was in them.
