## Chapter 7: The Horse and the Last Supper

In the years after he arrived in Milan, Leonardo took on two projects which were each, in their way, among the boldest in the whole Renaissance. One of them was a statue. The other was a painting. Both were meant to astonish, and the two of them had very different fates.

We will begin with the statue. In his letter to the duke, Leonardo had promised a bronze horse in honor of Ludovico's father, Francesco Sforza, the soldier who had made the family rulers of Milan. Ludovico took him at his word. The monument was to be a huge equestrian statue, a rider on a rearing or striding horse, several times larger than life, and it was to be made of bronze. Nothing of such a size had been cast in Italy before.

Leonardo threw himself into the task. He studied horses with an eye that missed nothing. He went to the duke's stables and drew the animals from every side, in every movement. He measured them. He wrote about their proportions, the angle of the neck, the swell of the muscles in the shoulders, and the way the legs fold at a trot. He made many sketches of the horse in different poses, some rearing up over a fallen enemy, and then some in a calmer stride, which would be easier to cast. He had to think, too, about the practical problem of how to cast such a vast piece of metal in a single pour, and he drew diagrams of furnaces, molds and pits. It was engineering and art in one.

By the autumn of fourteen hundred and ninety-three, he had built a full-size model of the horse in clay. It was more than seven meters tall, taller than a house of two floors. The model was displayed in public in Milan, at the time of the wedding of Ludovico's niece to Maximilian of Habsburg, the new Holy Roman Emperor, and it was greatly admired. People came from all over to marvel at it. The poets wrote verses. The duke was pleased.

But the bronze was never cast. Only a year later, in fourteen hundred and ninety-four, the king of France, Charles the Eighth, marched an army into Italy, and the dukes and princes of the peninsula became alarmed. Ludovico needed cannon, and he needed them quickly. The great stock of bronze which had been collected for the horse was sent away to be made into guns. The statue was put aside.

Years later, in fourteen hundred and ninety-nine, French soldiers entered Milan, and the clay model, which had stood outside for so many years, was damaged. There is a tradition that the crossbowmen used it for target practice. It did not survive. Leonardo's horse is one of the great lost works of art. It exists now only in drawings, in a few notes, and in the imagination.

Now let us turn to the painting.

On the northern edge of the city stood a church and a Dominican convent called Santa Maria delle Grazie, which means Saint Mary of the Graces. Ludovico was particularly fond of it, because he planned to be buried there. The friars had a long, high room where they ate their meals in silence, called the refectory, and the duke asked Leonardo to decorate the wall at one end.

The subject was the Last Supper. It was a very familiar subject in painting, and it was usually shown in a stiff, solemn way. Leonardo chose a different moment. He did not show the quiet meal itself, but the instant just after Jesus has said to his twelve disciples that one of them will betray him. It is the moment of shock. In an instant, the whole table is moved.

He arranged the thirteen figures in groups of three along a single long table, with Jesus at the center, calm and still, a pool of quiet in the middle of a storm. On each side, the disciples react. One leans forward in alarm. Another throws up his hands. One draws back. Another points, and another whispers to his neighbor, and one, with his hand on his chest, protests his innocence. Every face and every gesture has a different feeling in it. Leonardo wrote that a painter should paint not only the body but the movements of the mind, and here, perhaps more than anywhere, he did it.

The lines of the room, the beams of the ceiling, the tapestries on the walls, and the edges of the table all lead the eye toward the head of Christ, at the very center, with the light of a window behind him. It is perspective used to focus attention, and it makes the whole painting feel like an extension of the room in which it was painted, as if the monks eating at their long tables could look up and see another table, in another room, continuing the space.

He worked on it from about fourteen hundred and ninety-five. Vasari, and other writers, left accounts that may have been exaggerated, of how Leonardo would sometimes work all day without stopping, and sometimes would stand in front of the wall for hours, doing nothing but look, and then make a single brushstroke and leave. The prior of the convent is said to have complained to the duke, who asked Leonardo about it, and Leonardo replied, according to the story, that men of genius sometimes do the most when they seem to do the least, because they are forming ideas in their minds. Whether or not he said it, it sounds like him.

He finished the painting in about fourteen hundred and ninety-eight. And here, we must tell the sad part of the story, because it is also part of the story of Leonardo. Fresco, the usual method for painting on walls, is done quickly on wet plaster, which dries and fuses with the paint. This did not suit him. He wanted time, and he wanted the glow and the fine detail of oil paint. So he experimented, painting on a dry wall with a mix of materials, which allowed him to work slowly. It was a daring idea. It was also a mistake. Because the paint did not bond properly with the wall, it began to flake and fade within a few decades.

Over the centuries, the Last Supper suffered many misfortunes. Damp ran down the wall. Restorers painted over it again and again. In sixteen hundred and fifty-two, the monks cut a doorway through the bottom of the painting, removing Christ's feet. Soldiers used the room as a stable. And in August of nineteen hundred and forty-three, during the Second World War, bombs fell on Milan and destroyed much of the refectory. The wall bearing the painting was saved by sandbags and supports which had been put up to protect it, and it stood in the open air amid the rubble.

After the war, the painting was patiently treated. From nineteen hundred and seventy-eight to nineteen hundred and ninety-nine, a restorer named Pinin Brambilla Barcilon, together with a team, spent twenty-one years cleaning and stabilizing it, removing the later repaintings, and revealing as much of Leonardo's original work as remained. Today it is a fragile, ghostly thing. A good deal of what we see is not his hand. But it is still there, and it still moves everyone who stands before it, in small groups, in the cool air of the room.

There is something fitting about that. The painting reminds us that great things can be lost, and that some of the most valuable things are also the most delicate. And yet, it is still here.

The evening is quiet in the convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie. The friars have finished their meal, and the long room is empty. The last light from the high window falls across the wall, and on it, faintly, thirteen figures lean toward one another, caught in a single moment. The candles are put out, one by one. And for a while, in the dark, the room is silent.

In the next chapter, we will follow Leonardo as the world around him changes, and he becomes a traveler.
