## Chapter 11: Rome, and the Road to France

By the year fifteen hundred and thirteen, the political winds in northern Italy had turned again. The French, who had governed Milan, were driven out for a time by the armies of the Pope and his allies, and the world that had sheltered Leonardo for several years was suddenly unsafe. He was sixty-one years old. In the autumn, he left Milan, once more, with his notebooks, his instruments, and a household of pupils: Francesco Melzi, who was now a grown young man and his loyal companion, and Salaì, and two assistants. They went south toward Rome.

Rome was a very different city from Milan. It was still smaller than Florence in some ways, but it was full of building, of rubble and cranes and scaffolding, as the popes rebuilt the city. At the church of Saint Peter, the great architect Bramante was still at work on the plans for a new and vast basilica. On the walls of the papal apartments, the young painter Raphael was creating his frescoes. Michelangelo had already painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, and was elsewhere at work. The new Pope, Leo the Tenth, was a Medici, a son of Lorenzo the Magnificent, who loved music, festivals and learned company.

Leonardo came as the guest of the pope's brother, Giuliano de' Medici, who gave him rooms in the Belvedere, a pleasant palace on a hill above the Vatican gardens, and a monthly allowance. It was a gracious welcome. But the commissions he hoped for did not come. The pope's attention was turned to younger artists. There is a story that, when the pope asked Leonardo for a painting, Leonardo started to distill oils and herbs for a special varnish, and the pope, who heard of it, remarked with a sigh that this man would never accomplish anything, as he was thinking about the end before he had begun. It is another anecdote from a later writer, and we can't be sure that it happened. But it reflects how some people felt.

There were, too, difficulties with the German workmen who were assigned to him, and there were jealousies. Leonardo's notes from these years are fewer. He studied the geometry of curves, and the movement of water. He drew a wonderful series of studies of the swirling of floods. He made experiments with mirrors. He was almost certainly consulted about the draining of the Pontine Marshes, south of Rome, though his exact role isn't clear. He spent about three years in Rome, and then the situation changed again. Giuliano de' Medici died in the spring of fifteen hundred and sixteen, and Leonardo lost his protector.

At this moment, an invitation arrived from France.

The king of France was now a young man named Francis the First, only twenty years old when he came to the throne. He was tall, energetic, and adventurous, and he loved the arts of Italy. After a great victory at the battle of Marignano, in September of fifteen hundred and fifteen, he met the pope at Bologna, and may have seen Leonardo's work, or heard much about him. He had heard of the painter who made things that seemed alive. He sent an invitation.

Leonardo accepted. He was by now in his middle sixties, and he may have felt that Italy had little more to offer him. At the end of fifteen hundred and sixteen, he crossed the Alps for the last time, riding a mule, perhaps, along the old mountain roads, with Melzi beside him and baggage carrying his papers. With him went three paintings, which he had never given away: the portrait of the Florentine merchant's wife, the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne, and Saint John the Baptist.

The king gave him a house. It was a small, handsome manor of rose-colored brick, called Cloux, which is now known as Clos Lucé, in the town of Amboise. It stood in a garden, a short walk from the king's own castle, above the river Loire. A secret passage between the two buildings is part of local tradition, though that is only a story. The king gave him a generous pension, and the title of First Painter, Architect and Engineer to the King. He expected no work in exchange, except conversation and counsel.

Imagine this house in the autumn. The Loire flows broad and slow beneath the town, with its sandbanks and its willows. The woods are turning gold. The royal castle on the hill rises above the river, with its towers and chapels and its terraced gardens. And in the rose-brick manor, an old man sits by a window, with a book on his knee.

Francis loved to visit him. The king would come, often, to talk about art and nature and philosophy. Later the sculptor Benvenuto Cellini would record the king as having said that he did not believe any other man had been born who knew as much as Leonardo, in sculpture, painting and architecture, and that he was a very great philosopher. The two of them, the energetic young king and the gentle old master, seem to have become real friends.

Leonardo's body was beginning to fail. A visitor to Cloux in the autumn of fifteen hundred and seventeen, a cardinal's secretary named Antonio de Beatis, left a record of his visit. He reported that Leonardo's right hand was partly paralyzed, and that he could no longer paint with the skill of before. But his mind was clear. The master showed his visitors three pictures, including the portrait of a Florentine lady, and spoke of his studies of anatomy, which he said he had made on more than thirty bodies.

So the work changed. In France, Leonardo seems to have painted little. Instead, he organized his notebooks. He gave advice about architecture and a project for a royal palace at Romorantin, with canals and gardens, though it was never built. He designed machines and decorations for court festivals, and he is said to have built a mechanical lion for the king, which walked a few steps and opened its chest to reveal a bunch of lilies, the flowers of France. He drew storms and deluges, in a series of strange, swirling pages in which the world seems to be ending in water and wind. And he wrote, and wrote, and wrote.

He had the company of the people he loved. Melzi was with him, tending to his needs and writing his letters. And Salaì stayed for a time. In the evenings, the household gathered in the rooms of the manor, where there was the smell of woodsmoke, and the voices of friends.

The evening is soft on the Loire. A mist rises from the river, and a heron stands in the shallows. Up on the hill, the lights of the castle are being lit one by one. In the house of Cloux, a fire is glowing, and an old man in a warm robe sits listening to the sound of the river flowing, as it has flowed for a thousand years.

In the last chapter, we will say goodnight to Leonardo, and to the story.
